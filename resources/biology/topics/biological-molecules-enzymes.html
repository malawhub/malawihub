🧪 Biological Molecules & Enzymes

Carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, water, food tests and enzyme action.

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the major biological molecules in living organisms.
  • Describe the functions of carbohydrates, proteins and lipids.
  • Explain the importance of water in living organisms.
  • Describe food tests for major nutrients.
  • Explain enzyme action and factors affecting enzyme activity.

1. Biological Molecules

Living organisms contain several important chemical substances. The major biological molecules include carbohydrates, proteins and lipids.

2. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They include sugars and starch.

Functions

  • Provide energy for respiration.
  • Store energy, for example as starch in plants and glycogen in animals.
  • Provide structural material in some organisms.

Examples

  • Glucose
  • Sucrose
  • Starch
  • Cellulose

3. Proteins

Proteins are large molecules made from amino acids.

  • Growth and repair of tissues.
  • Formation of enzymes.
  • Formation of some hormones and antibodies.
  • Structural functions in cells and tissues.

4. Lipids

Lipids include fats and oils. They are important energy-storage molecules.

  • Long-term energy storage.
  • Thermal insulation.
  • Protection of organs.
  • Formation of cell membranes.

5. Water

Water is essential for life because many biological reactions occur in aqueous conditions.

  • Acts as a solvent.
  • Provides a medium for chemical reactions.
  • Transports dissolved substances.
  • Helps regulate body temperature.
  • Provides water for photosynthesis in plants.

6. Food Tests

Test for Starch

Add iodine solution to the food sample. A blue-black colour indicates starch.

Test for Reducing Sugar

Add Benedict's solution and heat the mixture in a water bath. A change from blue towards green, yellow, orange or brick-red indicates reducing sugar, depending on concentration.

Test for Protein

Add Biuret reagent. A lilac or purple colour indicates protein.

Test for Lipid

Shake the food sample with ethanol and then add water. A cloudy white emulsion indicates lipid.

7. Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up chemical reactions in living organisms without being used up permanently in the reaction.

8. Lock-and-Key Concept

An enzyme has an active site with a particular shape. A suitable substrate fits the active site and forms an enzyme-substrate complex.

Enzyme + substrate → enzyme-substrate complex → products + enzyme

9. Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

Temperature

Increasing temperature usually increases enzyme activity up to an optimum temperature. Above the optimum, bonds maintaining the enzyme's structure can be disrupted and the active site changes shape. The enzyme is then said to be denatured.

pH

Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Very acidic or alkaline conditions can change the shape of the active site and reduce activity.

Substrate Concentration

Increasing substrate concentration increases reaction rate until most available active sites are occupied. After this point, increasing substrate concentration has little further effect.

Enzyme Concentration

Increasing enzyme concentration can increase reaction rate when sufficient substrate is available.

10. Practical Investigation

A common investigation measures the effect of temperature or pH on enzyme activity.

  • Identify the independent variable.
  • Measure the dependent variable.
  • Keep other important variables constant.
  • Repeat measurements to improve reliability.
  • Calculate averages where appropriate.
  • Present results in a suitable table or graph.

MANEB-Style Practice

  1. State two functions of carbohydrates.
  2. Name the building blocks of proteins.
  3. Describe the test for starch.
  4. What is an enzyme?
  5. Explain why enzyme activity decreases sharply above the optimum temperature.
  6. Explain the effect of increasing substrate concentration on enzyme activity.
  7. State two variables that should be controlled when investigating enzyme activity.

Answers

  1. Energy supply and energy storage.
  2. Amino acids.
  3. Add iodine solution. A blue-black colour indicates starch.
  4. An enzyme is a biological catalyst that speeds up a reaction without being permanently consumed.
  5. High temperature can disrupt bonds maintaining the enzyme's three-dimensional structure, changing the active site and preventing the substrate from fitting properly.
  6. Rate initially increases because more substrate molecules are available to bind with enzymes. Eventually most active sites become occupied and the rate levels off.
  7. Examples include pH, enzyme concentration, substrate concentration and reaction volume, depending on the investigation.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not describe enzymes as being used up during a reaction.
  • Do not confuse denaturation with simply slowing down.
  • Remember that iodine tests for starch, not all carbohydrates.
  • In practical questions, distinguish independent, dependent and controlled variables.

Examination Tips

  • Memorise the colour changes for food tests.
  • When explaining enzyme graphs, mention the optimum condition.
  • Always connect enzyme structure to enzyme function.
  • For practical questions, state how measurements are taken and how reliability is improved.
