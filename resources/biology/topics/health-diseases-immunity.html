Health, Diseases & Immunity

Pathogens, disease transmission, body defences, immunity, vaccines and prevention.

Learning Objectives

  • Define health and disease.
  • Distinguish between communicable and non-communicable diseases.
  • Identify major groups of pathogens.
  • Explain how infectious diseases are transmitted.
  • Describe the body's defence mechanisms.
  • Explain antibodies, antitoxins and immune responses.
  • Explain vaccination and herd immunity.
  • Describe methods of disease prevention and control.

1. Health and Disease

Health is a state of physical, mental and social well-being.

A disease is a condition that interferes with the normal functioning of the body or causes abnormal changes in tissues or organs.

2. Communicable Diseases

Communicable diseases are diseases caused by pathogens that can be transmitted from one person or organism to another.

Examples include malaria, tuberculosis, cholera, HIV infection and influenza.

3. Non-Communicable Diseases

Non-communicable diseases are not normally transmitted from one person to another.

Examples include diabetes, coronary heart disease, many cancers and some inherited disorders.

4. Pathogens

A pathogen is a microorganism or infectious agent that causes disease.

Pathogen Group Example Example Disease
Bacteria Mycobacterium tuberculosis Tuberculosis
Viruses HIV HIV infection
Protozoa Plasmodium Malaria
Fungi Some pathogenic fungi Ringworm

5. Transmission of Disease

Pathogens can be transmitted through different routes.

  • Airborne droplets from coughing or sneezing.
  • Contaminated food and water.
  • Direct physical contact.
  • Sexual contact.
  • Blood and contaminated needles.
  • Vectors such as mosquitoes.

6. Malaria

Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites and is transmitted mainly by the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes.

Prevention can include mosquito nets, reducing mosquito breeding sites, insecticide-based control measures and appropriate medical treatment.

7. HIV and AIDS

HIV is a virus that attacks cells of the immune system. Without effective treatment, HIV infection can progress to AIDS, a condition in which immune function is severely weakened.

HIV can be transmitted through infected blood, sexual transmission and from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding.

Prevention includes safer sexual practices, appropriate testing, screened blood supplies and prevention of exposure to contaminated needles.

8. Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis is a bacterial disease that commonly affects the lungs. It can spread through airborne droplets from an infected person.

Symptoms may include persistent cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss and tiredness.

Diagnosis and treatment are important for protecting both the patient and the wider community.

9. Body Defences

The human body has several defence mechanisms that prevent pathogens from entering or spreading.

Physical and Chemical Defences

  • Skin forms a physical barrier.
  • Mucus traps pathogens and particles.
  • Cilia move mucus away from parts of the respiratory tract.
  • Stomach acid can kill many microorganisms.
  • Blood clotting helps seal damaged skin.

10. White Blood Cells

White blood cells are important in immune defence.

Phagocytosis

Some white blood cells engulf and digest pathogens. This process is called phagocytosis.

Antibodies

Lymphocytes can produce antibodies that bind specifically to antigens on pathogens or abnormal cells.

Antitoxins

Some antibodies can neutralise toxins produced by pathogens. These antibodies are called antitoxins.

11. Antigens

An antigen is a molecule or molecular structure recognised by the immune system as foreign or abnormal.

Different pathogens carry different antigens, allowing the immune system to respond specifically to them.

12. Immunity

Immunity is the ability of the body to resist or respond effectively to a particular infectious agent.

Active Immunity

Active immunity develops when the person's own immune system produces a response to an antigen.

It can occur after infection or vaccination.

Passive Immunity

Passive immunity occurs when antibodies are received from another source rather than produced by the person's own immune system.

For example, antibodies can pass from a mother to her baby.

13. Vaccination

Vaccination involves introducing a harmless form or component of a pathogen, or another preparation containing relevant antigens, to stimulate an immune response without causing the disease in the usual way.

The immune system produces antibodies and memory cells. If the same pathogen is encountered later, the immune response can be faster and stronger.

14. Herd Immunity

When a sufficiently large proportion of a population is immune to an infectious disease, transmission can be reduced because there are fewer susceptible people available for the pathogen to infect.

15. Antibiotics

Antibiotics are medicines used to treat certain bacterial infections.

They do not kill viruses and should not be used unnecessarily.

16. Antibiotic Resistance

Some bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics. Resistant bacteria survive treatment and reproduce, making infections more difficult to treat.

Correct use of antibiotics helps reduce the development and spread of antibiotic resistance.

17. Non-Communicable Diseases

Non-communicable diseases can result from combinations of genetic, environmental and lifestyle factors.

Risk Factors

  • Smoking
  • Unhealthy diet
  • Physical inactivity
  • Excess alcohol consumption
  • Obesity
  • Some inherited genetic factors
  • Exposure to harmful environmental substances

18. Cancer

Cancer occurs when abnormal cells divide uncontrollably and may invade surrounding tissues or spread to other parts of the body.

Risk factors vary between cancer types and can include tobacco use, certain infections, radiation exposure and inherited genetic factors.

19. Disease Prevention

  • Good personal hygiene.
  • Safe drinking water.
  • Proper sanitation.
  • Safe food preparation.
  • Vaccination.
  • Vector control.
  • Safe sexual practices.
  • Appropriate diagnosis and treatment.
  • Healthy diet and regular physical activity.

MANEB-Style Practice

  1. Define a pathogen.
  2. Distinguish between communicable and non-communicable diseases.
  3. State four ways pathogens can be transmitted.
  4. Explain how malaria is transmitted.
  5. State three ways HIV can be transmitted.
  6. Explain the role of phagocytes.
  7. What is an antibody?
  8. Explain how vaccination provides protection against disease.
  9. Explain why antibiotics do not treat viral infections.
  10. Explain antibiotic resistance.
  11. State four methods of preventing infectious diseases.

Answers

  1. A pathogen is an infectious agent that causes disease.
  2. Communicable diseases can be transmitted between individuals, while non-communicable diseases are not normally transmitted from person to person.
  3. Airborne droplets, contaminated food or water, direct contact, sexual contact, blood and vectors.
  4. Malaria parasites are transmitted mainly through bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes.
  5. Examples include infected blood, sexual transmission and mother-to-child transmission.
  6. Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens.
  7. An antibody is a specific protein produced by lymphocytes that binds to a particular antigen.
  8. Vaccination stimulates production of antibodies and memory cells, allowing a faster response if the pathogen is encountered later.
  9. Antibiotics target structures or processes found in bacteria and do not act against viruses in the same way.
  10. Antibiotic resistance occurs when resistant bacteria survive treatment and reproduce, making infections harder to treat.
  11. Vaccination, sanitation, safe water, hygiene, vector control, safe food preparation and appropriate treatment are examples.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not say antibiotics cure viral infections.
  • Do not confuse antibodies with antigens.
  • Remember that malaria is caused by a protozoan parasite, not a virus or bacterium.
  • Do not describe vaccination as treatment for an existing infection.

Examination Tips

  • Learn the pathogen, transmission route and prevention method for common diseases.
  • Practise explaining immune responses in logical sequence.
  • Know the differences between active and passive immunity.
  • For disease-prevention questions, link each method to the stage of transmission it interrupts.
