🧠 Nervous & Endocrine Coordination

The nervous system, reflex actions, hormones, sense organs and coordination.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the importance of coordination in humans.
  • Describe the organisation of the nervous system.
  • Describe the structure and function of neurones.
  • Explain reflex actions and reflex arcs.
  • Explain synaptic transmission.
  • Describe the role of major endocrine glands and hormones.
  • Compare nervous and hormonal coordination.

1. Coordination

Coordination allows different parts of the body to work together and respond appropriately to changes in the internal and external environment.

Humans coordinate responses mainly through the nervous system and endocrine system.

2. Nervous System

The nervous system receives information, processes it and coordinates rapid responses.

It consists mainly of:

  • Central nervous system
  • Peripheral nervous system

Central Nervous System

The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord.

Peripheral Nervous System

The peripheral nervous system consists of nerves that connect the central nervous system to receptors, muscles and glands.

3. Neurones

Neurones are specialised cells that transmit electrical impulses.

Types of Neurones

  • Sensory neurone: carries impulses from receptors to the central nervous system.
  • Relay neurone: carries impulses within the central nervous system.
  • Motor neurone: carries impulses from the central nervous system to effectors.

4. Structure of a Neurone

  • Cell body: contains the nucleus and most cell organelles.
  • Dendrites: receive signals from other cells or receptors.
  • Axon: carries impulses away from the cell body.
  • Myelin sheath: insulates the axon and increases the speed of impulse transmission.
  • Axon terminals: form connections with other cells.

5. Receptors and Effectors

A receptor detects a stimulus.

An effector produces a response. Muscles and glands are examples of effectors.

6. Reflex Actions

A reflex action is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus that occurs without conscious decision.

Reflex actions help protect the body from harm.

Reflex Arc

A typical reflex pathway is:

Stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector → response

7. Example of a Withdrawal Reflex

When a person touches a hot object, receptors in the skin detect the stimulus. An impulse travels through a sensory neurone to the spinal cord.

A relay neurone passes the impulse to a motor neurone. The motor neurone carries the impulse to the appropriate muscles, causing the hand to move away from the hot object.

The response occurs rapidly before the person consciously experiences the full sensation of pain.

8. Synapses

A synapse is a junction between two neurones or between a neurone and another cell.

When an electrical impulse reaches the end of a neurone, neurotransmitter chemicals may be released into the synaptic gap.

The neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap and binds to receptors on the next cell, triggering another impulse or response.

9. The Brain

The brain is the main control centre of the nervous system.

Cerebrum

The cerebrum is involved in conscious thought, memory, learning, interpretation of sensory information and voluntary movement.

Cerebellum

The cerebellum coordinates muscular activity, balance and posture.

Medulla

The medulla controls important involuntary activities such as breathing and heart rate.

10. Endocrine System

The endocrine system consists of glands that release hormones into the bloodstream.

Hormones are chemical messengers that travel in the blood to target cells or organs.

11. Important Endocrine Glands and Hormones

Gland Hormone Main Function
Pituitary gland Several hormones Controls growth and influences other endocrine glands.
Thyroid gland Thyroxine Helps regulate metabolic activity.
Pancreas Insulin and glucagon Regulates blood glucose concentration.
Adrenal glands Adrenaline Prepares the body for rapid action.
Testes Testosterone Controls male reproductive development and secondary sexual characteristics.
Ovaries Oestrogen and progesterone Control female reproductive development and reproductive cycles.

12. Adrenaline

Adrenaline is released during situations such as fear, excitement or danger.

It prepares the body for rapid action by increasing heart rate, increasing blood flow to skeletal muscles and increasing the availability of glucose for respiration.

13. Insulin and Blood Glucose

Blood glucose concentration must be kept within a suitable range.

When blood glucose concentration rises, the pancreas releases insulin. Insulin promotes uptake of glucose by cells and conversion of excess glucose into glycogen, especially in the liver and muscles.

When blood glucose concentration falls, glucagon promotes the breakdown of glycogen to glucose, helping restore the blood glucose level.

14. Nervous Versus Hormonal Coordination

Feature Nervous System Endocrine System
Signal Electrical impulses and neurotransmitters Hormones
Transport Neurones Bloodstream
Speed Usually very rapid Usually slower
Duration Usually short-lived Often longer-lasting
Targeting Specific pathways Target cells with appropriate receptors

15. Sense Organs

Sense organs contain receptors that detect specific stimuli.

  • Eye: detects light.
  • Ear: detects sound and contributes to balance.
  • Nose: detects chemicals associated with smell.
  • Tongue: contains receptors for taste.
  • Skin: contains receptors for touch, pressure, temperature and pain.

MANEB-Style Practice

  1. Define coordination.
  2. Name the two main divisions of the nervous system.
  3. State the function of a sensory neurone.
  4. State the function of a motor neurone.
  5. Define a reflex action.
  6. State the pathway of a simple reflex arc.
  7. Explain why reflex actions are important.
  8. What is a synapse?
  9. State two functions of the cerebrum.
  10. State the function of the cerebellum.
  11. Explain the role of insulin in controlling blood glucose.
  12. Give two differences between nervous and hormonal coordination.

Answers

  1. Coordination is the control and integration of activities so that different parts of the body respond appropriately.
  2. Central nervous system and peripheral nervous system.
  3. It carries impulses from receptors to the central nervous system.
  4. It carries impulses from the central nervous system to an effector.
  5. A reflex action is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus.
  6. Receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector.
  7. They provide rapid protective responses to potentially harmful stimuli.
  8. A synapse is a junction between neurones or between a neurone and another cell.
  9. Conscious thought and memory; interpretation of sensory information; voluntary movement.
  10. It coordinates muscular activity, balance and posture.
  11. Insulin lowers blood glucose by promoting glucose uptake and conversion of excess glucose into glycogen.
  12. Nervous coordination is generally faster and uses neurones; hormonal coordination is generally slower and uses hormones carried in the blood.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse sensory and motor neurones.
  • Remember that the brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system.
  • Do not say hormones travel through neurones; they are transported mainly in the blood.
  • Remember that reflex actions are rapid and automatic.

Examination Tips

  • Practise drawing and labelling a reflex arc.
  • Learn the functions of the main brain regions.
  • Memorise important endocrine glands, hormones and their functions.
  • For comparison questions, organise answers in a table.
  • When explaining homeostatic control, describe the change, hormone involved and resulting corrective response.
