Practical Biology

Experimental skills, biological investigations, measurements, observations, data analysis and examination techniques.

Learning Objectives

  • Plan a biological investigation.
  • Identify variables and controls.
  • Make accurate biological measurements.
  • Use appropriate apparatus safely.
  • Record observations and results in tables.
  • Construct and interpret graphs.
  • Calculate biological quantities.
  • Evaluate experimental methods and sources of error.

1. Biological Investigation

A biological investigation is a systematic study designed to answer a scientific question or test a hypothesis.

General Steps

  1. Identify the problem or research question.
  2. State a hypothesis.
  3. Identify variables.
  4. Plan the procedure.
  5. Collect observations and measurements.
  6. Record results accurately.
  7. Analyse the data.
  8. Draw a conclusion.
  9. Evaluate the investigation.

2. Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable statement or prediction about the relationship between variables.

Example: Increasing light intensity will increase the rate of photosynthesis up to a limiting level.

3. Variables

Variable Meaning
Independent variable The factor deliberately changed by the investigator.
Dependent variable The factor measured or observed as a result.
Controlled variable A factor kept constant so that it does not affect the investigation unfairly.

4. Fair Test

A fair test changes only the independent variable while other important variables are controlled.

This makes it easier to determine whether changes in the dependent variable were caused by the independent variable.

5. Replication

Repeating measurements or experiments improves reliability and helps identify unusual results.

Repeated results can be used to calculate a mean when appropriate.

6. Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the true or accepted value.

Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another.

7. Reliability

An investigation is more reliable when repeated measurements give similar results and the procedure is controlled carefully.

8. Validity

Validity refers to whether the investigation actually tests what it is intended to test.

A valid experiment should have appropriate variables, controls and a suitable method.

10. Using a Microscope

A light microscope is used to observe structures that are too small to be seen clearly with the unaided eye.

Basic Procedure

  1. Place the prepared slide on the stage.
  2. Secure the slide with the clips.
  3. Begin with the lowest-power objective.
  4. Use the coarse adjustment to obtain approximate focus.
  5. Use the fine adjustment to sharpen the image.
  6. Move to a higher magnification if necessary.
  7. Use the fine adjustment to obtain a clear image.

11. Magnification

Total magnification is calculated by multiplying eyepiece magnification by objective magnification.

Total magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification

12. Biological Drawings

A biological drawing should show the important structures clearly and accurately.

  • Use a sharp pencil.
  • Draw clear single lines.
  • Do not shade unnecessarily.
  • Use accurate proportions.
  • Label structures using straight lines.
  • Give the drawing a suitable title.
  • Include magnification or scale when required.

13. Food Tests

14. Testing a Leaf for Starch

Procedure

  1. Boil the leaf in water briefly.
  2. Heat the leaf in ethanol using a water bath to remove chlorophyll.
  3. Rinse the leaf in warm water to soften it.
  4. Add iodine solution.
  5. Observe the colour change.

A blue-black colour indicates the presence of starch.

15. Osmosis Experiment

Potato strips can be used to investigate the movement of water through partially permeable membranes.

Variables

  • Independent variable: concentration of surrounding solution.
  • Dependent variable: change in mass or length of potato.
  • Controlled variables: size of potato pieces, temperature, volume of solution and time.

Water moves by osmosis from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane.

16. Photosynthesis Experiment

An aquatic plant can be used to investigate factors affecting photosynthesis.

One method is to measure oxygen production by counting bubbles or collecting gas over a measured time.

Important Controls

  • Keep plant size similar.
  • Keep temperature constant.
  • Control carbon dioxide concentration.
  • Allow sufficient time for measurements.
  • Repeat measurements.

17. Enzyme Experiments

Enzyme investigations can examine the effects of temperature, pH or substrate concentration on reaction rate.

The independent variable should be changed systematically while other important factors are controlled.

18. Respiration Experiment

Respiration can be investigated by measuring oxygen consumption or carbon dioxide production.

For germinating seeds, an appropriate apparatus can be used to measure changes in gas volume or pressure.

19. Transpiration Experiment

A potometer can be used to estimate water uptake by a leafy shoot.

Water uptake is used as an indirect measure of transpiration because most of the water absorbed by the plant is eventually lost through transpiration.

20. Graphs

Graphs are used to show relationships between variables.

Rules for Good Graphs

  • Put the independent variable on the horizontal axis.
  • Put the dependent variable on the vertical axis.
  • Label both axes with quantities and units.
  • Use an appropriate scale.
  • Plot points accurately.
  • Draw a suitable line or curve of best fit when appropriate.

21. Tables

Results should be organised in a clear table.

  • Give each column a clear heading.
  • Include units in headings where appropriate.
  • Keep decimal places consistent when appropriate.
  • Record measurements accurately.

22. Mean

The mean is calculated by adding the measurements and dividing by the number of measurements.

Mean = total of measurements ÷ number of measurements

23. Anomalous Results

An anomalous result is a measurement that differs substantially from the general pattern of the other results.

Possible causes include measurement error, contamination, equipment problems or mistakes in following the procedure.

24. Sources of Experimental Error

  • Inaccurate measuring instruments.
  • Human reading errors.
  • Temperature fluctuations.
  • Contamination.
  • Uncontrolled variables.
  • Small sample size.
  • Incorrect timing.

25. Improving an Investigation

  • Repeat measurements.
  • Use a larger sample size.
  • Control important variables.
  • Use calibrated equipment.
  • Improve measurement technique.
  • Use more appropriate apparatus.
  • Average repeated measurements when appropriate.

MANEB-Style Practice

  1. Define an independent variable.
  2. Define a dependent variable.
  3. State two controlled variables in an osmosis experiment.
  4. State the positive result for a starch test.
  5. What is the purpose of ethanol in testing a leaf for starch?
  6. State two rules for drawing a biological specimen.
  7. Calculate the total magnification when the eyepiece is ×10 and the objective is ×40.
  8. Why should an experiment be repeated?
  9. What is an anomalous result?
  10. State four ways of improving the reliability of an experiment.

Answers

  1. The independent variable is the factor deliberately changed by the investigator.
  2. The dependent variable is the factor measured or observed.
  3. Examples include potato piece size, temperature, volume of solution and duration of the experiment.
  4. Blue-black colour.
  5. Ethanol removes chlorophyll so that the iodine colour change can be observed clearly.
  6. Use clear single lines, label accurately, avoid unnecessary shading and maintain correct proportions.
  7. 10 × 40 = ×400.
  8. Repeating an experiment improves reliability and helps identify anomalous results.
  9. An anomalous result is a measurement that does not fit the general pattern of the other results.
  10. Repeat measurements, increase sample size, control variables and use accurate or calibrated equipment.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not put the dependent variable on the horizontal axis.
  • Do not forget units on graph axes.
  • Do not call every unusual result an error without considering possible causes.
  • Do not confuse accuracy with reliability.
  • Do not use unsafe methods when heating ethanol; a water bath should be used.

Examination Tips

  • Learn the purpose of each step in common practical investigations.
  • Always identify independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  • Practise drawing tables and graphs from experimental data.
  • When asked to improve an experiment, give specific improvements rather than simply saying “repeat it”.
  • Know common food tests and their positive results.
