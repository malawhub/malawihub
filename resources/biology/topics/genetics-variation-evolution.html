Genetics, Variation & Evolution

Genes, chromosomes, inheritance, variation, mutation, natural selection and evolution.

Learning Objectives

  • Define genes, alleles, chromosomes and DNA.
  • Explain inheritance of characteristics.
  • Use genetic diagrams to solve inheritance problems.
  • Distinguish genotype from phenotype.
  • Explain dominant and recessive alleles.
  • Describe sources of variation.
  • Explain mutation and its effects.
  • Explain natural selection and evolution.
  • Describe selective breeding and its applications.

1. DNA and Genetic Information

DNA is the molecule that carries genetic information in cells.

A gene is a section of DNA that contains information used to determine a particular characteristic or to produce a functional product.

2. Chromosomes

Chromosomes are structures made mainly of DNA and associated proteins. They carry genes.

In humans, body cells normally contain 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs.

3. Alleles

An allele is an alternative form of a gene.

For a particular gene, an individual may inherit different alleles from the two parents.

4. Genotype and Phenotype

Genotype refers to the alleles an individual possesses for a particular gene or genes.

Phenotype refers to the observable characteristics of an organism.

Phenotype can be influenced by both genotype and environmental factors.

5. Dominant and Recessive Alleles

A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype when at least one copy is present.

A recessive allele is normally expressed only when two copies are present in a diploid individual.

Dominant does not mean more common, stronger or better than a recessive allele.

6. Homozygous and Heterozygous

  • Homozygous: having two identical alleles for a gene.
  • Heterozygous: having two different alleles for a gene.

7. Genetic Inheritance

During sexual reproduction, offspring receive genetic material from both parents.

Gametes are haploid and contain one allele of each gene pair. During fertilisation, the gametes combine to restore the diploid chromosome number.

8. Genetic Diagrams

Genetic diagrams can be used to predict possible offspring genotypes and phenotypes.

Example: Tt × Tt

T t
T TT Tt
t Tt tt

The predicted genotype ratio is 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

If T is completely dominant, the phenotype ratio is 3 dominant : 1 recessive.

9. Sex Determination in Humans

Human females normally have XX sex chromosomes, while human males normally have XY sex chromosomes.

Egg cells normally carry an X chromosome. Sperm cells can carry either X or Y.

The sperm chromosome therefore determines whether the resulting embryo is typically XX or XY.

10. Variation

Variation refers to differences in characteristics between individuals of the same species.

Genetic Causes

  • Mutation
  • Meiosis
  • Independent assortment of chromosomes
  • Crossing over
  • Random fertilisation

Environmental Causes

  • Nutrition
  • Temperature
  • Light
  • Exercise
  • Diseases

Many characteristics are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors.

11. Continuous and Discontinuous Variation

Continuous Variation

Continuous variation shows a range of values with many possible intermediate forms.

Examples include height and body mass in humans.

Discontinuous Variation

Discontinuous variation places individuals into distinct categories.

Some genetic blood-group systems are examples of discontinuous variation.

12. Mutation

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence of an organism.

Mutations can arise spontaneously or as a result of exposure to certain mutagenic agents.

Some mutations have little or no observable effect, some are harmful, and some can be beneficial depending on the environment.

13. Natural Selection

Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics that improve survival or reproduction tend to leave more offspring.

Basic Sequence

  1. Individuals in a population show variation.
  2. Some variation is inherited.
  3. There is competition for limited resources.
  4. Individuals with advantageous characteristics are more likely to survive and reproduce.
  5. They pass the relevant alleles to their offspring.
  6. Over many generations, advantageous alleles can become more common.

14. Evolution

Evolution is the change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations.

Natural selection is an important mechanism through which evolutionary change can occur.

15. Adaptation

An adaptation is an inherited characteristic that increases an organism's ability to survive and reproduce in a particular environment.

Examples include camouflage, specialised feeding structures and physiological adaptations.

16. Evidence for Evolution

Evidence for evolution includes:

  • Fossils.
  • Comparative anatomy.
  • Embryological evidence.
  • DNA and molecular similarities.
  • Observed changes in populations.

17. Selective Breeding

Selective breeding is the deliberate selection of organisms with desirable characteristics and breeding them to increase those characteristics in future generations.

Examples

  • Higher-yielding crops.
  • Disease-resistant plants.
  • Animals producing more milk or meat.
  • Improved livestock characteristics.

18. Genetic Engineering and Inheritance

Genetic engineering can introduce a particular gene into an organism. This differs from selective breeding because it can directly transfer genetic material rather than relying only on reproduction between selected individuals.

19. Importance of Genetic Variation

Genetic variation provides the raw material on which natural selection can act.

Without inherited variation, populations have less ability to respond evolutionarily to changing environmental conditions.

20. Evolution and Antibiotic Resistance

When a bacterial population contains variation in resistance, antibiotic treatment can kill susceptible bacteria while resistant bacteria survive.

The resistant bacteria reproduce and pass resistance alleles to their offspring. Over generations, resistant bacteria can become a larger proportion of the population.

  5. State two causes of genetic variation.
  6. Define mutation.
  7. Explain natural selection.
  8. Explain how natural selection can result in evolution.

Answers

  1. A gene is a section of DNA containing genetic information.
  2. An allele is an alternative form of a gene.
  3. Genotype refers to the alleles possessed by an individual, while phenotype refers to observable characteristics.
  4. Homozygous means having two identical alleles, while heterozygous means having two different alleles.
  5. Mutation, meiosis, crossing over, independent assortment and random fertilisation.
  6. A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence.
  7. Natural selection favours inherited characteristics that increase survival or reproductive success in a particular environment.
  8. Individuals with advantageous inherited characteristics leave more offspring, causing associated alleles to become more common over generations.
  9. Fossils, comparative anatomy, DNA similarities and embryological evidence are examples.
  10. Selective breeding involves choosing organisms with desirable characteristics and breeding them to increase those characteristics in later generations.
  11. Susceptible bacteria are killed by the antibiotic while resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, increasing the frequency of resistance in the population.
  12. 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse a gene with an allele.
  • Do not say that dominant means stronger or more common.
  • Remember that evolution occurs in populations over generations, not because an individual chooses to change.
  • Do not describe natural selection as organisms developing characteristics because they need them.
  • Distinguish inherited variation from variation caused only by the environment.

Examination Tips

  • Practise Punnett squares and genetic ratios.
  • Learn the meanings of gene, allele, chromosome, genotype and phenotype.
  • When explaining natural selection, always mention variation, inheritance, selection, reproduction and change over generations.
  • Use clear genetic diagrams when a question asks for predicted offspring.
  • For evolution questions, explain changes in allele frequencies or inherited characteristics across generations.
