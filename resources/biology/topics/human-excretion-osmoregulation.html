🧪 Human Excretion & Osmoregulation

Removal of metabolic wastes, kidney function, urine formation and water balance.

Learning Objectives

  • Define excretion and explain its importance.
  • Identify the main human excretory organs.
  • Describe the structure and function of the kidney.
  • Describe the structure and function of a nephron.
  • Explain ultrafiltration and selective reabsorption.
  • Explain osmoregulation and the role of ADH.
  • Describe kidney failure, dialysis and kidney transplantation.

1. Excretion

Excretion is the removal of toxic metabolic waste products and substances present in excess from the body.

Excretion is different from egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food from the digestive tract, whereas excretion involves metabolic waste products.

2. Importance of Excretion

Metabolic wastes can be harmful if they accumulate in the body. Excretion prevents toxic substances from reaching dangerous concentrations and helps maintain a stable internal environment.

3. Main Excretory Organs

Organ Main Excretory Product
Lungs Carbon dioxide and water vapour
Kidneys Urea, excess water and excess mineral ions
Skin Water, mineral ions and small amounts of urea
Liver Forms urea from excess amino acids and processes harmful substances

4. Formation of Urea

Excess amino acids cannot be stored in the body in the same way as glucose or fat.

In the liver, the nitrogen-containing part of excess amino acids is removed. This process is called deamination.

Ammonia produced during this process is converted into the less toxic compound urea. Urea is transported in the blood to the kidneys for removal.

5. Human Urinary System

The urinary system consists mainly of:

  • Two kidneys
  • Two ureters
  • Urinary bladder
  • Urethra

Functions

  • Kidneys: filter blood and form urine.
  • Ureters: carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder.
  • Bladder: stores urine temporarily.
  • Urethra: carries urine out of the body.

6. Structure of the Kidney

The kidney contains an outer cortex, an inner medulla and a central renal pelvis.

The renal artery brings blood to the kidney, while the renal vein carries filtered blood away.

7. Nephron

A nephron is the functional unit of the kidney.

Important parts include:

  • Bowman's capsule
  • Glomerulus
  • Proximal convoluted tubule
  • Loop of Henle
  • Distal convoluted tubule
  • Collecting duct

8. Ultrafiltration

Ultrafiltration occurs in the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule.

High blood pressure forces small molecules such as water, glucose, mineral ions and urea from the blood into Bowman's capsule.

Large plasma proteins and blood cells are normally too large to pass through the filtration barrier and remain in the blood.

9. Selective Reabsorption

Useful substances are reabsorbed from the filtrate back into the blood.

  • All or almost all glucose is normally reabsorbed.
  • Useful mineral ions are reabsorbed according to the body's needs.
  • Most water is reabsorbed.

Active transport is important for the reabsorption of some substances, while water can move by osmosis.

10. Urine

Urine contains substances that the body needs to remove, including urea, excess water and excess mineral ions.

Urine passes from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis, then through the ureter to the bladder.

11. Osmoregulation

Osmoregulation is the control of the water and dissolved-solute balance of body fluids.

The kidneys help maintain the correct water content of the blood by adjusting how much water is reabsorbed.

12. ADH

Antidiuretic hormone, or ADH, helps regulate the amount of water reabsorbed by the kidneys.

When the blood becomes too concentrated, more ADH is released. The collecting ducts become more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood.

This produces a smaller volume of more concentrated urine.

When the blood contains excess water, less ADH is released. Less water is reabsorbed, producing a larger volume of more dilute urine.

13. Water Balance

Condition ADH Level Water Reabsorption Urine
Blood too concentrated High More Small volume, concentrated
Blood too dilute Low Less Large volume, dilute

14. Kidney Failure

Kidney failure occurs when the kidneys cannot perform their functions effectively.

Possible consequences include accumulation of urea and other wastes, disturbance of water and ion balance and serious changes in the internal environment.

Some Kidney Disorders

  • Kidney stones
  • Nephritis
  • Kidney failure
  • Some infections and other conditions that damage kidney tissue

15. Dialysis

Dialysis can help remove waste products and excess substances from the blood when the kidneys are severely impaired.

Blood is passed through a dialysis machine where waste products and excess ions diffuse into dialysis fluid across a partially permeable membrane.

The dialysis fluid is designed to contain suitable concentrations of useful substances so that excessive loss of important materials is reduced.

16. Kidney Transplant

A kidney transplant involves replacing a severely damaged kidney with a functioning kidney from a suitable donor.

Transplantation can provide long-term treatment for some patients with kidney failure, although problems such as tissue rejection can occur.

17. Investigating Urine

Urine can be tested for substances that may indicate abnormal kidney function or other health conditions.

For example, glucose or significant amounts of protein in urine may indicate that further medical investigation is required.

  1. Define excretion.
  2. Distinguish between excretion and egestion.
  3. State two substances excreted by the kidneys.
  4. Explain how urea is formed.
  5. Name the main parts of the urinary system.
  6. State the function of the nephron.
  7. Explain ultrafiltration.
  8. Explain selective reabsorption.
  9. Define osmoregulation.
  10. Explain the role of ADH when the blood becomes too concentrated.
  11. State two consequences of kidney failure.
  12. Explain how dialysis helps a person with kidney failure.

Answers

  1. Removal of toxic metabolic waste products and substances present in excess from the body.
  2. Excretion removes metabolic wastes, while egestion removes undigested food from the digestive tract.
  3. Urea and excess water. Excess mineral ions are also excreted.
  4. The nitrogen-containing part of excess amino acids is removed by deamination in the liver, and the resulting ammonia is converted into urea.
  5. Kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder and urethra.
  6. The nephron filters blood and processes the filtrate to form urine while returning useful substances to the blood.
  7. High pressure in the glomerulus forces small molecules from the blood into Bowman's capsule.
  8. Useful substances such as glucose, appropriate mineral ions and most water are returned from the filtrate to the blood.
  9. Osmoregulation is the control of the water and dissolved-solute balance of body fluids.
  10. More ADH is released, increasing water reabsorption and producing a smaller volume of concentrated urine.
  11. Accumulation of urea and disruption of water and mineral-ion balance.
  12. Dialysis removes waste products and excess substances from the blood by allowing them to move across a partially permeable membrane into dialysis fluid.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse excretion with egestion.
  • Remember that urea is formed mainly in the liver, not the kidney.
  • Do not say that all substances filtered at the glomerulus are excreted.
  • Remember that selective reabsorption returns useful substances to the blood.
  • High ADH generally means more water is reabsorbed and urine becomes more concentrated.

Examination Tips

  • Practise drawing and labelling the urinary system and nephron.
  • Learn the sequence of ultrafiltration, selective reabsorption and urine formation.
  • For ADH questions, clearly connect blood concentration, ADH level, water reabsorption and urine concentration.
  • Know the differences between dialysis and kidney transplantation.
