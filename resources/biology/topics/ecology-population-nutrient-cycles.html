Ecology, Population & Nutrient Cycles

Ecological organisation, food chains, populations, communities, ecosystems and nutrient cycling.

Learning Objectives

  • Define habitat, population, community and ecosystem.
  • Distinguish between biotic and abiotic factors.
  • Construct and interpret food chains and food webs.
  • Explain energy flow through ecosystems.
  • Describe population growth and limiting factors.
  • Explain competition between organisms.
  • Describe the carbon and nitrogen cycles.
  • Explain the importance of decomposers.
  • Explain human effects on ecosystems.
  • Suggest methods of environmental conservation.

1. Ecology

Ecology is the study of interactions between organisms and between organisms and their physical environment.

2. Habitat

A habitat is the place where an organism normally lives and obtains the conditions and resources it needs for survival.

Examples include forests, grasslands, rivers, lakes, wetlands and agricultural fields.

3. Population

A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in the same area at the same time.

4. Community

A community consists of populations of different species living and interacting in the same area.

5. Ecosystem

An ecosystem consists of a community of organisms together with the non-living components of their environment.

6. Biotic and Abiotic Factors

Factor Meaning Examples
Biotic Living components or interactions Predation, competition, disease, food availability
Abiotic Non-living environmental conditions Temperature, light intensity, rainfall, pH, soil minerals

7. Producers

Producers are organisms that manufacture organic food from inorganic substances, usually using energy from sunlight.

Green plants and algae are important producers in many ecosystems.

8. Consumers

Consumers obtain organic nutrients by feeding on other organisms.

  • Primary consumers: feed mainly on producers.
  • Secondary consumers: feed on primary consumers.
  • Tertiary consumers: feed on secondary consumers.

9. Decomposers

Decomposers, particularly bacteria and fungi, break down dead organisms and waste material.

They release nutrients back into the environment and play an important role in nutrient cycles.

10. Food Chains

A food chain shows the transfer of food energy and nutrients from one organism to another.

Example: maize → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

The arrow points from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it.

11. Food Webs

A food web consists of interconnected food chains.

Food webs show that organisms often have more than one food source and may be eaten by several different organisms.

12. Trophic Levels

A trophic level is a feeding position in a food chain or food web.

Trophic Level Typical Organism
First Producer
Second Primary consumer
Third Secondary consumer
Fourth Tertiary consumer

13. Energy Flow

Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight. Producers convert light energy into chemical energy stored in organic compounds.

Energy is transferred between trophic levels when organisms feed.

Energy is lost at each trophic level through processes such as respiration, movement, growth, heat loss and waste.

14. Pyramids of Energy

A pyramid of energy represents the amount of energy available at different trophic levels.

The amount of energy available generally decreases at higher trophic levels because energy is lost during transfer.

15. Competition

Competition occurs when organisms require the same limited resource.

Examples of Resources

  • Food
  • Water
  • Light
  • Mineral nutrients
  • Space
  • Mates

Competition can occur between members of the same species or between different species.

16. Predation

Predation is an interaction in which one organism, the predator, captures and feeds on another organism, the prey.

Changes in predator numbers can affect prey populations, while prey availability can influence predator populations.

17. Population Size

Population size is influenced by births, deaths, immigration and emigration.

Population change = births + immigration − deaths − emigration

18. Population Growth

When resources are abundant and environmental resistance is low, a population may increase rapidly.

As resources become limited, competition increases and population growth slows.

19. Limiting Factors

A limiting factor is a condition that restricts the size or growth of a population.

  • Food availability
  • Water availability
  • Predation
  • Disease
  • Competition
  • Temperature
  • Light
  • Space
  • Availability of mineral nutrients

20. Carrying Capacity

Carrying capacity is the maximum population size that an environment can support sustainably under particular conditions.

It can change when environmental conditions or resource availability change.

21. Sampling Populations

Biologists can estimate population size or distribution using sampling methods.

Quadrats

Quadrats are frames placed randomly or systematically in an area to estimate the abundance or distribution of organisms such as plants.

Transects

A transect is a line across an area along which organisms and environmental conditions are recorded.

Mark-Release-Recapture

Animals can be captured, marked harmlessly, released and later recaptured to estimate population size.

22. Carbon Cycle

Carbon moves continuously between the atmosphere, organisms, soil, oceans and other parts of the environment.

Main Processes

  • Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.
  • Feeding transfers carbon between organisms.
  • Respiration releases carbon dioxide.
  • Decomposition releases carbon compounds and carbon dioxide.
  • Combustion releases carbon dioxide.

23. Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is essential for making proteins and nucleic acids.

Atmospheric nitrogen cannot normally be used directly by most plants. Nitrogen must be converted into usable forms through biological and chemical processes.

Important Processes

  • Nitrogen fixation.
  • Nitrification.
  • Plant uptake.
  • Feeding.
  • Death and decomposition.
  • Ammonification.
  • Denitrification.

24. Nitrogen Fixation

Nitrogen fixation converts atmospheric nitrogen into compounds that can enter the nitrogen cycle.

Some nitrogen-fixing bacteria live freely in soil, while others form associations with plants such as legumes.

25. Decomposition and Nutrient Recycling

Decomposers obtain nutrients and energy from dead organisms and waste.

They release mineral nutrients into the environment where they can become available for uptake by plants.

26. Human Effects on Ecosystems

  • Deforestation.
  • Overfishing.
  • Pollution.
  • Habitat destruction.
  • Unsustainable agriculture.
  • Mining.
  • Introduction of invasive species.
  • Climate change.

27. Deforestation

Deforestation is the removal of forests on a large scale.

It can cause habitat loss, soil erosion, reduced biodiversity and increased carbon dioxide in the atmosphere when forests are burned or decomposed.

28. Conservation

Conservation is the protection and sustainable management of natural resources and biodiversity.

Examples

  • Afforestation and reforestation.
  • Protected areas.
  • Sustainable farming.
  • Sustainable fishing.
  • Waste reduction and recycling.
  • Protection of endangered species.
  • Environmental education.

MANEB-Style Practice

  1. Define an ecosystem.
  2. Distinguish between biotic and abiotic factors.
  3. What is a population?
  4. Construct a food chain containing a producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer and tertiary consumer.
  5. State two functions of decomposers.
  6. Explain why energy decreases at higher trophic levels.
  7. State four factors that can limit population growth.
  8. Explain the importance of quadrats in ecological studies.
  9. State three processes in the carbon cycle that release carbon dioxide.
  10. Explain the role of nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
  11. State four effects of deforestation.
  12. Suggest four methods of conserving ecosystems.

Answers

  1. An ecosystem consists of a community of organisms and the non-living components of their environment.
  2. Biotic factors involve living organisms or their interactions, while abiotic factors are non-living environmental conditions.
  3. A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in the same area at the same time.
  4. Example: maize → grasshopper → frog → snake.
  5. They break down dead organisms and recycle nutrients.
  6. Energy is lost through respiration, heat, movement, waste and other processes during transfer between trophic levels.
  7. Food, water, disease, predation, competition, temperature, light and space are examples.
  8. Quadrats allow researchers to estimate abundance and distribution of organisms in an area.
  9. Respiration, decomposition and combustion.
  10. They convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms that can enter the nitrogen cycle and become available for biological use.
  11. Habitat loss, soil erosion, biodiversity loss and increased atmospheric carbon dioxide are examples.
  12. Protected areas, reforestation, sustainable farming, sustainable fishing, recycling and protection of endangered species.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse a habitat with an ecosystem.
  • Remember that arrows in food chains show the direction of energy and food transfer.
  • Do not say that energy is recycled in an ecosystem; nutrients are recycled but energy flows through the system.
  • Do not confuse population with community.
  • Remember that decomposers are important for nutrient recycling.

Examination Tips

  • Practise constructing food chains and food webs correctly.
  • Learn the definitions of habitat, population, community and ecosystem.
  • Be able to explain limiting factors using specific examples.
  • Practise explaining the carbon and nitrogen cycles in logical sequences.
  • For environmental questions, link human activities to their effects and then suggest realistic conservation measures.
