Topic 1: Elements and the periodic table

1.1 Metals, non-metals and metalloids

Elements can be broadly grouped by their properties. Metals are generally shiny, conduct heat and electricity and can be shaped. Non-metals are often poor conductors and may be gases or brittle solids. Metalloids show a mixture of metallic and non-metallic properties; silicon is a familiar example.

1.2 Periodic trends

The periodic table arranges elements by increasing atomic number. Atomic radius describes atomic size. It generally increases down a group as additional electron shells are occupied and generally decreases across a period as nuclear charge increases within the same main shell.

First ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom. It generally decreases down a group because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded. It generally increases across a period, although there are exceptions. Electron affinity describes the energy change when an isolated gaseous atom gains an electron. In the simplified trend used at this level, the attraction for an added electron generally becomes stronger across a period and weaker down a group, but measured electron-affinity values have important exceptions. Electronegativity is an atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a bond; it generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.

TrendDown a group (general)Across a period (general)
Atomic radiusIncreasesDecreases
First ionisation energyDecreasesIncreases
ElectronegativityDecreasesIncreases

1.3 Group I — alkali metals

Lithium, sodium and potassium are soft, shiny when freshly cut and good conductors. They have one outer electron and form +1 ions. They react with oxygen to form oxides (the exact product depends on the metal and conditions) and react with water to form a metal hydroxide and hydrogen. Reactivity increases down the group, so potassium reacts more vigorously than sodium, which reacts more vigorously than lithium.

2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)
4Li(s) + O₂(g) → 2Li₂O(s)

Uses include lithium compounds in rechargeable batteries and specialised greases; sodium compounds in glass, soap and detergents; and potassium compounds in fertilisers. Alkali metals are stored and handled under controlled conditions because of their reactivity.

1.4 Group II — alkaline earth metals

Group II includes beryllium, magnesium and calcium. They have two outer electrons and commonly form +2 ions. They react with oxygen to form oxides. Calcium reacts with cold water more readily than magnesium; magnesium reacts more readily with steam than with cold water. Reactivity generally increases down the group.

2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Magnesium is used in lightweight alloys and bright flares; calcium compounds are important in cement, lime and construction materials. Beryllium and its compounds are hazardous and require specialist controls.

1.5 Group VII — halogens

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine are diatomic non-metals. At room temperature, fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid. Down the group, colour becomes darker and melting/boiling points increase. Reactivity generally decreases down the group because gaining an electron becomes less favourable.

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution. Chlorine displaces bromide and iodide ions; bromine displaces iodide ions.

Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq)

Chlorine is used to disinfect water under carefully controlled conditions; iodine compounds are used in antiseptics and iodised salt; fluoride compounds help protect teeth. Halogens and their compounds must be handled carefully.

1.6 Group VIII/18 — noble gases

Helium, neon and argon are colourless, monoatomic gases with full outer electron shells. They are very unreactive. Helium is used in balloons and specialised cooling, neon in illuminated signs and argon as a shielding gas in welding and in some lamps.

  5. Give two uses of noble gases.

Topic 2: Chemical bonding

2.1 Why atoms bond

Atoms form chemical bonds when the resulting arrangement is more stable and usually lower in energy. In many simple compounds, atoms gain, lose or share outer-shell electrons to achieve a stable outer electron arrangement.

Valence electrons are electrons in the outermost occupied shell. Valency is the combining capacity of an atom or ion. A cation is a positive ion formed by loss of electrons; an anion is a negative ion formed by gain of electrons.

2.2 Ionic bonding

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It commonly forms when a metal transfers electrons to a non-metal. The metal becomes a cation and the non-metal becomes an anion.

Example: magnesium loses two electrons and each chlorine atom gains one. One Mg²⁺ ion is attracted to two Cl⁻ ions, giving MgCl₂. The total charge must be zero in a neutral ionic compound.

Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
2Cl + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻
Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → MgCl₂

2.3 Deducing ionic formulae

  1. Write the cation and anion with their charges.
  2. Choose the smallest whole-number ratio that makes total positive and negative charge equal.
  3. Write the formula without the charges.
Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ → CaCl₂
Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → Al₂O₃
Na⁺ and O²⁻ → Na₂O

2.4 Properties of ionic compounds

Ionic solids usually have high melting and boiling points because strong electrostatic attractions hold the ions in a lattice. They are often hard and brittle. Many dissolve in water, but solubility varies. They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved because ions can move; they do not conduct as solids because the ions are fixed in place.

2.5 Covalent bonding

Covalent bonding involves sharing one or more pairs of electrons between non-metal atoms. A single bond is one shared pair; a double bond is two shared pairs; a triple bond is three shared pairs.

Each pair of dots represents a shared pair of electrons.

Simple covalent substances often have relatively low melting and boiling points because the forces between molecules are weak. They generally do not conduct electricity because they lack mobile ions or electrons. Some giant covalent structures, such as diamond and silicon dioxide, have very high melting points; graphite conducts electricity. Therefore, properties depend on structure.

2.6 Metallic bonding

Metallic bonding is the attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and delocalised electrons that can move through the structure. Mobile electrons explain why metals conduct electricity and heat. Layers of metal ions can slide while the attraction is maintained, helping metals to be malleable and ductile. Metals are often shiny and many have high melting points, although there are exceptions.

Topic 3: Acids and bases

3.1 Properties of acids and bases

Acids in water produce hydrogen ions (more accurately, hydronium ions). They have pH below 7, turn blue litmus red and react with bases. Alkalis are soluble bases; they produce hydroxide ions in water, have pH above 7 and turn red litmus blue. Never identify chemicals by tasting them.

IndicatorIn acidIn alkali
LitmusRedBlue
Methyl orangeRedYellow
PhenolphthaleinColourlessPink
Universal indicatorRed/orange/yellow depending on pHBlue/purple depending on pH

3.2 The pH scale

The pH scale commonly runs from 0 to 14 for school-level aqueous solutions. pH 7 is neutral at about room temperature; values below 7 are acidic and values above 7 are alkaline. The farther a value is from 7, the more strongly acidic or alkaline the solution is. Universal indicator gives an approximate pH, while a pH meter can provide a more precise reading.

3.3 Electrical conductivity

Acidic and alkaline aqueous solutions conduct electricity because they contain mobile ions. A solution with more mobile ions may conduct better, but brightness of a bulb also depends on concentration, electrode spacing, electrode area and the circuit. A fair test keeps these conditions constant.

3.4 Reactions of acids

Acid + reactive metal → salt + hydrogen
Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
Acid + base/alkali → salt + water
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
2HCl + Na₂CO₃ → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂
Acid + metal oxide → salt + water
CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O

The salt name depends on the acid: hydrochloric acid forms chlorides, sulphuric acid forms sulphates and nitric acid forms nitrates. Use a balanced equation and check atom counts.

3.5 Uses of acids and bases

Sulphuric acid is used in lead-acid batteries and industry; hydrochloric acid is present in gastric juice and used in chemical processing; nitric acid is used in fertiliser manufacture. Sodium hydroxide is used in soap and paper manufacture; calcium hydroxide is used to reduce soil acidity; magnesium hydroxide is used in some antacid preparations. Handle all corrosive substances with appropriate protection.

3.6 Acid-base indicators

Commercial indicators include litmus, methyl orange, phenolphthalein and universal indicator. A local indicator can be prepared from coloured plant material such as hibiscus petals: chop the petals, warm or boil gently in a little water, cool and filter. Test separate portions with a known acidic solution such as vinegar and a mild alkaline solution such as sodium bicarbonate solution. A useful indicator changes colour differently in the two conditions.

3.7 Neutralisation

Neutralisation is the reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and usually water. It is applied when correcting acidic soil with agricultural lime, and in antacid products that neutralise excess stomach acid.

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Topic 4: Hydrocarbons

4.1 Organic families and homologous series

Hydrocarbons are compounds containing carbon and hydrogen only. A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same functional group and general formula, similar chemical reactions and a gradual change in physical properties. Consecutive members differ by a –CH₂– unit. Common families include alkanes, alkenes, alkynes and alkanols; alkanoic acids form another important family of organic compounds.

4.2 Saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons

Saturated hydrocarbons have carbon–carbon single bonds only. Unsaturated hydrocarbons contain at least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond. Alkanes are saturated; alkenes contain a C=C double bond.

4.3 Alkanes

Alkanes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for open-chain molecules with single bonds. The first ten straight-chain alkanes are methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀, pentane C₅H₁₂, hexane C₆H₁₄, heptane C₇H₁₆, octane C₈H₁₈, nonane C₉H₂₀ and decane C₁₀H₂₂.

Carbon atomsPrefixAlkaneFormula
1Meth-MethaneCH₄
2Eth-EthaneC₂H₆
3Prop-PropaneC₃H₈
4But-ButaneC₄H₁₀
5Pent-PentaneC₅H₁₂
6Hex-HexaneC₆H₁₄

Organic formulae can be represented in different ways: molecular formula (atom counts), full structural formula (all bonds), condensed structural formula (groups atoms together) and skeletal formula (carbon framework drawn as lines).

4.4 Physical and chemical properties of alkanes

Small alkanes are gases, medium-chain members are liquids and very large members may be waxy solids at room temperature. Boiling points and viscosity generally increase with chain length. Alkanes are non-polar, insoluble in water and do not conduct electricity.

Complete combustion in excess oxygen forms carbon dioxide and water; incomplete combustion with insufficient oxygen can form carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot) as well as water. Alkanes also undergo substitution with halogens in ultraviolet light, where a hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (complete combustion)
2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O (one possible incomplete equation)
CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (UV light)

4.5 Sources and uses of alkanes

Alkanes are obtained mainly from crude oil and natural gas. Methane is a fuel; propane and butane are used in LPG for cooking and heating; petrol contains hydrocarbons used in vehicle engines. Larger hydrocarbons can be cracked to form smaller, more useful molecules, including alkenes.

4.6 Alkenes

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons with at least one carbon–carbon double bond. The open-chain mono-alkenes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ. The smallest is ethene, C₂H₄. Names end in –ene: ethene, propene, butene and so on. Number the chain to give the double bond the lowest possible position.

Ethene: CH₂=CH₂
Propene: CH₂=CH–CH₃
But-1-ene: CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃

4.7 Naming, sources and physical properties of alkenes

Name an alkene using the longest carbon chain containing the C=C bond. Replace the ending –ane with –ene and number from the end nearest the double bond. For example, CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃ is but-1-ene, while CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃ is but-2-ene. The position number is important when the double bond can occur in more than one place.

Alkenes are produced mainly by cracking long-chain hydrocarbons from petroleum. Small alkenes are gases at room temperature; larger members have higher boiling points as molecular size increases. They are generally insoluble in water and do not conduct electricity.

Alkenes can be obtained by cracking larger hydrocarbons and by dehydration of suitable alcohols. They undergo addition reactions because the double bond can open: hydrogenation adds H₂, halogenation adds a halogen, and hydration adds water under suitable conditions. They also combust in oxygen. Ethene is used to manufacture poly(ethene) and to promote fruit ripening; other alkenes are important starting materials for plastics and industrial chemicals.

C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ (hydrogenation, catalyst)
C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ (bromine addition)
C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH (hydration, suitable catalyst/conditions)
C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O
Test for unsaturation: bromine water changes from orange/brown to colourless when it reacts with an alkene under suitable conditions. An alkane does not rapidly decolourise bromine water in the absence of ultraviolet light.

Topic 5: Air

5.1 Composition of air

Air is a mixture of gases. Dry air near sea level contains approximately 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and small quantities of noble gases and other gases. Water vapour and dust vary with location and weather.

Approximate composition; diagram is schematic and not intended for precise measurement.

5.2 Separating gases from air

Air can be cooled until it liquefies, then separated by fractional distillation. The liquid mixture is warmed gradually. Nitrogen, with the lower boiling point, vaporises and is collected before oxygen. The process uses differences in boiling points.

5.3 Uses of gases in air

  • Nitrogen: dilutes oxygen in air; used to manufacture ammonia and provide inert atmospheres. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume root nodules help convert atmospheric nitrogen into compounds plants can use.
  • Oxygen: needed for aerobic respiration and supports combustion; used in medical oxygen supplies, metal cutting and steelmaking.
  • Carbon dioxide: used by plants in photosynthesis and in some fire extinguishers and carbonated drinks.
  • Noble gases: argon is used in lamps and welding; helium in balloons and specialised cooling; neon in signs.

5.4 Oxygen in air: simple investigation

A school demonstration can estimate the oxygen fraction by trapping air above water and allowing a burning candle or other suitable oxygen-consuming process to use some of the oxygen. As the flame goes out and the apparatus cools, water rises into the container. The volume decrease is used as an estimate of oxygen consumed. Results are affected by carbon dioxide dissolving, temperature changes, leaks and the exact setup, so this is an approximate method rather than a precise gas analysis.

Estimated oxygen percentage = (decrease in gas volume ÷ initial gas volume) × 100%

5.5 Oxygen properties and preparation

Oxygen is a colourless, odourless gas, slightly soluble in water and slightly denser than air. It supports combustion but is not itself a fuel. In the laboratory it can be prepared by decomposing hydrogen peroxide using manganese(IV) oxide as a catalyst.

2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) (MnO₂ catalyst)

5.6 Chemical properties and uses of oxygen

Oxygen supports combustion and reacts with many elements to form oxides. Metals commonly form metal oxides, while non-metals form non-metal oxides. The reactions below illustrate these patterns.

2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)
S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g)

Oxygen is used in medical treatment when prescribed, in oxy-fuel metal cutting and welding, in steel manufacture and in some chemical industries. Oxygen itself is not a fuel, but it makes combustion more vigorous. Keep oxygen cylinders and oxygen-enriched environments away from oil, grease, flames and combustible materials.

Ozone has different effects depending on where it occurs: stratospheric ozone protects life by absorbing harmful ultraviolet radiation, while ground-level ozone is an air pollutant that irritates the lungs. Chlorine-containing compounds such as some older refrigerants can contribute to stratospheric ozone depletion.

5.8 Reducing air pollution

  • Improve combustion efficiency and maintain engines and industrial equipment.
  • Use cleaner fuels and reduce sulphur in fuels.
  • Install suitable filters and emission-control systems.
  • Avoid open burning of rubbish and reduce smoke from household cooking.
  • Protect forests, use energy efficiently and expand cleaner energy options.

Topic 6: Soil

6.1 What soil is

Soil is the upper layer of the Earth's surface in which many plants grow. It contains mineral particles from weathered rocks, organic matter, water, air and living organisms. The proportions vary from one soil to another.

6.2 Soil composition

A simplified ideal soil composition is often represented as about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air. These are approximate teaching values, not fixed proportions for every soil.

Approximate composition of an ideal mineral soil; actual values vary.
  • Mineral matter: sand, silt, clay and mineral nutrients such as compounds of calcium, potassium, iron and aluminium.
  • Organic matter (humus): decomposed plant and animal remains; improves soil structure and helps retain water and nutrients.
  • Water: dissolves mineral nutrients and allows them to move to plant roots.
  • Air: occupies pore spaces and supplies oxygen for roots and soil organisms.

6.3 Chemical composition and properties of soil

Soil minerals contain elements needed by plants. Important plant nutrients include nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, as well as calcium, magnesium and sulphur. Plants absorb many nutrients as dissolved ions, for example nitrate (NO₃⁻), ammonium (NH₄⁺), phosphate ions and potassium ions. The exact chemical composition varies with parent rock, climate, organisms and land use.

Soil pH indicates acidity or alkalinity and influences nutrient availability and microbial activity. Many crops grow well in slightly acidic to near-neutral soil, but the preferred range varies by crop. Very acidic or alkaline soil can make some nutrients unavailable or increase the solubility of harmful elements.

Soil salinity refers to the amount of dissolved salts in soil. Excess salts make it harder for plant roots to absorb water and can damage crops.

Cation exchange capacity (CEC) describes the soil's ability to hold and exchange positively charged nutrient ions such as calcium, magnesium, potassium and ammonium. Higher CEC often helps a soil retain nutrients, although soil texture and organic matter also matter.

6.4 Soil pollution

  • Agrochemicals: excessive or incorrect use of fertilisers, herbicides, insecticides and fungicides can harm soil organisms or contaminate water.
  • Oil and petroleum products: spills can coat soil particles and harm microorganisms and plants.
  • Solid waste: plastics, batteries, glass and industrial waste may persist or release harmful substances.
  • Air pollution and acid deposition: sulphur and nitrogen oxides can form acids that alter soil pH and leach nutrients.
  • Mining and industrial activity: may introduce metals and other contaminants.

6.5 Preventing soil pollution

  • Reduce, reuse and recycle waste; dispose of hazardous waste through approved systems.
  • Apply fertilisers and pesticides at recommended rates and use integrated pest management.
  • Prevent oil leaks and clean spills using appropriate procedures.
  • Use compost and suitable organic practices while monitoring soil nutrients.
  • Control industrial emissions and treat wastewater before discharge.
  • Protect vegetation and use erosion-control measures to keep fertile topsoil in place.
Practice
  1. Name the four major components of soil.
  2. Explain two functions of soil water and soil air.
  3. Define soil pH, salinity and cation exchange capacity.
  4. Describe three ways agrochemicals can affect soil.
  5. Suggest five practical ways to prevent soil pollution in a farming community.
