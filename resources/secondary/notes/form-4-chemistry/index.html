Topic 1: Rates of reactions

The rate of reaction describes how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. It is the change in amount, mass or concentration per unit time. A fast reaction produces a measurable change in a short time; a slow reaction takes longer.

1.1 Collision theory

Reacting particles must collide for a reaction to occur. However, not every collision produces a reaction. A collision is successful only when the particles have sufficient energy to overcome the activation energy and meet in a suitable orientation. Increasing the number of successful collisions per second increases the reaction rate.

Schematic: reactant particles collide and rearrange to form products when the collision is successful.

1.2 Factors affecting rate

  • Concentration: a more concentrated solution has more particles per unit volume, increasing collision frequency.
  • Temperature: particles move faster and a larger fraction have energy equal to or above activation energy.
  • Surface area: powdered solids expose more particles to the other reactant than large lumps do.
  • Catalyst: provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy and is regenerated at the end.

1.3 Measuring reaction rate

Rate can be followed by measuring gas volume, change in mass, time for a reactant to disappear or time for a set amount of product to form. Choose a method that measures a property changing during the reaction.

Average rate = total change ÷ total time
Average gas rate = volume of gas produced ÷ time taken
Rate between t₁ and t₂ = (V₂ − V₁) ÷ (t₂ − t₁)

For a gas-forming reaction, connect a flask to a gas syringe and record volume at regular intervals. Keep the apparatus airtight and start timing consistently. A graph of gas volume against time usually rises steeply at first and then levels off when a reactant is used up. The gradient represents rate: a steeper slope means a faster reaction.

Worked example

Magnesium reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid and produces 40 cm³ hydrogen in 5 minutes. Average rate = 40 ÷ 5 = 8 cm³ min⁻¹. If 14 cm³ is produced in the first minute and 25 cm³ by the end of the second minute, the second-minute rate is (25 − 14) ÷ 1 = 11 cm³ min⁻¹.

1.4 Reversible and irreversible reactions

A reversible reaction can proceed in both directions under suitable conditions. Heating hydrated copper(II) sulphate removes water and changes blue crystals to white anhydrous salt; adding water restores the blue hydrated crystals.

CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) ⇌ CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l)

An irreversible reaction proceeds effectively in one direction under the stated conditions. For example, thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate forms calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. Escaping carbon dioxide makes the reverse reaction difficult under ordinary conditions.

CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

Topic 2: Acids and bases

2.1 Brønsted–Lowry theory

A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton (H⁺), while a Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton. The same substance can behave as an acid in one reaction and a base in another, depending on its reaction partner.

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻
HCl = proton donor (acid); H₂O = proton acceptor (base)

In water, a free proton is associated with water molecules to form the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺. This is why aqueous acids are more accurately represented as producing hydronium ions.

2.2 Conjugate acids and bases

A conjugate acid is formed when a base gains one proton. A conjugate base is formed when an acid loses one proton. Members of a conjugate acid–base pair differ by exactly one H⁺.

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
Pairs: NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻

In the equation above, NH₃ accepts a proton and becomes NH₄⁺. Water donates a proton and becomes OH⁻. Identify conjugate pairs by comparing each reactant with its corresponding product.

2.3 Strong and weak acids and bases

Strength describes the extent of ionisation in water, not the amount of acid added. Strong acids ionise almost completely; weak acids ionise only partially and establish an equilibrium. Strong bases dissociate or ionise extensively to produce hydroxide ions; ammonia is a common weak base.

TypeExamplesBehaviour in water
Strong acidsHCl, HNO₃; first ionisation of H₂SO₄Extensive ionisation
Weak acidsCH₃COOH, H₂CO₃Partial ionisation
Strong basesNaOH, KOHDissociate extensively
Weak baseNH₃Partially reacts with water
CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

2.4 Electrical conductivity and strength

A solution conducts electricity when it contains mobile ions. At the same concentration, a strong acid or base generally gives a higher conductivity than a weak one because it produces more ions. A fair comparison keeps concentration, temperature, electrode material, exposed electrode area and electrode spacing constant. Do not confuse strength with concentration: a dilute strong acid can contain fewer acid particles per unit volume than a concentrated weak acid.

2.5 Oxides

  • Basic oxides are commonly metal oxides. They neutralise acids; some dissolve in water to form alkaline solutions, for example CaO forms Ca(OH)₂.
  • Acidic oxides are commonly non-metal oxides. Examples include CO₂ and SO₂; they react with bases and some form acids in water.
  • Amphoteric oxides react with both acids and bases, for example Al₂O₃ and ZnO.

2.6 Regulating pH and preparing salts

Antacids such as magnesium hydroxide neutralise excess stomach acid. Agricultural lime can reduce soil acidity. Select treatment carefully to avoid over-correction or environmental damage.

Choose a salt-preparation method according to the solubility of the salt and the reactants. Soluble salts can be prepared using an acid with a suitable metal, insoluble base or carbonate, followed by filtration of excess solid and crystallisation. When both reactants are soluble and the product is soluble, use titration to find the exact neutralising volumes, then repeat without indicator to obtain a clean salt solution. Insoluble salts are prepared by precipitation: mix two suitable soluble salt solutions, filter the precipitate, wash and dry it.

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl

2.7 Solubility rules and precipitation

All common sodium, potassium and ammonium salts and all nitrates are soluble. Most chlorides are soluble except silver chloride and lead(II) chloride (which is more soluble in hot water). Most sulphates are soluble except barium and lead(II) sulphates; calcium sulphate is sparingly soluble. Most carbonates are insoluble except sodium, potassium and ammonium carbonates.

Precipitation is useful in water treatment and industrial effluent treatment because selected dissolved ions can be converted into insoluble solids and removed by settling or filtration. The precipitate and resulting waste must be handled safely.

Topic 3: Oxidation and reduction

3.1 Meaning of oxidation and reduction

Oxidation and reduction always occur together in a redox reaction. Use the definitions that best fit the reaction: oxidation is loss of electrons, gain of oxygen, loss of hydrogen or increase in oxidation number. Reduction is gain of electrons, loss of oxygen, gain of hydrogen or decrease in oxidation number.

Memory aid: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation)
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (reduction)

3.2 Oxidation numbers

An oxidation number is a bookkeeping value used to track electron transfer. Apply these rules: an uncombined element has oxidation number 0; a monatomic ion has a value equal to its charge; oxygen is usually −2; hydrogen is usually +1; the sum in a neutral compound is 0; and the sum in a polyatomic ion equals the ion charge. Peroxides and some compounds have exceptions.

Worked example: sulphate ion

Let sulphur in SO₄²⁻ be x. Then x + 4(−2) = −2, so x − 8 = −2 and x = +6.

Worked example: permanganate ion

For MnO₄⁻, let Mn = x. Then x + 4(−2) = −1, so x = +7. If it forms Mn²⁺, its oxidation number falls from +7 to +2; manganese is reduced.

3.3 Half-equations and net ionic equations

A half-equation shows either oxidation or reduction separately, including electrons. In a balanced redox process, electrons lost must equal electrons gained. Multiply one or both half-equations as needed, add them and cancel electrons.

Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Overall: Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu

Spectator ions are ions that remain unchanged in solution. Remove them from the complete ionic equation to obtain the net ionic equation. Atoms and total charge must balance on both sides.

3.4 Oxidising and reducing agents

An oxidising agent accepts electrons and is itself reduced. A reducing agent donates electrons and is itself oxidised. For Cu + 2Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Ag, copper is the reducing agent and silver ions are the oxidising agent.

3.5 Reactivity series and displacement

A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from a solution of its ions. In Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu, iron loses electrons and copper(II) ions gain them. The blue solution becomes paler/greenish as Cu²⁺ is replaced by Fe²⁺, while copper coats the iron.

The common activity order from more to less reactive is K, Na, Li, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au (the exact sequence depends on the series being used). A metal above another can generally displace it from its salt solution. Greater separation often gives a more vigorous reaction, though surface conditions and concentration also matter.

3.6 Voltaic cells and electrode potentials

A voltaic (galvanic) cell converts energy from a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. It contains two half-cells connected by an external wire and a salt bridge. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Electrons travel through the external circuit from anode to cathode; ions move through the salt bridge to maintain electrical neutrality.

Schematic zinc–copper cell: zinc is the anode; copper is the cathode.

Cell notation for this cell is Zn(s)|Zn²⁺(aq)||Cu²⁺(aq)|Cu(s). The single vertical line marks a phase boundary and the double line represents the salt bridge. Standard cell potential is calculated from standard reduction potentials:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

For Zn/Cu: E°cell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V. A positive value indicates the reaction as written is spontaneous under standard conditions.

3.7 Corrosion and rusting

Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal through chemical or electrochemical reactions with its environment. Rusting is the corrosion of iron in the presence of both oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide. Salt can speed up rusting by increasing conductivity of the water layer.

Rusting can be reduced by painting, oiling or greasing, plastic coating, galvanising with zinc, alloying (for example stainless steel) and electroplating. Zinc can protect iron sacrificially because zinc oxidises more readily than iron.

Topic 4: Electrolysis

4.1 What electrolysis means

Electrolysis uses direct electrical energy to decompose or drive a chemical change in an electrolyte. An electrolyte conducts when molten or dissolved because it contains mobile ions. Electrodes provide surfaces where oxidation and reduction occur.

  • Cathode: negative electrode in an electrolytic cell; cations move towards it and gain electrons (reduction).
  • Anode: positive electrode in an electrolytic cell; anions move towards it and lose electrons (oxidation).
  • Inert electrodes: do not normally react, for example graphite or platinum.

4.2 Electrolysis of molten ionic compounds

Only ions from the molten compound are present to be discharged. Positive ions move to the cathode and are reduced; negative ions move to the anode and are oxidised. For molten lead(II) bromide:

Cathode: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb(l)
Anode: 2Br⁻ → Br₂(g) + 2e⁻
Overall: PbBr₂(l) → Pb(l) + Br₂(g)

4.3 Electrolysis of aqueous solutions

Water also contributes H⁺ and OH⁻ ions, so the products may differ from electrolysis of the molten salt. At the cathode, hydrogen is commonly produced when the metal is more reactive than hydrogen; less reactive metal ions may be reduced to the metal. At the anode, concentrated halide solutions commonly produce the halogen; in dilute halide solutions or solutions without halide ions, oxygen is commonly produced from hydroxide/water.

These are useful school-level prediction rules, but actual products can depend on concentration, electrode material and overpotential. Always state the conditions given in the question.

4.4 Electrolysis of brine and dilute sodium chloride

Concentrated sodium chloride solution (brine) contains Na⁺, Cl⁻ and ions from water. Hydrogen forms at the cathode and chlorine at the anode; sodium hydroxide remains in solution.

Cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻
Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ + Cl₂

In dilute sodium chloride solution, oxygen is usually produced at the anode instead of chlorine. Hydrogen is produced at the cathode. The water is effectively decomposed, leaving the sodium ions in solution.

4.5 Effect of electrode material

During electrolysis of copper(II) sulphate with inert electrodes, Cu²⁺ ions are reduced to copper at the cathode, while oxygen is formed at the anode. The blue colour fades as Cu²⁺ is removed. With copper electrodes, copper dissolves from the anode to form Cu²⁺ and an equal amount is deposited at the cathode; the solution's blue colour stays nearly constant.

Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Copper anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻

4.6 Writing electrode half-equations

  1. List the ions present, including ions from water if the electrolyte is aqueous.
  2. Predict the product at each electrode using the conditions.
  3. Write the correct elemental formula (for example Cl₂, not Cl).
  4. Balance atoms and then charge using electrons.
  5. Check that electrons lost and gained match in the overall reaction.

4.7 Uses of electrolysis

Electrolysis is used for electroplating, purification of metals and extraction of very reactive metals from molten compounds. In copper purification, impure copper is the anode, a thin sheet of pure copper is the cathode and acidified copper(II) sulphate is the electrolyte. Copper dissolves from the anode and deposits on the cathode; many insoluble impurities collect as anode sludge.

For electroplating, the object to be coated is the cathode, the coating metal is often the anode and the electrolyte contains ions of the coating metal. Clean the object first. Electroplating improves appearance, can reduce corrosion and can provide a useful surface finish.

Topic 5: Isomerism

5.1 Isomers and isomerism

Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements. Different structures can give different physical and chemical properties. Do not confuse structural isomers with conformations: conformations arise by rotation around single bonds without changing which atoms are connected.

Two different carbon skeletons with the same molecular formula C₅H₁₂.

5.2 Conformations

Rotation around a carbon–carbon single bond changes the spatial arrangement of atoms. These arrangements are conformations of the same molecule, not different structural isomers, because the atom-to-atom connectivity remains unchanged.

5.3 Structural isomerism

  • Chain isomerism: carbon atoms are connected in different arrangements, such as straight and branched chains.
  • Position isomerism: the same functional group or multiple bond occurs at different positions along the carbon chain.

5.4 Naming and drawing isomers

  1. Determine the molecular formula and draw possible carbon skeletons.
  2. Choose the longest suitable parent chain that contains the principal functional group or multiple bond.
  3. Number from the end that gives the principal functional group or multiple bond the lowest possible number.
  4. Identify branches and their positions; use di-, tri- and similar prefixes for repeated identical branches.
  5. Write the full systematic name and check that each structure has the required molecular formula and valency.

Topic 6: Polymerisation

6.1 Polymers and monomers

A polymer is a very large molecule made from many repeating smaller units. The small molecules that join to form a polymer are called monomers. Natural polymers include proteins, starch, cellulose, natural rubber and DNA. Synthetic polymers include polyethene, PVC, nylon, polyester and PTFE.

6.2 Addition polymerisation

Addition polymerisation joins many unsaturated monomers, commonly alkenes. The carbon–carbon double bond opens and forms new single bonds that connect monomer units. No small molecule is eliminated.

n CH₂=CH₂ → [–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ
Ethene → poly(ethene)
Simplified addition-polymerisation representation.

Chloroethene forms poly(chloroethene), commonly called PVC. The chlorine substituent remains attached to the polymer backbone.

n CH₂=CHCl → [–CH₂–CHCl–]ₙ

6.3 Condensation polymerisation

Condensation polymerisation joins monomers that have suitable functional groups and eliminates a small molecule, often water or hydrogen chloride, during each linking step. Nylon and polyesters are examples. The polymer chain forms through repeated condensation reactions between functional groups.

6.4 Properties and uses of synthetic polymers

PolymerMonomer(s)Typical uses
Poly(ethene)EtheneBags, bottles, pipes, packaging and wire insulation.
PVCChloroethenePipes, cable insulation, flooring and artificial leather.
PolystyreneStyreneProtective packaging, insulation and moulded items.
PTFE (Teflon)TetrafluoroetheneNon-stick coatings and chemically resistant components.
PerspexMethyl methacrylateTransparent sheets, lenses and glazing substitutes.

Many synthetic polymers are light, durable, corrosion-resistant and good electrical or thermal insulators. They can be moulded into useful shapes. However, many persist in the environment, may fragment into microplastics and can release harmful products if burned improperly. Recycling and responsible disposal are important.

6.5 Thermoplastics and thermosetting plastics

Thermoplastics soften when heated and can be reshaped repeatedly because their chains are not joined by a dense network of permanent cross-links. Examples include polyethene, PVC and polystyrene. Thermosetting plastics form a strongly cross-linked network; once set, they do not melt and flow again. They tend to be rigid and may decompose when strongly heated. Examples include Bakelite and melamine-formaldehyde resin.

Topic 7: Water

7.1 Natural sources and the water cycle

Water sources include underground water (wells, boreholes and springs), surface water (rivers, lakes, dams, streams and oceans) and atmospheric water (rain). Water continually moves between the land, water bodies and atmosphere through the hydrological cycle.

Simplified water cycle: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, runoff and collection.

Sunlight drives evaporation from water surfaces and transpiration from plants. Water vapour rises, cools and condenses into cloud droplets. When droplets grow large enough, they fall as precipitation. Water returns to rivers, lakes, oceans and underground stores through runoff and infiltration.

7.2 Physical properties and importance

Pure water is colourless, odourless and tasteless. At standard atmospheric pressure it freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. Its density is approximately 1.0 g cm⁻³ near 4 °C. Water is important as a solvent, coolant, reactant, transport medium, for domestic use, agriculture and hydroelectric power generation.

7.3 Hard and soft water

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and/or magnesium ions and does not lather readily with soap. Soft water contains little of these ions and lathers easily. Hardness can cause soap scum and scale in kettles, boilers and pipes. Calcium and magnesium in drinking water can also contribute useful dietary minerals.

TypeMain causeRemoval
Temporary hardnessCalcium and magnesium hydrogencarbonatesBoiling or lime treatment.
Permanent hardnessCalcium and magnesium sulphates or chloridesWashing soda, ion exchange or distillation.

7.4 Removing hardness

Boiling decomposes hydrogencarbonates and forms insoluble deposits that can be removed. Sodium carbonate (washing soda) precipitates calcium and magnesium ions as insoluble carbonates. Ion-exchange resins replace Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ with Na⁺ ions. Distillation separates water from dissolved salts but requires considerable energy.

Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq) → CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)
Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s)

7.5 Water pollution

Water pollution occurs when harmful substances or organisms enter water and make it unsafe or damage ecosystems. Sources include untreated sewage, industrial effluents, agricultural fertilisers and pesticides, oil spills, plastics and poorly managed waste. Effects include water-borne disease, toxic contamination, eutrophication, reduced dissolved oxygen and disruption of food webs.

Control measures include treating sewage and industrial effluent before discharge, managing farm chemicals, protecting wells and catchments, preventing oil spills, reducing plastic waste and enforcing environmental standards. Household water should be treated using approved methods when its safety is uncertain.

Topic 8: Greenhouse gases and the ozone layer

8.1 Natural disasters and human influence

Natural hazards include droughts, storms, earthquakes, wildfires, floods and volcanic eruptions. Human activities can increase the severity of some impacts. Deforestation, poorly planned urban development, loss of wetlands and soil degradation can increase surface runoff, erosion and flood risk. These activities do not cause every natural hazard, but they can make consequences worse.

8.2 Greenhouse gases

Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some infrared radiation emitted by Earth's surface. The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm enough for life, but increasing concentrations from human activity strengthen the effect and contribute to global warming. Important greenhouse gases include carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), water vapour and industrial gases. Sulphur dioxide is an air pollutant but is not usually classified as a major long-lived greenhouse gas.

GasImportant sources
Carbon dioxideBurning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement production and respiration.
MethaneLivestock, rice cultivation, wetlands, landfill and fossil-fuel extraction.
Nitrous oxideAgricultural soils and fertilisers, some industrial processes and combustion.
Ozone near ground levelForms through sunlight-driven reactions involving nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds.

8.3 Global warming and its effects

Global warming is the long-term rise in Earth's average surface temperature, mainly driven by the increased greenhouse effect from human emissions. Effects include heat extremes, changing rainfall patterns, drought and flood risks, sea-level rise, stress on crops and ecosystems and risks to human health. Impacts vary by region.

8.4 Mitigation and air quality

Mitigation reduces the causes of climate change. Measures include using renewable energy, improving energy efficiency, protecting and restoring forests, reducing methane leaks, improving public transport and using cleaner industrial technologies. Catalytic converters reduce certain harmful vehicle exhaust pollutants, but they do not remove all greenhouse gases.

Air-quality standards set limits for pollutants such as particulate matter, carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide, ozone and lead. Concentration units include ppm (parts per million) and µg m⁻³ (micrograms per cubic metre). Compare a measured value with the correct pollutant, averaging period and standard; these details matter.

8.5 The ozone layer

The ozone layer is a region of the stratosphere, mainly about 15–35 km above Earth, with relatively high ozone concentration. It absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation, protecting living organisms.

8.6 Ozone depletion

Some ozone-depleting substances, including chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), can release chlorine radicals in the stratosphere under ultraviolet light. These radicals catalyse the breakdown of ozone and can be regenerated, allowing one radical to destroy many ozone molecules.

Cl· + O₃ → ClO· + O₂
ClO· + O → Cl· + O₂
Net: O₃ + O → 2O₂

Ozone depletion increases the amount of harmful UV-B reaching the surface. Possible effects include increased skin cancer and cataract risk, harm to crops and damage to phytoplankton and aquatic food webs. International controls on ozone-depleting substances have helped the ozone layer begin a gradual recovery.

Topic 9: Waste management

9.1 Meaning and classification of waste

Waste is unwanted material produced by human activities. It may be classified by physical form as solid, liquid, gas or sludge, or by its ability to decompose as biodegradable or non-biodegradable. Biodegradable materials can be broken down by microorganisms; many plastics and glass persist for long periods.

9.2 Sources of waste

  • Domestic: food scraps, packaging, plastics, sewage and household chemicals.
  • Industrial: process residues, used oils, metals, solvents and effluents.
  • Agricultural: crop residues, animal waste, pesticide containers and excess fertilisers.
  • Medical: sharps, contaminated dressings, expired medicines and clinical waste.
  • Laboratory: broken glass, unused reagents, contaminated materials and chemical residues.

9.3 Treatment and disposal methods

MethodDescription and considerations
IncinerationControlled high-temperature treatment in a suitable facility. Reduces volume; emissions and ash require control.
Sanitary landfillWaste is placed in engineered cells, compacted and covered; leachate and gas must be managed.
CompostingControlled aerobic decomposition of suitable organic waste to produce a soil conditioner.
RecyclingMaterials are collected, sorted and processed into feedstock for new products.
ReuseAn item is used again, preferably for its original or a safe alternative purpose.
Open dumping/burningUnsafe practices that can pollute air, soil and water and should be avoided.

9.4 Waste minimisation and recycling

The waste hierarchy prioritises preventing waste, reducing consumption, reusing products, recycling materials and recovering value before final disposal. Metal and plastic recycling commonly involves collection, sorting, cleaning, size reduction and reprocessing. Correct sorting is important because different polymer types and contaminated materials may not be compatible.

Recycling can conserve raw materials, reduce energy use and lower pollution. It does not remove the need to reduce unnecessary consumption. Reusable containers and durable products can prevent waste at source.

9.5 Safe waste handling

Separate ordinary recyclable waste from hazardous, medical, electronic and chemical waste. Never mix unknown chemicals or pour hazardous reagents into drains. Broken glass and sharps belong in designated puncture-resistant containers. Follow laboratory instructions, safety data and local authority requirements; hazardous waste should be handled by trained personnel and authorised services.
