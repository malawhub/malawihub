Topic 1: Experimental techniques

Experimental chemistry uses careful observation and controlled procedures to obtain evidence about substances and reactions. A good result depends on selecting suitable apparatus, changing one factor at a time, recording measurements with units and explaining observations using chemical ideas.

1.1 Laboratory wastes

Laboratory waste includes unused reagents, soluble inorganic salts, broken glass, sharp objects, oils, contaminated paper and gloves, plastics and poisonous gases. Waste must be separated into categories before disposal. The correct method depends on the chemical and the rules of the laboratory; never assume that a substance is safe to put into a drain or ordinary bin.

Harmless, water-soluble salts and some dilute acids, alkalis or detergents may be disposed of through a laboratory drain only where the institution specifically permits it and the approved procedure is followed. Toxic metal compounds, arsenic compounds, pesticides, oils, hydrocarbons and other hazardous substances must not be washed into drains. Ordinary non-recyclable waste goes into the correct waste bin. Broken glass and sharp metal objects go into a puncture-resistant sharps container. Some combustible chemical wastes require treatment by an authorised incineration facility; this is not a procedure for learners to perform. Keep each waste stream labelled and never mix incompatible chemicals.

Wear a laboratory coat, eye protection and suitable gloves. Tie back long hair, keep the bench clear and never taste chemicals. Waft vapour towards the nose only when instructed. A fume cupboard is used when harmful vapours, gases or fine particles may be released. If a chemical contacts the skin or eyes, rinse immediately with plenty of running water and alert the teacher or laboratory supervisor. For a fire, spill or exposure, follow the laboratory emergency procedure rather than attempting an unfamiliar clean-up.

1.2 Designing scientific investigations

An investigation begins with a clearly stated problem or question. A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation. Identify the independent variable (the factor deliberately changed), dependent variable (the factor measured) and controlled variables (conditions kept the same). Choose apparatus with suitable precision, repeat measurements where possible and record observations in a table before drawing a conclusion.

For example, to investigate whether moisture and air are both needed for iron to rust, prepare comparable clean iron nails under four conditions: both air and water present; air present but water excluded with a drying agent; water present but air excluded using boiled water covered by oil; and a dry control. Keep the nails, containers and observation period comparable. Rust forms where oxygen and water are available. The comparison supports the conclusion that both oxygen and moisture are required under these conditions.

1.3 Determining the purity of a substance

A pure substance has a characteristic melting point or boiling point at a stated pressure. A pure solid usually melts over a narrow temperature range, while impurities commonly lower and broaden its melting range. Dissolved non-volatile impurities usually raise the boiling point of a liquid. Boiling point also depends on external pressure, so comparisons must be made under comparable conditions.

To assess a solid, heat a small sample slowly in a suitable melting-point apparatus and record the temperature at which melting begins and ends. Compare the range with a reliable reference value. A pure solid normally melts over a narrow range; an impurity often lowers the melting temperature and makes the range wider. For a liquid, measure its boiling point at a known pressure and compare it with the accepted value. A dissolved non-volatile impurity generally raises the boiling point. These are useful tests, but thermometer calibration, heating rate, sample size and pressure can affect the result.

1.4 Paper chromatography

Paper chromatography separates components of a mixture because they distribute differently between a stationary phase (the paper, with a thin layer of water associated with it) and a moving solvent (the mobile phase). A component that is more soluble in the solvent and less strongly attracted to the paper travels farther.

Draw a pencil baseline near the bottom of the paper. Place a small concentrated spot of sample on the line and allow it to dry. Stand the paper in a suitable solvent with the baseline above the solvent level. Cover the container and allow the solvent to rise. Remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top, mark the solvent front immediately and dry the chromatogram. Separate spots indicate different components. Pencil is used because graphite does not normally dissolve in the solvent; ink could run and interfere.

Rf = distance travelled by centre of substance spot ÷ distance travelled by solvent front

Measure both distances from the baseline. An Rf value is between 0 and 1 for a normal ascending chromatogram. It is useful for comparison only when the solvent, paper and conditions are the same. A single spot suggests one detectable component under those conditions, but does not prove absolute purity.

Schematic chromatogram: separated spots travel different distances.

Topic 2: Nitrogen, sulphur and phosphorus

Nitrogen, sulphur and phosphorus are non-metals that form important compounds in the atmosphere, soils, living organisms and industry. Their chemical behaviour is linked to their electron arrangements and the oxidation states they can adopt.

2.1 Nitrogen and its compounds

Nitrogen exists naturally as a diatomic molecule, N₂. The two atoms are joined by a strong triple covalent bond, making nitrogen relatively unreactive at ordinary temperatures. Nitrogen makes up most of dry air and is essential in proteins and nucleic acids, but most plants cannot use atmospheric N₂ directly.

At high temperature and pressure in the presence of an iron catalyst, nitrogen reacts reversibly with hydrogen to form ammonia. This is the Haber process.

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)  ΔH < 0

The reaction is exothermic and produces fewer gas molecules on the product side. High pressure favours ammonia formation because it favours the side with fewer gas molecules. Lower temperature favours the exothermic forward reaction, but makes the reaction slower. Industry therefore uses a compromise temperature, elevated pressure and an iron catalyst. Unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled after ammonia is separated by cooling.

Ammonia is a colourless, pungent, alkaline gas that is very soluble in water. It reacts with water to form a weakly alkaline solution and reacts with acids to form ammonium salts.

NH₃(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

Ammonia is used to manufacture fertilisers such as ammonium salts and urea, and is also used in the production of nitric acid. In the Ostwald process, ammonia is oxidised over a platinum-rhodium catalyst to nitrogen monoxide, which is further oxidised to nitrogen dioxide and absorbed in water to form nitric acid.

4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O
2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂
3NO₂ + H₂O → 2HNO₃ + NO

Nitric acid is a strong acid and an oxidising agent. It reacts with bases and carbonates to form nitrate salts. Concentrated nitric acid can passivate some metals by forming a protective surface layer. Nitrates are important fertilisers, but excessive nitrate runoff can contribute to eutrophication.

2.2 Sulphur and its compounds

Sulphur is a yellow non-metal found in elemental deposits and in sulphide and sulphate minerals. It commonly forms covalent molecules and can show oxidation states −2, +4 and +6. Burning sulphur in oxygen produces sulphur dioxide, a colourless, acidic, choking gas.

S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g)

Sulphur dioxide dissolves in water to form an acidic solution and acts as a reducing agent in many reactions. It can be oxidised to sulphur trioxide, which reacts with water to form sulphuric acid. The Contact process manufactures sulphuric acid: sulphur dioxide is purified, oxidised using vanadium(V) oxide catalyst at a suitable temperature, and sulphur trioxide is absorbed in concentrated sulphuric acid to form oleum. Oleum is then carefully diluted.

2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)  ΔH < 0
SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇
H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄

Directly adding water to concentrated sulphuric acid is dangerous because the process releases much heat and can cause acid to spray. When dilution is required, add acid slowly to water while stirring and cooling. Concentrated sulphuric acid is corrosive, dehydrating and can act as an oxidising agent.

Hydrogen sulphide, H₂S, is a poisonous gas with a characteristic rotten-egg odour. It forms sulphide precipitates with many metal ions. Sulphur dioxide and hydrogen sulphide must never be identified by deliberately smelling them.

2.3 Phosphorus and its compounds

Phosphorus occurs mainly in phosphate minerals because it is reactive. White phosphorus is highly reactive and must be stored under water; red phosphorus is less reactive. Phosphorus is essential in DNA, ATP, phospholipids and bones. Plants obtain it mainly as phosphate ions from soil.

When phosphorus burns in excess oxygen it forms phosphorus(V) oxide, commonly represented by P₄O₁₀. This acidic oxide reacts with water to form phosphoric acid.

P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀
P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄

Phosphoric acid is a triprotic acid: one molecule can donate three protons in successive stages. Phosphate fertilisers supply phosphorus needed for root growth, energy transfer and seed formation. Excess phosphate entering lakes and rivers encourages rapid algal growth; decomposition of the algae consumes dissolved oxygen and can harm aquatic organisms.

Topic 3: Chemical bonding and properties of matter

The properties of a substance depend on the particles present and the forces or bonds holding them together. A substance may consist of ions, molecules or a giant covalent network. Distinguishing bonds within particles from forces between particles helps explain melting points, boiling points, hardness and electrical conductivity.

3.1 Properties of ionic and covalent compounds

An ionic compound contains positive and negative ions arranged in a giant lattice. Strong electrostatic attractions act in all directions. Much energy is needed to separate the ions, so ionic solids generally have high melting and boiling points. They are usually brittle: when layers shift, like charges can become adjacent and repel, causing the crystal to fracture.

In the solid state, ions are fixed in position and cannot carry charge through the material. When molten or dissolved in water, ions can move and conduct electricity. Solubility depends on the balance between lattice attractions and the interactions formed with the solvent; not every ionic compound dissolves in water.

Covalent bonding occurs when atoms share pairs of electrons. Simple molecular substances contain separate molecules. The covalent bonds within each molecule are strong, but attractions between molecules are often much weaker. Consequently many simple molecular substances have relatively low melting and boiling points and do not conduct electricity.

Giant covalent structures contain atoms joined by an extended network of covalent bonds. Diamond has each carbon atom bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional arrangement, making it extremely hard and giving it a high melting point. Graphite has layers of carbon atoms, each bonded to three neighbours; the remaining electron is delocalised. The layers slide over one another, so graphite is soft and useful as a lubricant, while its delocalised electrons allow electrical conduction along the layers.

3.2 Covalent bonds and dative bonds

A covalent bond is formed by sharing an electron pair. In a coordinate (dative) covalent bond, both electrons in the shared pair are supplied by the same atom. Once formed, a coordinate bond behaves like an ordinary covalent bond; the term describes how the pair was donated during bond formation.

NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺
H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺

In ammonium formation, nitrogen in ammonia donates its lone pair to a proton. In hydronium formation, oxygen in water donates a lone pair. The arrow in a dot-and-cross or structural diagram points from the donor atom towards the electron-pair acceptor.

3.3 Intermolecular forces

Intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules. London dispersion forces arise from temporary fluctuations in electron distribution and occur in all atoms and molecules. They become stronger as particles have more electrons and are more polarizable. Permanent dipole–dipole attractions occur between polar molecules. Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong type of dipole attraction when hydrogen bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine is attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F in a neighbouring molecule.

Stronger intermolecular attractions generally require more energy to overcome, producing higher boiling points and often greater viscosity. Water has an unusually high boiling point for its small molecular size because its molecules form an extensive hydrogen-bond network. Hydrogen bonding also helps explain why ice is less dense than liquid water.

3.4 Allotropy

Allotropy is the existence of an element in two or more different structural forms in the same physical state. The different arrangements produce different properties. Carbon has diamond, graphite and other forms such as fullerenes. Oxygen has O₂ and ozone, O₃. Phosphorus has white and red forms. Sulphur commonly occurs in different crystalline forms. Allotropes are forms of the same element, not compounds of different elements.

Topic 4: Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry is the quantitative study of the amounts of reactants and products in chemical reactions. Balanced equations provide mole ratios, while measured masses, volumes and concentrations allow the quantities to be calculated.

4.1 Chemical formulae and equations

A chemical formula shows the elements present and the number or simplest ratio of their atoms or ions. For an ionic compound, the total positive and negative charges must balance. For example, magnesium forms Mg²⁺ and chloride forms Cl⁻, so the formula is MgCl₂. A balanced chemical equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides and the same overall charge.

4.2 Relative formula mass

Relative formula mass, Mᵣ, is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms shown in a formula. It has no unit. The molar mass has the same numerical value but is expressed in g mol⁻¹.

Mᵣ(MgCl₂) = 24.3 + (2 × 35.5) = 95.3
n = m ÷ M  therefore m = nM

4.3 The mole and reacting quantities

One mole contains the Avogadro constant, approximately 6.02 × 10²³ specified particles. The particles may be atoms, molecules, ions or formula units. To calculate a reacting mass, first balance the equation, convert the known mass to moles, use the equation's mole ratio, then convert the required moles into mass.

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
n(Mg) = mass of Mg ÷ 24.3
n(MgO) = n(Mg) × 2/2
mass of MgO = n(MgO) × 40.3 g

4.4 Molar gas volume

At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules. The molar gas volume depends on the conditions used. At room temperature and pressure, many school calculations use approximately 24 dm³ mol⁻¹; at standard temperature and pressure a different value is used. Always use the conditions specified in the question.

n(gas) = volume in dm³ ÷ molar gas volume
1 dm³ = 1000 cm³

4.5 Water of crystallisation

Some crystals contain a fixed number of water molecules within their crystal structure. These are waters of crystallisation. Heating a hydrated salt may remove this water and leave an anhydrous salt. The mass lost is used to calculate the number of water molecules in the formula.

For a hydrate with formula salt·xH₂O, calculate the moles of anhydrous salt from its mass and molar mass. Calculate the moles of water from the mass lost divided by 18.0. Divide both mole values by the smaller to obtain the simplest whole-number ratio and hence x. Heat gently at first to avoid spitting, cool before weighing and repeat heating and weighing until a constant mass is obtained.

4.6 Formulae from composition

To determine an empirical formula from percentage composition, assume a 100 g sample. Convert each element's mass to moles by dividing by its relative atomic mass. Divide all mole values by the smallest and convert the ratio to the simplest whole numbers. The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

Molecular formula multiplier = molecular mass ÷ empirical formula mass

4.7 Concentration of solutions

Concentration in mol dm⁻³ is the amount of solute, in moles, dissolved per cubic decimetre of solution. Convert cm³ to dm³ before using the equation. Mass concentration may be expressed in g dm⁻³.

c = n ÷ V  n = cV
V in dm³; c in mol dm⁻³

4.8 Standard solutions and titration

A standard solution has an accurately known concentration. It can be prepared by weighing a suitable pure, stable solid, dissolving it in distilled water and making the solution up to a fixed volume in a volumetric flask. Rinse the weighing vessel and funnel into the flask to transfer all the solute. Add water to the calibration mark at eye level, stopper and invert several times to mix.

In an acid–base titration, a measured volume of one solution is transferred with a pipette into a conical flask and a few drops of a suitable indicator are added. The other solution is delivered from a burette until the indicator shows a permanent end-point colour change. Rinse the burette with the solution it will contain and rinse the pipette with the solution it will transfer. The conical flask is rinsed with distilled water: this removes contamination but does not change the number of moles delivered by the pipette. A rough titre is followed by accurate titres until concordant values are obtained. The end point is the observed indicator change; the equivalence point is the exact stoichiometric completion. They should be close when a suitable indicator is chosen.

Use the balanced equation to relate moles at the equivalence point. For a reaction aA + bB → products, n(A)/a = n(B)/b. Calculate moles using n = cV, with V in dm³, and then find the unknown concentration.

4.9 Percentage yield and limiting reagent

Theoretical yield is the maximum product amount predicted from the limiting reactant by the balanced equation. Actual yield is the amount collected in the experiment. A limiting reagent is the reactant that is used up first and therefore limits the amount of product.

Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100%

To identify the limiting reagent, calculate the available moles of each reactant and divide each by its coefficient in the balanced equation. The smallest result identifies the limiting reactant. A yield below 100% may result from incomplete reaction, side reactions, transfer losses or product remaining dissolved. An apparent yield above 100% usually indicates that the product is wet or contaminated, or that measurements were inaccurate.

Worked calculations

Example 1: calculating moles from mass

Calculate the amount of sodium chloride in 5.85 g. Given Mᵣ(NaCl) = 58.5. Use n = m ÷ M = 5.85 ÷ 58.5 = 0.100 mol.

Example 2: empirical formula from composition

A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Assume 100 g: C = 40.0 g, H = 6.7 g and O = 53.3 g. Moles: C = 40.0 ÷ 12 = 3.33; H = 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7; O = 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33. Divide by 3.33 to obtain 1 : 2 : 1. The empirical formula is CH₂O.

Example 3: concentration from a titration

25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid exactly neutralises 20.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Moles of HCl = cV = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol. The ratio is 1 : 1, so NaOH also contains 0.00250 mol. Convert 20.0 cm³ to 0.0200 dm³. Concentration of NaOH = 0.00250 ÷ 0.0200 = 0.125 mol dm⁻³.

Example 4: water of crystallisation

A 2.50 g sample of hydrated copper(II) sulphate leaves 1.60 g of anhydrous CuSO₄ after heating to constant mass. Water lost = 2.50 − 1.60 = 0.90 g. Moles of water = 0.90 ÷ 18.0 = 0.0500 mol. Moles of CuSO₄ = 1.60 ÷ 159.6 = 0.0100 mol. Divide by 0.0100: CuSO₄ : H₂O = 1 : 5. The formula is CuSO₄·5H₂O.

Example 5: limiting reagent and percentage yield

For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, suppose 6.0 mol H₂ reacts with 2.0 mol O₂. Divide each amount by its equation coefficient: H₂ gives 6.0 ÷ 2 = 3.0; O₂ gives 2.0 ÷ 1 = 2.0. Oxygen is limiting because it gives the smaller value. It forms 2 × 2.0 = 4.0 mol H₂O. If the theoretical product mass is 72.0 g but 57.6 g is collected, percentage yield = (57.6 ÷ 72.0) × 100 = 80.0%.

Topic 5: Chemical reactions

Chemical reactions involve the breaking of bonds in reactants and the formation of new bonds in products. Energy is transferred between the reacting system and its surroundings. The energy change can be investigated by measuring temperature changes or by considering bond energies.

5.1 Heats of reaction

The enthalpy change of a reaction, ΔH, is the heat energy change at constant pressure. An exothermic reaction releases heat to the surroundings and has a negative ΔH. An endothermic reaction absorbs heat and has a positive ΔH. Temperature rise in the surroundings usually indicates an exothermic process; a temperature fall usually indicates an endothermic process.

5.2 Exothermic and endothermic reactions

Combustion, neutralisation of a strong acid by a strong alkali and many oxidation reactions are exothermic. Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate and some dissolving processes are endothermic. Whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic depends on the overall energy balance, not simply on whether heat is supplied to start it.

5.3 Measuring temperature changes

A simple calorimetry experiment can estimate the heat released when an acid reacts with an alkali. Measure known volumes and concentrations, record their initial temperatures, mix them in an insulated cup, stir and record the highest temperature reached. Repeat with consistent volumes and conditions. Use a lid and insulation to reduce heat loss. The temperature change is the final temperature minus the initial temperature.

q = mcΔT
q = heat transferred (J); m = mass of solution (g); c ≈ 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹; ΔT = temperature change (°C)

For a dilute aqueous solution, its density is often approximated as 1.00 g cm⁻³ so its volume in cm³ is approximately its mass in grams. The molar enthalpy change is obtained by dividing the heat change by the number of moles that reacted, with the sign assigned for the reaction. A temperature rise means the reaction released heat, so ΔH is negative. Heat lost to the surroundings makes the measured magnitude smaller than the true value.

5.4 Energy-level diagrams

An energy-level diagram shows the relative energy of reactants and products. In an exothermic profile, products are at a lower energy than reactants and the vertical difference represents a negative enthalpy change. In an endothermic profile, products are higher and ΔH is positive. Both profiles have an activation-energy barrier: the minimum energy needed for successful reaction. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy but does not change the enthalpy difference between reactants and products.

Schematic exothermic profile: products are at lower energy than reactants.
Schematic endothermic profile: products are at higher energy than reactants, so ΔH is positive.

5.5 Bond breaking and bond formation

Breaking a chemical bond requires energy; forming a bond releases energy. The overall enthalpy change can be estimated using average bond enthalpies.

ΔH ≈ Σ(bond energies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond energies of bonds formed)

If more energy is released in forming product bonds than is absorbed in breaking reactant bonds, the reaction is exothermic. Bond enthalpies are average values, so calculations give estimates rather than exact enthalpy changes for every molecule.

Topic 6: Alkanols

Alkanols are organic compounds containing a hydroxyl functional group, –OH, attached to a saturated carbon atom. The hydroxyl group strongly influences their physical and chemical properties.

6.1 Functional group of alkanols

The functional group is the hydroxyl group, –OH. It is attached to a saturated carbon atom. The oxygen atom is more electronegative than carbon and hydrogen, so the O–H and C–O bonds are polar. The oxygen atom can form hydrogen bonds with water and with other alkanol molecules.

General functional group: R–OH

6.2 General formula and nomenclature of alkanols

For saturated, open-chain monohydric alkanols, the general formula is CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH, also written CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O. Select the longest carbon chain containing the carbon attached to –OH. Number the chain from the end nearest the –OH group and replace the final -e of the alkane name with -ol. Show the position of –OH when needed.

Methanol: CH₃OH
Ethanol: CH₃CH₂OH
Propan-1-ol: CH₃CH₂CH₂OH
Propan-2-ol: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃

6.3 Classification of alkanols

Alkanols are classified according to the number of carbon atoms attached to the carbon that carries the –OH group. In a primary alkanol that carbon is attached to one other carbon; in a secondary alkanol it is attached to two; in a tertiary alkanol it is attached to three. Methanol is considered separately because its –OH-bearing carbon is attached to no other carbon. This classification is important because the three groups behave differently during oxidation.

ClassGeneral arrangementExample
PrimaryR–CH₂OHEthanol, CH₃CH₂OH
SecondaryR–CH(OH)–R′Propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃
TertiaryR–C(OH)(R′)–R″2-methylpropan-2-ol

6.4 Preparation of ethanol

Ethanol can be prepared by fermentation of glucose using yeast. The solution is kept warm and oxygen is excluded. Enzymes in yeast convert glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Fermentation stops when the ethanol concentration becomes high enough to inhibit the yeast. The dilute ethanol is then separated from much of the water by distillation.

C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2CO₂(g)

Ethanol can also be manufactured by hydration of ethene. Ethene reacts with steam in the presence of an acid catalyst under controlled temperature and pressure. This is an addition reaction.

C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ C₂H₅OH(g)

6.5 Properties of alkanols

Lower alkanols are usually colourless, volatile liquids. Their –OH groups form hydrogen bonds, giving them higher boiling points than similar-sized hydrocarbons. The smaller alkanols mix well with water. As the hydrocarbon chain becomes longer, the non-polar part becomes more important and water solubility decreases. Alkanols are flammable and must be kept away from naked flames.

Complete combustion in excess oxygen produces carbon dioxide and water. In limited oxygen, incomplete combustion may produce carbon monoxide or carbon (soot).

C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Alkanols react with active metals such as sodium to form an alkoxide and hydrogen gas. The reaction shows that the hydrogen atom in the hydroxyl group can be replaced.

2C₂H₅OH + 2Na → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂

6.6 Oxidation of ethanol

Ethanol is a primary alkanol. Controlled oxidation produces ethanal, while further oxidation produces ethanoic acid. To obtain ethanal, use a limited amount of oxidising agent and distil the ethanal off as it forms. To obtain ethanoic acid, use excess oxidising agent and heat under reflux so that the ethanal remains in contact with the oxidising agent.

CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O
CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH

Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) changes from orange to green during oxidation. More generally, primary alkanols oxidise to alkanals and then alkanoic acids; secondary alkanols oxidise to alkanones. Tertiary alkanols resist oxidation under ordinary laboratory conditions because the carbon carrying –OH has no hydrogen atom.

Topic 7: Alkanals and alkanones

Alkanals and alkanones are carbonyl compounds. Both contain a carbonyl group, C=O, but the position of this group differs. In an alkanal it is at the end of the carbon chain; in an alkanone it is within the chain.

7.1 Alkanals

An alkanal has the functional group –CHO and the general formula CₙH₂ₙO for saturated open-chain members. The carbonyl carbon is attached to at least one hydrogen. Names end in -al: methanal, ethanal and propanal. The carbonyl carbon is carbon 1, so its position does not need a number in a simple alkanal.

Methanal: HCHO
Ethanal: CH₃CHO
Propanal: CH₃CH₂CHO

7.2 Alkanones

An alkanone has a carbonyl group between two carbon atoms. Saturated open-chain alkanones have the general formula CₙH₂ₙO. Their names end in -one and the position of the carbonyl group is shown by a number, for example propanone (CH₃COCH₃) and butan-2-one (CH₃COCH₂CH₃).

7.3 Structural differences and properties

Alkanals and alkanones are polar because the C=O bond is polar. They can accept hydrogen bonds from water but cannot hydrogen-bond to each other as donors because they do not contain an O–H bond. The smaller members are therefore appreciably soluble in water; solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain grows. Their boiling points are usually higher than those of comparable non-polar hydrocarbons but lower than those of alcohols of similar size.

7.4 Sources and preparation

Alkanals can be prepared by controlled oxidation of primary alkanols. Alkanones can be prepared by oxidation of secondary alkanols. In the laboratory, the product and reaction conditions are selected to prevent further oxidation or unwanted decomposition. Some carbonyl compounds also occur naturally in fragrances, flavourings and biological pathways.

7.5 Tests for alkanals and alkanones

Alkanals are readily oxidised to alkanoic acids, while alkanones are not easily oxidised by mild oxidising agents. Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror with many alkanals; Fehling's or Benedict's solution may give a brick-red precipitate with suitable alkanals. Most simple alkanones give no such positive result. These tests should be performed with fresh reagents and the specified laboratory safety precautions.

Topic 8: Alkanoic acids

Alkanoic acids are carboxylic acids containing the carboxyl functional group, –COOH. The group contains a carbonyl bond (C=O) and a hydroxyl bond (O–H) on the same carbon atom.

8.1 General formula and IUPAC nomenclature of alkanoic acids

Saturated open-chain monocarboxylic acids have the general formula CₙH₂ₙO₂. Choose the longest chain containing the carboxyl carbon and replace the ending of the corresponding alkane name with -oic acid. The carboxyl carbon is always carbon 1.

Methanoic acid: HCOOH
Ethanoic acid: CH₃COOH
Propanoic acid: CH₃CH₂COOH
Butanoic acid: CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH

8.2 Natural sources

Ethanoic acid is present in vinegar, produced when ethanol is oxidised by bacteria in the presence of oxygen. Methanoic acid occurs in some insect stings and plants. Longer-chain carboxylic acids occur in fats and oils as part of triglyceride molecules. Natural sources may contain mixtures, so a natural material should not be treated as a pure acid without purification.

8.3 Physical and chemical properties

Lower alkanoic acids are colourless liquids with sharp smells. They form hydrogen-bonded pairs (dimers) in some conditions and have higher boiling points than many compounds of similar molar mass that cannot hydrogen-bond. The smaller acids dissolve readily in water; solubility decreases as the hydrocarbon chain becomes longer.

Alkanoic acids are weak acids: they partially ionise in water. They turn blue litmus red and react with reactive metals, bases and carbonates. The products are salts, with hydrogen gas from metals, water from bases and carbon dioxide from carbonates.

2CH₃COOH + Mg → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂
CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂

Ethanoic acid reacts with an alkanol in the presence of an acid catalyst to form an alkanoate (ester) and water. This is esterification. The reaction is reversible.

CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O

Topic 9: Alkanoates

Alkanoates are esters. They are formed when an alkanoic acid reacts with an alkanol. Many small esters have characteristic fruity smells and are used in flavourings, fragrances and as solvents.

9.0 Introduction

An ester is an organic compound containing the –COO– linkage. Esters occur naturally in fruits, flowers, fats and oils. The ester linkage is formed when an acid and an alcohol react with the elimination of water.

9.1 Functional group

The functional group of an alkanoate is the ester linkage, –COO–. A general structural formula is R–COO–R′. The carbonyl-containing part comes from the alkanoic acid, while the –O–R′ part comes from the alkanol.

R–C(=O)–O–R′

9.2 Nomenclature of alkanoates

Name the alkyl group attached to the oxygen first; this group comes from the alkanol. Name the acid-derived part second, changing the ending of the alkanoic acid name from -oic acid to -oate. For example, ethanol and ethanoic acid form ethyl ethanoate.

CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ = ethyl ethanoate
HCOOCH₃ = methyl methanoate

9.3 Structure of alkanoates

In an ester, the carbonyl carbon is bonded to an oxygen atom that is in turn bonded to another carbon group. The two carbon-containing groups may be different. In the esterification reaction, the acid supplies the R–COO– part and the alkanol supplies the R′ group attached to oxygen.

RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O

A few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid may be used as an acid catalyst. The mixture is warmed gently, commonly in a water bath, because organic liquids and their vapours may be flammable. The reaction is reversible. Using an excess of one reactant or removing water can favour ester formation. A catalyst increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached but does not change the equilibrium position.

9.4 Sources of alkanoates

Esters occur in many fruits and flowers and contribute to their characteristic aromas. Fats and oils are naturally occurring triesters formed from glycerol and long-chain carboxylic acids. Esters can also be prepared in the laboratory by reacting an alkanoic acid with an alkanol.

9.5 Physical properties of esters

Many lower-molecular-mass esters are volatile liquids with characteristic fruity smells. Their boiling points are generally lower than those of comparable alcohols and carboxylic acids because ester molecules cannot donate hydrogen bonds to one another. Small esters may dissolve to some extent in water, but solubility usually decreases as the hydrocarbon portions become larger. Many esters dissolve organic substances and are used as solvents.

9.6 Saponification

Saponification is the alkaline hydrolysis of an ester. When an ester reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide, an alkanol and a carboxylate salt are formed. For example:

CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH

Fats and oils are triglycerides: triesters formed from glycerol and long-chain fatty acids. Heating them with sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide breaks the ester bonds, producing glycerol and salts of the fatty acids. These salts are soaps. A soap molecule has a water-attracting ionic end and a water-repelling hydrocarbon tail, allowing it to help disperse oily dirt in water.

Acid hydrolysis is different: it produces an alkanol and an alkanoic acid and is reversible. In alkaline hydrolysis, the acid product is converted into its carboxylate salt, helping drive the reaction towards products.

Topic 10: Identification of unknown organic compounds

Identification of an unknown organic compound is based on a sequence of observations and chemical tests. No single result should be used in isolation when several compounds may give similar reactions. Start with physical properties and solubility, then use tests that identify functional groups and confirm the likely structure.

10.1 Deducing families and formulae

First note whether the unknown is a solid, liquid or gas, its colour and any safe-to-observe physical properties. Do not directly smell or taste an unknown substance. Test its solubility in water and, where appropriate, its effect on indicator paper. Use the results to decide which functional-group tests are suitable.

Calculate possible molecular formulae from percentage composition or molar mass where these data are available. Determine the empirical formula by converting percentage composition to mole ratios. Use the molecular mass to obtain the molecular formula multiplier. Then compare the formula with the general formulae of possible homologous series.

A positive aldehyde test supports an alkanal; a negative result, together with a positive carbonyl test, supports an alkanone. 10.4 Drawing a conclusion

A good identification report records the test, the exact observation and the inference separately. For example: “2,4-DNPH produced an orange precipitate” is an observation; “a carbonyl group is likely present” is the inference. A positive Tollens' test then supports an alkanal. The final conclusion should state the most likely family and explain which results support it, while acknowledging any test that was inconclusive.

Safety: Tollens' reagent must be freshly prepared and never stored; residues can form hazardous explosive compounds on standing. Dispose of it immediately using the laboratory's approved procedure. Bromine water, concentrated acids and organic solvents require appropriate protective equipment and supervision. Never carry out unknown-compound testing outside a properly equipped laboratory.
