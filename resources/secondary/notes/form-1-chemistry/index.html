Topic 1: Introduction to chemistry

1.1 What chemistry studies

Chemistry is the science that studies substances: what they are made of, how their particles are arranged, the properties they show and how they change into other substances.

1.2 Main branches of chemistry

  • Physical chemistry: explains energy, rates and the physical principles behind chemical processes.
  • Environmental chemistry: studies chemicals in air, water and soil and how human activities affect them.
  • Analytical chemistry: identifies substances and measures how much of each is present.
  • Industrial chemistry: applies chemical processes to convert raw materials into useful products.
  • Organic chemistry: studies most carbon compounds, especially those containing carbon–hydrogen bonds.
  • Inorganic chemistry: studies elements and compounds outside the main area of organic chemistry.

1.3 Chemistry in daily life and careers

Chemistry is used in water treatment, cooking, food preservation, soap and detergent production, medicines, fertilisers, fuels, mining and the manufacture of plastics and building materials. Doctors and nurses use chemical knowledge to understand body processes and medicines; pharmacists prepare and dispense medicines; food chemists test food quality; teachers explain scientific ideas; environmental chemists monitor pollution and waste.

1.4 The chemistry laboratory

A laboratory is a room equipped for scientific investigations. Apparatus commonly includes test tubes, beakers, conical flasks, measuring cylinders, funnels, glass rods, burettes, pipettes, thermometers, balances, evaporating dishes, crucibles, tongs, tripods, wire gauze and Bunsen burners.

1.5 Laboratory safety

  • Wear a lab coat, suitable footwear and eye protection; use gloves when instructed.
  • Never taste chemicals or pipette by mouth. Do not eat or drink in the laboratory.
  • Read labels and instructions before using a chemical. Use only the quantities and procedures directed by the teacher.
  • Keep flammable liquids away from flames and electrical equipment away from water.
  • Tie back long hair, keep benches clear and report spills, breakages or injuries immediately.
  • Do not run, play or carry out unauthorised experiments. Wash hands after practical work.
  • Know the location of the eyewash, safety shower, fire extinguisher and exits.

1.6 SI units and measurement

Measurements should include a number and a unit. Common base quantities used at this level are length (metre, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s) and temperature (kelvin, K). Common derived units include area (m²), volume (m³), speed (m s⁻¹) and density (kg m⁻³). In the laboratory, volume is often recorded in cm³ or mL and temperature in °C.

  • Mass: measure using a balance. Check zero before use and read the displayed value carefully.
  • Volume: use a measuring cylinder for ordinary liquid volumes. Read the bottom of a concave meniscus at eye level.
  • Temperature: use a thermometer; keep the bulb immersed without touching the container and wait for a steady reading.
  • Time: use a stopwatch; start and stop consistently and record the appropriate units.

1.7 Scientific investigation

  1. Identify a question or problem.
  2. Suggest a testable hypothesis (a proposed explanation or prediction).
  3. Plan a fair experiment, identifying the variable changed, the variable measured and variables kept constant.
  4. Collect observations and measurements carefully.
  5. Organise and analyse the results, often using a table or graph.
  6. Draw a conclusion supported by the evidence and state possible sources of error.

Topic 2: Essential mathematical skills in chemistry

2.1 Standard form

Standard form writes a number as a × 10ⁿ, where a is at least 1 but less than 10 and n is an integer. It is useful for very large and very small measurements.

45 000 = 4.5 × 10⁴
0.00072 = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴

For a large number, move the decimal point left until one non-zero digit remains before it; the exponent is positive. For a small decimal, move it right; the exponent is negative.

2.2 Significant figures

  • All non-zero digits are significant: 583 has 3 significant figures.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits count: 4007 has 4.
  • Leading zeros do not count: 0.0048 has 2.
  • Zeros after a decimal point at the end of a measured value count: 2.50 has 3.
  • For a whole number ending in zeros, use standard form to make the intended precision clear.

To round, keep the required number of digits and inspect the next digit. If it is 5 or more, increase the last retained digit by one; if it is 4 or less, leave it unchanged.

Worked example

Round 18.476 to 3 significant figures. Keep 1, 8 and 4; the next digit is 7, so round up: 18.5.

For multiplication and division, report the answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise measured value. For addition and subtraction, the result is limited by the least precise decimal place. Follow the convention your teacher specifies for the calculation.

2.3 Accuracy and precision

Accuracy describes how close a result is to the accepted or true value. Precision describes how close repeated measurements are to one another. A set of readings can be precise but inaccurate if they cluster tightly around the wrong value.

2.4 Graphs

  • Line graph: shows how a continuous quantity changes with another quantity.
  • Bar graph: compares separate categories.
  • Pie chart: shows parts of a whole; all sectors total 360°.

For a graph of A against B, put A on the vertical axis and B on the horizontal axis. Include a clear title, labelled axes with units, an even scale and accurately plotted points. For a pie chart, sector angle = (category amount ÷ total) × 360°.

Practice
  1. Write 6 250 000 in standard form.
  2. Write 0.000036 in standard form.
  3. How many significant figures are in 0.02040?
  4. Round 0.006784 to 2 significant figures.
  5. Explain the difference between accuracy and precision.
  6. A category represents 25% of a total. Calculate its pie-chart angle.

Topic 3: Composition and classification of matter

3.1 Matter and its states

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. It is commonly classified as solid, liquid or gas. The differences can be explained by the arrangement, spacing and movement of particles.

StateParticle arrangement and motionShape and volumeCompressibility
SolidClosely packed; vibrate around fixed positionsFixed shape and volumeVery difficult to compress
LiquidClose together but irregular; slide past one anotherFixed volume; takes container shapeVery difficult to compress
GasFar apart; move rapidly and randomlyNo fixed shape or volumeReadily compressed
Particle model: spacing and freedom of movement increase from solid to gas.

3.2 Diffusion

Diffusion is the net spreading of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration because particles move randomly. It is usually fastest in gases, slower in liquids and extremely slow in solids. The spreading of perfume through a room and potassium manganate(VII) colour through still water are examples.

3.3 Elements, symbols and molecules

An element is a pure substance made of one type of atom and cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by ordinary chemical methods. A chemical symbol is a short way of writing an element's name. The first letter is capital; a second letter, if present, is lowercase.

A molecule is a group of atoms chemically bonded together. Monoatomic particles contain one atom (for example He), diatomic molecules contain two atoms (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂) and polyatomic molecules contain more than two atoms (H₂O, CO₂, P₄, S₈).

Models of molecule formation

Atoms combine in fixed ratios when chemical bonds form. A model can show which atoms are present and how they are connected. For example, two hydrogen atoms combine to form one hydrogen molecule, while two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom form one water molecule.

Simplified particle model: the larger oxygen atom is joined to two hydrogen atoms in water.

3.4 Compounds and formulae

A compound contains two or more different elements chemically combined in fixed proportions. A compound has properties different from those of its constituent elements. Its chemical formula uses element symbols and subscripts to show the number of atoms.

H₂O: 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom
CO₂: 1 carbon atom and 2 oxygen atoms
Ca(OH)₂: 1 calcium, 2 oxygen and 2 hydrogen atoms
Cu(NO₃)₂: 1 copper, 2 nitrogen and 6 oxygen atoms

A subscript outside brackets multiplies every atom inside the brackets. A coefficient in front of a formula multiplies the entire formula: 3H₂O contains 6 H atoms and 3 O atoms.

3.5 Pure substances, mixtures and solutions

A pure substance has a fixed composition and characteristic properties. A mixture contains substances physically combined in variable proportions; its components retain their chemical identities and can often be separated physically. A homogeneous mixture is uniform throughout, while a heterogeneous mixture is not uniform.

A solution contains a solute dissolved in a solvent. An aqueous solution uses water as the solvent; a non-aqueous solution uses another solvent. Solutions can be solid-in-solid (alloys), liquid-in-liquid, solid-in-liquid or gas-in-liquid. An unsaturated solution can dissolve more solute at a given temperature; a saturated solution contains the maximum amount that can dissolve under those conditions.

3.6 Solubility and separation methods

Solubility is the amount of solute that dissolves in a stated amount of solvent at a specified temperature. For many solid solutes, solubility increases with temperature, but this is not universal. Stirring and crushing usually increase the rate of dissolving rather than the equilibrium solubility. The nature of solute and solvent also matters.

MethodPrinciple and suitable use
FiltrationSeparates an insoluble solid from a liquid using filter paper; solid remains as residue and liquid passes as filtrate.
DecantationCarefully pours liquid away from settled solid or separates immiscible liquid layers.
Evaporation/crystallisationRemoves some solvent to obtain a dissolved solid; crystallisation gives crystals as the solution cools or becomes concentrated.
Simple distillationEvaporates and condenses a liquid to separate it from dissolved non-volatile substances or recover a solvent.
Fractional distillationSeparates miscible liquids with different boiling points using repeated vaporisation and condensation in a fractionating column.
ChromatographySeparates dissolved substances because they travel at different rates between a stationary phase and a moving solvent.
Magnetic separationUses a magnet to remove magnetic material from a non-magnetic mixture.
Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid.

Topic 4: Atomic structure

4.1 Structure of an atom

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons occupying energy levels around it. Most of the atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

ParticleRelative chargeApproximate relative massLocation
Proton+11Nucleus
Neutron01Nucleus
Electron−11/1840 (very small)Energy levels around nucleus

4.2 Atomic number and mass number

The atomic number (proton number, Z) is the number of protons. The mass number (nucleon number, A) is the total number of protons and neutrons. Therefore:

Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number
For a neutral atom: electrons = protons = atomic number

For an ion, electrons differ from protons: a positive ion has lost electrons; a negative ion has gained electrons. Example: Na⁺ has 11 protons and 10 electrons; Cl⁻ has 17 protons and 18 electrons.

4.3 Nuclide notation and isotopes

A nuclide is written with the mass number at the upper left and atomic number at the lower left of the symbol: ᴬZX. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

¹²₆C and ¹⁴₆C: both have 6 protons; neutrons are 6 and 8 respectively.

4.4 Electron arrangement

Electrons occupy energy levels (shells). For the first twenty elements at this level, the simple shell capacities used are 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second and 8 in the third before the fourth begins. Write the electron arrangement by filling the inner shells first.

4.5 Relative atomic mass from isotopes

The relative atomic mass of an element is the weighted mean of the masses of its naturally occurring isotopes, using their relative abundances.

Relative atomic mass = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance)
Worked example

An element has isotopes of mass 10 (20%) and mass 11 (80%). Relative atomic mass = (10 × 0.20) + (11 × 0.80) = 2.0 + 8.8 = 10.8.

Topic 5: The periodic table

5.1 Organisation of the periodic table

The periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number. A horizontal row is a period; a vertical column is a group. Elements in the same group have similar outer-shell electron arrangements and often show similar chemical behaviour.

5.2 Groups, periods and electron arrangement

  • For main-group elements, the period number indicates the number of occupied electron shells.
  • For Groups I to VII in the older school notation, the group number generally indicates the number of outer-shell electrons.
  • Noble gases have full outer shells (helium has 2; the others have 8 in their outer shell in the simple model).

5.3 First twenty elements

Learn the names, symbols and atomic numbers from hydrogen (1) to calcium (20). The first twenty include H, He, Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, K and Ca.

5.4 Families of elements

  • Group I — alkali metals: soft, reactive metals with one outer electron. They form +1 ions. Reactivity generally increases down the group. They react with water to produce an alkaline hydroxide and hydrogen gas.
  • Group II — alkaline earth metals: metals with two outer electrons, commonly forming +2 ions. They react with oxygen to form oxides; reactivity with water generally increases down the group.
  • Group VII — halogens: reactive non-metals that exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂). They gain one electron to form −1 ions. Reactivity generally decreases down the group, while melting and boiling points increase.
  • Group VIII/18 — noble gases: colourless, monoatomic gases with full outer shells and very low chemical reactivity.

5.5 Trends across and down the table

Atomic radius generally increases down a group because extra electron shells are added. Across a period, atomic radius generally decreases because nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same main shell. For main-group elements, first ionisation energy generally decreases down a group and increases across a period, although there are exceptions. Electronegativity generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.

Practice
  1. Define a group and a period.
  2. Explain why elements in the same main group often react similarly.
  3. Give the shell arrangements of sodium, chlorine and calcium.
  4. Compare the reactivity trends of Group I metals and Group VII halogens.
  5. Explain why atomic radius increases down Group I.

Topic 6: Physical and chemical changes

6.1 Physical changes

A physical change alters the state, shape or appearance of a substance without changing its chemical identity. Examples include melting ice, boiling water, cutting paper and dissolving sugar in water. Many physical changes can be reversed by a physical process.

6.2 Chemical changes and reactions

A chemical change forms one or more new substances with different properties. Examples include burning, rusting, cooking and decomposition. Common evidence includes a colour change, gas formation, precipitate formation or temperature/light change; no single observation alone proves a chemical reaction in every case.

Physical changeChemical change
No new chemical substance is formed.New substances are formed.
Usually involves change of state, shape or size.Atoms are rearranged as bonds break and form.
Often easier to reverse by physical means.Often difficult to reverse by simple physical means.

6.3 Reactants, products and equations

Reactants are the starting substances; products are the substances formed. A chemical equation represents the rearrangement of atoms. Reactants are written on the left and products on the right. A plus sign means “reacts with” and the arrow means “forms”. State symbols are (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) aqueous solution.

Word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
Symbol equation: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)

6.4 Balancing equations

An equation is balanced when the number of atoms of every element is the same on both sides. Change coefficients in front of formulae; never change subscripts because that would change the substance.

Worked example

Balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. Put 2 before water to balance oxygen, then 2 before hydrogen: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. There are four H atoms and two O atoms on each side.

6.5 Conservation of matter and simple mass calculations

In a chemical reaction in a closed system, matter is conserved: atoms are rearranged but not created or destroyed. Therefore, total mass of reactants equals total mass of products.

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
48 g Mg + 32 g O₂ → 80 g MgO

Use the balanced equation and relative atomic masses to form mass ratios. Example: 24 g of magnesium reacts with 16 g of oxygen to form 40 g of magnesium oxide.

6.6 Percentage composition

Relative formula mass (RFM) = sum of (atomic mass × number of atoms)
% by mass of an element = (mass contributed by that element ÷ RFM) × 100
Worked example: calcium carbonate, CaCO₃

Using Ca = 40, C = 12 and O = 16: RFM = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. Calcium = 40%, carbon = 12%, oxygen = 48%. The percentages add to 100%.

Practice

  4. Explain why a reaction in an open container may appear to lose mass when gas escapes.
  5. Calculate the mass of oxygen needed to react completely with 6 g magnesium, using 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Mg = 24, O = 16).
  6. Calculate the percentage by mass of hydrogen in water (H = 1, O = 16).

Topic 7: Organic compounds

7.1 Organic compounds

Organic chemistry focuses on carbon compounds, especially compounds containing carbon–hydrogen bonds. Many substances in living organisms, fuels, plastics, medicines, fats and carbohydrates are organic. Carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide and metal carbonates are usually treated as inorganic compounds.

7.2 Historical idea and sources

Early scientists believed that organic substances could only be produced by living organisms. Later laboratory synthesis showed that organic compounds can also be made artificially. Sources include plants and animals, crude oil, natural gas and coal.

7.3 Fuels

A fuel is a substance that releases useful energy when it reacts, usually by combustion. Household examples include wood, charcoal, paraffin (kerosene), LPG (mainly propane and butane), biogas and ethanol. Fuels may be solid, liquid or gaseous.

  • Biofuels: produced from recently living biological material; examples include wood, biogas, bioethanol and biodiesel. Many can be replenished, although sustainability depends on how they are produced.
  • Fossil fuels: formed from ancient organic remains over geological time; examples are coal, petroleum and natural gas. They are non-renewable on a human timescale.

7.4 Petroleum and its fractions

Petroleum (crude oil) is a naturally occurring mixture of hydrocarbons. A hydrocarbon contains carbon and hydrogen only. Crude oil is separated into fractions by fractional distillation because the hydrocarbons have different boiling ranges. The column is hot near the bottom and cooler near the top: vapours rise and condense at different levels.

Simplified fractionating column: lighter fractions leave higher up; heavier fractions condense lower down.
FractionCommon uses
Refinery gases (LPG)Cooking and heating fuel.
Petrol/gasolineFuel for many cars and small engines.
Kerosene/paraffinJet fuel, heating and lighting.
DieselFuel for buses, trucks, tractors and some generators.

7.5 Environmental considerations

Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide; incomplete combustion can produce poisonous carbon monoxide and soot. Sulphur impurities can form sulphur dioxide. Efficient appliances, cleaner fuels, renewable energy and careful maintenance can reduce harmful emissions.
