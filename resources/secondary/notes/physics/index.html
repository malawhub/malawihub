1. Physics as a science

Physics is the study of matter, energy and the forces and interactions that explain natural events. It uses observation, measurement, experiments and mathematical relationships to explain how the physical world behaves.

Branches and applications

  • Mechanics: motion, forces and energy.
  • Thermal physics: heat, temperature and changes in matter.
  • Electricity and magnetism: charges, current, circuits and magnetic effects.
  • Waves and optics: sound, light and wave behaviour.
  • Nuclear physics: atomic nuclei and radioactivity.

Physics supports engineering, telecommunications, medical imaging, transport, electricity generation, meteorology and many agricultural technologies.

2. Scientific investigation

A scientific investigation is an organised way of answering a question using evidence. Begin with an observation, define a focused question and propose a testable hypothesis. Plan a fair test, collect measurements, analyse the results and make a conclusion supported by the evidence.

Variables

  • Independent variable: the factor deliberately changed.
  • Dependent variable: the quantity measured as a response.
  • Controlled variables: factors kept the same to make the test fair.

Record results with units in a table. A graph should have a title, labelled axes, units, a sensible scale and accurately plotted points. The conclusion should state whether the results support the hypothesis and mention limitations or sources of error.

3. Laboratory safety and apparatus

Wear suitable protective clothing and eye protection, follow instructions and never taste chemicals. Keep flammable materials away from flames, report breakages and spills, and wash hands after practical work. Hazard labels warn about risks such as flammability, toxicity, corrosiveness and environmental harm.

Common measuring instruments

  • Metre rule or tape measure: length.
  • Balance: mass.
  • Measuring cylinder: liquid volume; read the bottom of a concave meniscus at eye level.
  • Thermometer: temperature.
  • Stopwatch: time.
  • Ammeter: current; connect in series.
  • Voltmeter: potential difference; connect in parallel.

4. Measurements and SI units

A physical quantity is something that can be measured and expressed as a number with a unit. The SI system provides internationally agreed units.

QuantitySI unitSymbol
Lengthmetrem
Masskilogramkg
Timeseconds
TemperaturekelvinK
Electric currentampereA

Derived quantities are formed from base quantities. For example, speed = distance ÷ time and area = length × width.

speed = distance / time (m s⁻¹)
density = mass / volume (kg m⁻³)

Prefixes represent powers of ten: kilo (k) = 10³, centi (c) = 10⁻², milli (m) = 10⁻³ and micro (µ) = 10⁻⁶. Scientific notation has the form a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ a < 10.

5. Particulate nature of matter

Matter consists of tiny particles. In a solid, particles are close together and vibrate around fixed positions. In a liquid, particles remain close but can slide past one another. In a gas, particles are far apart and move rapidly in random directions.

Original particle model: particle spacing and freedom of movement increase from solid to gas.

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration due to random motion. It is faster at higher temperatures because particles have greater average kinetic energy.

6. Changes of state

Heating can cause melting (solid to liquid), evaporation or boiling (liquid to gas) and sublimation (solid to gas). Cooling can cause condensation (gas to liquid), freezing (liquid to solid) and deposition (gas to solid).

During a change of state, energy is used to change the separation and arrangement of particles. For a pure substance at constant pressure, temperature remains constant during melting or boiling even though energy continues to be transferred.

melting ⇄ freezing
evaporation/boiling ⇄ condensation
sublimation ⇄ deposition

7. Force and friction

A force is a push or pull. It can change an object's speed, direction or shape. Force is a vector quantity and its SI unit is the newton (N). The resultant force is the single force that has the same effect as all forces acting together.

resultant force = vector sum of all forces

Balanced forces have zero resultant and do not change the state of motion. Unbalanced forces cause acceleration. Friction acts opposite to relative motion or attempted motion between surfaces. It depends on the nature of the surfaces and how strongly they are pressed together.

Friction can be useful for walking, braking and gripping, but it also causes wear and unwanted heating. Lubrication, rollers and smoother surfaces can reduce friction.

8. Work

Work is done when a force causes displacement in its direction. Pushing a wall that does not move does no mechanical work on the wall.

W = F × s
W in joules (J), F in newtons (N), s in metres (m)

If force and displacement are not in the same direction, only the component of force along the displacement contributes to work.

9. Energy

Energy is the capacity to do work. Common forms include kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, chemical, thermal, electrical, light, sound and nuclear energy. Energy can be transferred or transformed but the total energy of an isolated system is conserved.

kinetic energy: Eₖ = ½mv²
gravitational potential energy: Eₚ = mgh

Energy sources include solar, wind, hydroelectric, biomass, fossil fuels and nuclear fuels. Compare sources by reliability, cost, environmental effects and whether they are renewable.

10. Electric current and circuits

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. A circuit needs a complete conducting path and an energy source. A switch opens or closes the path. Conventional current is defined as flowing from the positive terminal to the negative terminal through the external circuit.

I = Q / t
current (A) = charge (C) / time (s)

An ammeter is connected in series so the same current passes through it. In a series circuit, current is the same at every point. In a parallel circuit, current divides between branches.

Simplified circuit: a cell, switch and lamp connected in one complete loop.

11. Voltage (potential difference)

Potential difference is the energy transferred per unit charge between two points. A voltmeter is connected in parallel across the component.

V = W / Q
1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb

In a series circuit, the supply voltage is shared between components. In a parallel circuit, each branch has the same potential difference as the supply.

12. Electrical resistance

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current. It is measured in ohms (Ω). For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference.

V = IR
R = V / I

Resistance depends on the material, length, cross-sectional area and temperature of a conductor. A longer wire usually has greater resistance; a thicker wire usually has lower resistance.

13. Effects of electric current

Current can produce heating, magnetic and chemical effects. A heating element converts electrical energy into thermal energy. Current in a wire produces a magnetic field. In electrolysis, current through an electrolyte causes chemical changes and may deposit material at an electrode.

electrical power: P = VI
electrical energy: E = Pt

Use fuses and circuit breakers to interrupt excessive current. Never overload sockets or handle damaged electrical equipment.

14. Magnetism

Magnets have north and south poles. Like poles repel and unlike poles attract. Magnetic materials include iron, nickel and cobalt. The magnetic field is the region where a magnetic force can be detected; field lines are drawn from north to south outside a magnet.

1. Thermal expansion

Thermal expansion is an increase in the dimensions of a substance when its temperature rises. Heating increases the average kinetic energy of particles. In solids, particles vibrate more strongly about their fixed positions; the average separation increases slightly. Cooling generally causes contraction.

For equal temperature rises, gases usually expand more than liquids and liquids more than solids. Different materials also expand by different amounts.

Applications

  • Expansion gaps in railway tracks and bridges prevent buckling in hot weather.
  • Overhead cables are left with slack to allow for contraction in cold weather.
  • A tight metal bottle cap can be loosened by warming it because the cap expands.
  • Bimetallic strips bend when heated because the two bonded metals expand by different amounts; this is used in thermostats.
Schematic bimetallic strip: the more expansive layer becomes the outside of the bend.

Anomalous expansion of water

Water behaves unusually between 0 °C and 4 °C. When warmed from 0 °C to 4 °C, its volume decreases and its density increases. At 4 °C, water has maximum density. Above 4 °C, it expands when heated like most liquids. This helps explain why ice floats and why lakes can freeze at the surface while deeper water remains liquid.

2. Density and relative density

Density is mass per unit volume. It describes how much matter is packed into a given space.

ρ = m / V
SI unit: kg m⁻³

For a regular solid, calculate volume from its measured dimensions. For an irregular solid, use water displacement: the object's volume equals the final cylinder reading minus the initial reading.

Relative density is the ratio of the density of a substance to the density of water at the stated reference temperature. It has no unit.

relative density = density of substance / density of water

An object tends to float if its average density is lower than the fluid's density and sink if its average density is higher, provided other effects are negligible.

The rise in liquid level is the volume of the immersed object.

3. Specific heat capacity

Specific heat capacity is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K (or 1 °C). A substance with a high specific heat capacity needs more energy for the same mass and temperature rise.

Q = mcΔT
Q = thermal energy (J); m = mass (kg); c = specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹); ΔT = temperature change (K or °C)

Water has a relatively high specific heat capacity. It warms and cools slowly, moderating temperatures near large bodies of water and making it useful in some cooling systems.

4. Heat transfer

Conduction transfers thermal energy through a material without bulk movement of the material. It is especially effective in metals because mobile electrons help transfer energy.

Convection transfers energy by the bulk movement of a fluid. Heated fluid usually becomes less dense and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks, forming convection currents.

Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and does not require a medium. Dark, dull surfaces are generally good absorbers and emitters; shiny surfaces are generally poor absorbers and emitters.

5. Power and machines

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

P = W / t = E / t
1 watt = 1 joule per second

A machine makes a task easier by changing the size or direction of a force or the distance over which it acts. The effort is the input force and the load is the force overcome.

MA = load / effort
VR = distance moved by effort / distance moved by load
Efficiency = (MA / VR) × 100%

Simple machines include levers, pulleys, wheel and axle, inclined planes, wedges and screws. In an ideal machine, output work equals input work. Real machines have efficiency below 100% because some energy is dissipated, commonly as heat and sound.

The principle of moments states that, for rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about a pivot equals the sum of anticlockwise moments.

moment = force × perpendicular distance from pivot

6. Electrostatics

Electrostatics studies electric charges at rest. There are two kinds of charge: positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charging by friction transfers electrons between materials. A charged body can attract a neutral object by inducing charge separation (polarisation).

An electroscope can detect charge. When a charged object is brought near its cap, charge redistributes and the leaves diverge because they carry like charges. Earthing provides a conducting path for charge to flow to or from the Earth.

7. Light

Light travels in straight lines through a uniform transparent medium. Reflection occurs when light returns from a surface. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, measured from the normal. Refraction is the change in direction when light passes between media because its speed changes.

Shadows form when an opaque object blocks light. A pinhole camera forms an inverted image because light travels in straight lines through the small aperture. Eclipses occur when the Sun, Earth and Moon align so that one body blocks light from another.

8. Introduction to nuclear physics

An atom has a small, dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable nuclei.

Alpha radiation consists of helium nuclei, beta radiation consists of fast particles emitted in nuclear change and gamma radiation is high-energy electromagnetic radiation. They differ in penetrating and ionising abilities. Radiation can be useful in medicine, industry and research, but exposure must be controlled using appropriate shielding, distance and time precautions.

Use the exact nuclear notation and decay equations taught in class; always conserve nucleon number and proton number when balancing a nuclear equation.

1. Measurements and measuring instruments

Physics measurements require suitable instruments, correct units and careful reading. The seven SI base quantities are length (m), mass (kg), time (s), electric current (A), thermodynamic temperature (K), amount of substance (mol) and luminous intensity (cd). Derived units are combinations of base units.

A vernier calliper measures internal or external dimensions and depth more accurately than an ordinary ruler. A micrometer screw gauge measures very small thicknesses or diameters. Check for zero error before taking readings.

Vernier reading = main-scale reading + (coinciding vernier division × least count)
Micrometer reading = sleeve reading + (thimble division × pitch/number of divisions)

Write answers with units and sensible significant figures. Scientific notation is a × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ a < 10.

2. Scientific investigation and errors

A good investigation begins with a precise question and a testable hypothesis. Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables. Choose apparatus whose range and resolution are suitable, repeat measurements where possible and record observations systematically.

Random errors cause readings to vary unpredictably and can be reduced by repeating and averaging. Systematic errors shift readings consistently, for example because of zero error or poor calibration; repeating alone does not remove them. Avoid parallax by reading a scale at eye level.

mean = sum of readings / number of readings
percentage error = (absolute error / measured value) × 100%

3. Kinetic theory of matter

The kinetic theory explains matter in terms of particles in continuous motion. In solids, particles vibrate about fixed positions; in liquids, particles move around one another; in gases, particles move rapidly and randomly with large separations. Heating generally increases average kinetic energy.

Diffusion is the net movement from higher to lower concentration. Gas pressure results from particles colliding with the walls of their container. More frequent or more forceful collisions produce greater pressure.

4. Thermometry

Temperature indicates how hot or cold a body is and is related to the average kinetic energy of its particles. A thermometer uses a property that changes predictably with temperature, such as liquid volume, electrical resistance or thermoelectric voltage.

Kelvin temperature: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15
θ(°C) = T(K) − 273.15

Common thermometer types include liquid-in-glass, resistance, thermocouple and clinical thermometers. A fixed point is a reproducible temperature used for calibration. Read the scale only after the thermometer reaches thermal equilibrium with the object.

5. Pressure

Pressure is normal force per unit area. For the same force, a smaller area produces a greater pressure. This explains why a sharp blade cuts more easily than a blunt one.

P = F / A (Pa = N m⁻²)
Liquid pressure: p = ρgh

Liquid pressure increases with depth and density. At the same depth in a connected stationary liquid, pressure is the same. Pascal's principle states that pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally throughout the fluid; hydraulic systems use this effect.

Atmospheric pressure is caused by the weight of air. A barometer measures it. Archimedes' principle states that an immersed body experiences an upthrust equal to the weight of fluid displaced. An object floats when the upthrust balances its weight.

6. Gas laws

Gas laws describe how pressure, volume and temperature are related for a fixed mass of gas. Temperature must be expressed in kelvin when using the gas laws.

Boyle's law (constant T): p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
Charles' law (constant p): V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
Pressure law (constant V): p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂

Boyle's law means pressure rises when volume is reduced at constant temperature. Charles' law means volume increases in proportion to absolute temperature at constant pressure. The pressure law means pressure increases in proportion to absolute temperature at constant volume.

7. Scalars and vectors

A scalar has magnitude only, for example mass, time, distance, speed and energy. A vector has magnitude and direction, for example displacement, velocity, acceleration and force.

Vectors can be added using a scale drawing or by resolving them into perpendicular components. For two perpendicular components:

R = √(A² + B²)
tan θ = B / A
Perpendicular vectors A and B combine to give resultant R.

8. Linear motion

Distance is the total path travelled; displacement is the straight-line change in position with direction. Speed is distance per unit time; velocity is displacement per unit time. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

speed = distance / time
velocity = displacement / time
a = (v − u) / t
v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as

Use the equations of uniformly accelerated motion only when acceleration is constant. Near Earth's surface, the acceleration due to gravity is approximately 9.8 m s⁻² downward, subject to local conditions and air resistance.

On a distance-time graph, gradient gives speed. On a displacement-time graph, gradient gives velocity. On a velocity-time graph, gradient gives acceleration and area under the graph gives displacement.

9. Work and energy

Work is the product of a force and displacement in the direction of that force. Energy is conserved but may be transferred between stores.

W = Fs
Eₖ = ½mv²
Eₚ = mgh
power = work / time

The work-energy principle links the net work done on an object to its change in kinetic energy. In real processes, some useful mechanical energy is dissipated to the surroundings, often as thermal energy.

10. Machines

Machines change the size, direction or point of application of a force. Mechanical advantage compares load with effort; velocity ratio compares the distances moved by effort and load.

MA = load / effort
VR = distance moved by effort / distance moved by load
efficiency = (MA / VR) × 100%

Levers, pulleys, gears, wheel-and-axle systems and inclined planes are simple machines. A machine cannot create energy; friction and deformation reduce efficiency.

11. Electric current and potential difference

Electric current is the rate of flow of charge. Conventional current direction is from positive to negative in the external circuit; electron drift is in the opposite direction in metal wires.

I = Q / t
V = W / Q
emf = energy supplied per unit charge by a source

An ammeter is connected in series and a voltmeter in parallel. In a series circuit, current is common and potential differences add to the supply. In a parallel circuit, potential difference is common across branches.

12. Electrical resistance and power

Resistance is the ratio of potential difference to current for an ohmic conductor at constant physical conditions. Ohm's law states that current is directly proportional to potential difference when temperature and other conditions remain constant.

V = IR
P = VI = I²R = V²/R
E = Pt = VIt

Resistance increases with conductor length and decreases with cross-sectional area. It also depends on the material and temperature. Household circuits use parallel connections so appliances receive the supply voltage and can be controlled independently. Fuses and circuit breakers protect against excessive current.

13. Oscillations and waves

An oscillation is repeated motion about an equilibrium position. Amplitude is maximum displacement from equilibrium; period is time for one complete oscillation; frequency is number of oscillations per second.

f = 1 / T
v = fλ

A wave transfers energy without net transfer of matter. Transverse waves vibrate perpendicular to the direction of travel; longitudinal waves vibrate parallel to it. Wave behaviour includes reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference.

Wave diagram showing amplitude measured from equilibrium and wavelength between matching points.

14. Sound

Sound is produced by vibrating objects and travels through a medium as a longitudinal wave. It cannot travel through a vacuum. Frequency affects pitch; amplitude affects loudness. Resonance occurs when a system is driven near its natural frequency and responds with a large amplitude.

Sound speed depends on the medium and its conditions. In general, sound travels faster in solids than in liquids and faster in liquids than in gases. Echoes are reflected sound; they can be used to estimate distance when the sound speed and travel time are known.

distance to reflecting surface = (speed of sound × echo time) / 2

1. Thermal expansion, temperature and heat

Thermal expansion is the increase in size of a substance when heated. Temperature describes the degree of hotness or coldness of a body and is measured in kelvin (K) or degrees Celsius (°C). Heat is energy transferred from a body at higher temperature to one at lower temperature and is measured in joules (J).

According to the kinetic model, heating increases the kinetic energy of particles. In a solid, particles vibrate more strongly about their fixed positions and their average separation increases. Cooling reduces particle motion and usually causes contraction.

Expansion in solids

When a solid is heated, its length, area and volume can increase. The ball-and-ring experiment demonstrates this: a ball that passes through a ring when cold may no longer pass after it is heated. Heating the ring can enlarge its opening so that the ball passes again.

Original schematic of the ball-and-ring demonstration. Heat causes the solid components to expand.

Different solids expand by different amounts

A bimetallic strip consists of two different metals firmly joined together. When heated, the metal with the greater expansion bends the strip so that it lies on the outside of the curve. On cooling, the strip moves back towards its original shape. This principle is used in thermostats and automatic temperature switches.

Expansion in liquids and gases

Liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled. A liquid-in-glass thermometer uses this change in volume to indicate temperature. Gases also expand when heated; their particles move faster and spread further apart when the gas is free to expand.

Anomalous expansion of water

Unlike most liquids, water contracts when heated from 0 °C to 4 °C and has its greatest density at 4 °C. Above 4 °C, it expands as it is heated. When water cools below 4 °C, it expands as it approaches freezing. Ice is less dense than liquid water and floats.

Schematic volume–temperature curve: water has minimum volume (maximum density) at 4 °C.

Practical applications

  • Expansion gaps in railway tracks, bridges and fences prevent buckling or cracking.
  • Overhead telephone and electricity cables are installed with slack to allow for contraction in cold weather.
  • Heating a tight metal bottle cap can make it expand and loosen.
  • Hot rivets contract as they cool and pull joined metal plates tightly together.
  • Shrink fitting uses cooling to reduce a metal part's size before fitting; warming then produces a tight joint.

2. Newton's laws of motion, momentum and friction

The Form 4 source continues with Newton's laws and momentum on its printed pages 8–15. These ideas explain how forces change motion and why moving bodies resist changes in their motion.

Newton's first law: inertia

An object remains at rest or continues moving at constant velocity in a straight line unless acted on by a resultant external force. Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of motion. Greater mass means greater inertia. Seat belts help restrain passengers when a vehicle stops suddenly.

Newton's second law

The resultant force on an object is proportional to the rate of change of its momentum and acts in the direction of that force. For constant mass this becomes:

F = ma
momentum, p = mv
impulse, J = FΔt = Δp

Force is measured in newtons (N), momentum in kg m s⁻¹ and impulse in N s. A larger force or a longer contact time produces a larger change in momentum. Increasing the time over which a passenger is brought to rest reduces the average force.

Newton's third law

When one body exerts a force on another, the second body exerts an equal-sized force in the opposite direction on the first. The action and reaction forces act on different bodies, so they do not cancel each other on a single object. Walking, swimming and the propulsion of a boat are everyday examples.

Conservation of linear momentum

For a system with no resultant external force, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after it.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

In an elastic collision, total momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved. In an inelastic collision, momentum is conserved for an isolated system but kinetic energy is not; in a perfectly inelastic collision the bodies stick together.

Frictional force

Friction opposes relative motion or the tendency of surfaces to slide. It arises from interactions between contacting surfaces. It can cause heating, wear and sound. Friction is useful for walking, braking and gripping. It can be reduced by lubrication, polishing, rollers and ball bearings.

Frictional force = μN
μ = coefficient of friction; N = normal reaction

This simplified relation is used for the friction model in the notes. The normal reaction is the contact force perpendicular to the surface; it is not always equal to the object's weight, especially when additional vertical forces act.

Free fall, air resistance and terminal velocity

In ideal free fall, gravity is the only force acting. Near Earth's surface, the acceleration is approximately 9.8 m s⁻² downward. When an object falls through air or a liquid, it experiences resistive drag; an immersed object may also experience upthrust. As speed increases, drag generally increases. Terminal velocity is reached when the upward resistive forces balance the object's weight, making the resultant force zero and the velocity constant.

The speed increases at first, then levels off when drag balances weight.

3. Elasticity and Hooke's law

Elasticity is the ability of a material to return to its original shape and size after the deforming force is removed. Extension is the increase in length of a stretched spring. If the elastic limit is not exceeded, the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the applied force.

F = ke
k = F/e
k is the spring constant (N m⁻¹); e is extension (m)

The gradient of a force–extension graph in its straight-line region gives the spring constant. Beyond the proportional limit, the graph is no longer straight; beyond the elastic limit, permanent deformation occurs. The source demonstrates the law using a clamp stand, spring, ruler and added masses.

Schematic force–extension curve. Hooke's law applies in the straight-line region, before the proportional limit.

For identical springs, the equivalent spring constant is smaller in series and larger in parallel:

n identical springs in series: kₛ = k/n
n identical springs in parallel: kₚ = nk

Applications include spring balances, vehicle suspension, shock absorbers and elastic supports.

4. Uniform circular motion

Uniform circular motion is motion along a circular path at constant speed. Although speed is constant, velocity changes because direction changes continuously. The acceleration is directed towards the centre of the circle and is called centripetal acceleration.

one revolution = 2π radians = 360°
arc length: s = rθ (θ in radians)
angular velocity: ω = θ/t = 2πf
linear speed: v = ωr
centripetal acceleration: a = v²/r = ω²r
centripetal force: F = mv²/r

Angular displacement is measured in radians. One radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc whose length equals the radius. The centripetal force is the resultant inward force that keeps the object moving in a circular path; it is not an additional new kind of force.

5. Moments and torque

The moment of a force is its turning effect about a pivot. It depends on the size of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line of action.

moment = force × perpendicular distance
τ = Fd (unit: N m)

For an object in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about a point equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about the same point. A couple consists of two equal, opposite and parallel forces acting along different lines; it produces rotation without a resultant force. The moment of a couple is also called torque.

Examples include opening a door, turning a tap, using a spanner, scissors and a wheelbarrow.

6. Magnetism and electromagnetism

Magnets attract ferromagnetic materials such as iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Like poles repel and unlike poles attract. A magnetic material can be magnetised by stroking, induction, hammering in a suitable magnetic field or using an electric current. Hard magnetic materials retain magnetism; soft magnetic materials are easily magnetised and demagnetised.

Domain theory explains magnetisation: in an unmagnetised material, magnetic domains point in different directions. Magnetisation aligns more domains in one direction. Heating strongly, hammering or using an alternating magnetic field can demagnetise a magnet.

A current-carrying conductor produces a magnetic field. Around a straight wire, field lines are circular. The right-hand grip rule gives the field direction: point the thumb in the direction of conventional current and curled fingers show the field direction. A solenoid produces a field similar to that of a bar magnet; increasing current, number of turns or adding a soft-iron core strengthens the electromagnet.

Electromagnets are used in electric bells, relays, loudspeakers and telephone receivers. A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force. Fleming's left-hand rule gives the directions of force, field and current for the motor effect.

7. Electromagnetic induction and transformers

Electromagnetic induction occurs when a changing magnetic field through a conductor or coil induces an electromotive force. Moving a magnet faster, using a stronger magnet or increasing the number of turns can increase the induced emf. Faraday's law relates induced emf to the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage. Lenz's law states that the induced effect opposes the change that produces it.

Fleming's right-hand rule is used for the generator effect: thumb shows conductor motion, first finger magnetic field and second finger induced current. A transformer transfers electrical energy between circuits by mutual electromagnetic induction. Alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic field in the core, inducing an emf in the secondary coil.

ideal transformer: Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ
current ratio: Iₛ/Iₚ = Nₚ/Nₛ
efficiency η = (output power / input power) × 100%

A step-up transformer has more turns on the secondary coil and increases voltage. A step-down transformer has fewer secondary turns and reduces voltage. In an ideal transformer, input power equals output power; real transformers have losses.

8. Transformers, power losses and electricity transmission

Transformers are designed for alternating current because a changing current produces the changing magnetic field needed to induce an emf in the secondary coil. Real transformers lose energy through heating in the coils, eddy currents in the core, hysteresis and magnetic flux leakage.

Losses can be reduced by using thick, low-resistance copper wire, a soft magnetic core and a laminated core to reduce eddy currents. Oil can help remove heat. Electricity is transmitted at high voltage and low current to reduce heating losses in long transmission cables because cable power loss is Pₗₒₛₛ = I²R. Substations then reduce voltage for distribution to consumers.

P = VI
power loss in a cable = I²R

High-voltage transmission lines are dangerous. They are supported well above the ground or installed underground and must never be approached.

9. Generators, motors and sound transducers

An alternating-current generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy. A rotating coil cuts magnetic field lines and an alternating emf is induced; slip rings maintain connection to the rotating coil. A direct-current generator uses a split-ring commutator so the external current flows in one direction.

A direct-current motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences forces that produce a turning effect. A split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil every half-turn so the rotation continues in the same direction.

A moving-coil loudspeaker converts an alternating electrical signal into sound: current in a coil interacts with a permanent magnetic field, moving the coil and attached cone. A moving-coil microphone works in reverse: sound vibrations move a coil in a magnetic field and induce a small electrical signal.

10. Electronics and semiconductors

Conductors allow charge to move easily; insulators strongly resist charge flow. Semiconductors have conductivity between conductors and insulators. Silicon and germanium are common semiconductor materials. Their conductivity can be increased by heating or by adding controlled impurities, a process called doping.

In n-type material, donor impurities provide extra electrons; electrons are the majority charge carriers. In p-type material, acceptor impurities create holes; holes are the majority carriers. Joining p-type and n-type materials forms a p–n junction. A diode is a two-terminal device that allows current to flow readily in one direction when forward biased and blocks most current when reverse biased.

Rectification converts alternating current into direct current. A half-wave rectifier uses one diode and passes one half-cycle. A bridge full-wave rectifier uses four diodes so both half-cycles produce current through the load in the same direction.

Other electronic components in the source include:

  • Capacitor: stores electric charge and can smooth or filter signals.
  • Inductor: stores energy in a magnetic field.
  • Light-dependent resistor (LDR): its resistance decreases as light intensity increases.
  • Thermistor: its resistance changes with temperature; the direction of change depends on the type.
  • Light-emitting diode (LED): emits light when correctly forward biased.
  • Transistor: a three-terminal semiconductor device that can act as a switch or amplifier.
  • Photovoltaic cell: converts light energy directly into electrical energy.

An LDR and transistor can be combined to make a light-operated switch. The changing resistance of the LDR changes the transistor's base current, switching a lamp or other load on or off at a chosen light level.

11. Electronic signals and logic gates

An analogue signal varies continuously with time. A digital signal uses discrete levels, commonly represented by binary 0 and 1. Digital systems are less affected by gradual noise and can be stored, copied and processed reliably, while analogue systems represent continuous variation directly.

A logic gate performs a Boolean operation on one or more binary inputs and produces a binary output. The common gates are:

For two inputs A and B, the standard input combinations are 00, 01, 10 and 11. Construct a truth table by applying the gate rule to each row. Logic gates are built using electronic components such as transistors.

12. Electromagnetic waves

Electromagnetic waves consist of changing electric and magnetic fields that are perpendicular to one another and to the direction of travel. They are transverse waves and can travel through a vacuum at approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. They transfer energy and obey the wave equation.

c = fλ
c = speed (m s⁻¹); f = frequency (Hz); λ = wavelength (m)

The electromagnetic spectrum in order of increasing frequency is radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. As frequency increases, wavelength decreases and photon energy increases.

  • Radio: broadcasting and communication.
  • Microwaves: satellite links, radar and microwave cooking.
  • Infrared: heating, thermal imaging and remote controls.
  • Visible light: vision, photography and optical communication.
  • Ultraviolet: fluorescence and controlled sterilisation; excessive exposure damages skin and eyes.
  • X-rays: medical imaging and inspection of materials; exposure must be controlled.
  • Gamma rays: radiotherapy and sterilisation; they are highly penetrating ionising radiation.

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum, carry no electric charge and can be reflected, refracted, diffracted and absorbed.

13. Light and lenses

A lens is a transparent optical component that refracts light. A convex (converging) lens is thicker at the centre and brings parallel rays towards a focal point. A concave (diverging) lens is thinner at the centre and spreads parallel rays apart. Important terms include the optical centre, principal axis, principal focus and focal length.

thin lens equation: 1/f = 1/u + 1/v
magnification m = image height / object height = v/u

Use a consistent sign convention when applying the lens equation. For a convex lens, the image depends on the object's position: beyond 2f gives a real, inverted, diminished image; at 2f gives a real, inverted image of equal size; between f and 2f gives a real, inverted, magnified image; inside f gives a virtual, upright, magnified image on the object's side. A concave lens normally forms a virtual, upright and diminished image for a real object.

Convex lenses are used in cameras, projectors, magnifiers and optical instruments. The focal length can be estimated by focusing a distant object onto a screen, measuring object and image distances or using a graphical method with several measurements.

14. Optical instruments and the human eye

A camera uses a convex lens to form a real, inverted image on a light-sensitive film or sensor. The aperture controls the amount of light entering, the shutter controls exposure time and the focusing mechanism adjusts the lens position. A projector uses a converging lens to form an enlarged real image on a screen. A telescope uses an objective lens to form an image of a distant object and an eyepiece to magnify it.

The human eye focuses light on the retina. The cornea and eye lens refract light, the iris controls pupil size and the ciliary muscles change the shape of the lens for accommodation. The retina detects the image and the optic nerve carries signals to the brain.

Short-sightedness (myopia) makes distant objects unclear because the image forms in front of the retina; a concave lens helps diverge the rays before they enter the eye. Long-sightedness (hypermetropia) makes near objects unclear because the image would form behind the retina; a convex lens helps converge the rays.

15. Nuclear physics and radioactivity

An atom contains protons and neutrons in a small nucleus, with electrons around it. The atomic number Z is the number of protons. The mass number A is the total number of protons and neutrons. For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

neutron number = A − Z
nuclide notation: ᴬ_ZX

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different numbers of neutrons and therefore different mass numbers. Relative atomic mass can be calculated as the abundance-weighted mean of the isotopic masses.

Radioactivity is the spontaneous decay of unstable nuclei, emitting alpha particles, beta particles or gamma radiation. It is random for an individual nucleus but predictable for a large sample. Activity is the number of nuclear disintegrations per second and is measured in becquerels (Bq).

RadiationNatureRelative penetrationTypical shielding
Alpha (α)Helium nucleusLow; strongly ionisingPaper or outer skin
Beta (β)Fast electron or positronModerateThin aluminium
Gamma (γ)High-energy electromagnetic waveHigh; weakly ionising compared with alphaThick lead or concrete

Half-life is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei or the activity of a sample to fall to half its initial value.

N = N₀(½)^(t/T½)
A = A₀(½)^(t/T½)

After each half-life, half of the remaining radioactive nuclei have decayed. Background radiation is radiation that is always present in the environment, including contributions from cosmic rays and naturally occurring radioactive materials. To find a corrected count rate, subtract the background count rate from the measured count rate.

corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate

Radioactive sources must be handled using appropriate time, distance and shielding precautions. Never handle an unknown radioactive source.
