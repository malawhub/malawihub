1. Quadratic Equations

A quadratic expression has the form \(ax^2+bx+c\), where \(a\ne0\). A quadratic equation is an equation containing a squared unknown, usually written \(ax^2+bx+c=0\). The solutions are called roots.

Factorising when the coefficient of x² is 1

Find two numbers whose product is the constant term \(c\) and whose sum is the coefficient of \(x\), \(b\). Use them to split the middle term.

Worked example 1: Factorise \(x^2+7x+12\).
  1. Find two numbers with product 12 and sum 7: 3 and 4.
  2. Split the middle term: \(x^2+3x+4x+12\).
  3. Group: \(x(x+3)+4(x+3)\).
  4. Take out the common bracket: \(\boxed{(x+3)(x+4)}\).

Factorising when a is not 1

For \(ax^2+bx+c\), find two numbers whose product is \(ac\) and sum is \(b\), split the middle term and factor by grouping.

Worked example 2: Factorise \(2x^2+7x+3\).

Here \(ac=6\). The numbers 6 and 1 multiply to 6 and add to 7.

\(2x^2+6x+x+3=2x(x+3)+1(x+3)=\boxed{(2x+1)(x+3)}\).

Solving by factorisation

Set the equation equal to zero, factorise, then use the zero-product rule: if \(AB=0\), then \(A=0\) or \(B=0\).

Worked example 3: Solve \(x^2-5x+6=0\).

\((x-2)(x-3)=0\), so \(x-2=0\) or \(x-3=0\). Therefore \(\boxed{x=2\text{ or }x=3}\).

Completing the square

For \(x^2+bx+c=0\), move the constant, add \((b/2)^2\) to both sides, then write the left side as a perfect square. If the coefficient of \(x^2\) is not 1, divide through first.

Worked example 4: Solve \(x^2+6x+5=0\).

\(x^2+6x=-5\). Add 9 to both sides: \((x+3)^2=4\). Thus \(x+3=\pm2\), giving \(\boxed{x=-1\text{ or }-5}\).

Quadratic formula

For \(ax^2+bx+c=0\):
\[x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\]
Identify the signs of \(a,b,c\) before substituting. The discriminant is \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\).
  • \(\Delta>0\): two distinct real roots.
  • \(\Delta=0\): one repeated real root.
  • \(\Delta<0\): no real roots.
Worked example 5: Solve \(2x^2-3x-2=0\).

\(a=2,b=-3,c=-2\).

\(x=\frac{3\pm\sqrt{9+16}}4=\frac{3\pm5}4\). Hence \(\boxed{x=2\text{ or }x=-\frac12}\).

Forming equations from roots and word problems

If the roots are \(r_1,r_2\), a monic equation is \((x-r_1)(x-r_2)=0\). In a word problem, define the unknown, express the other quantities in terms of it, form the equation and reject impossible values such as a negative age or length.

Exam tip: Keep the equation equal to zero before factorising. Check each root by substituting it into the original equation.

Practice

  1. Factorise \(x^2+9x+20\).
  2. Solve \(x^2-x-12=0\).
  3. Solve \(3x^2+x-2=0\).
  4. Form a quadratic equation with roots 4 and −2.
Answers: 1. \((x+4)(x+5)\). 2. \(x=4,-3\). 3. \(x=\frac23,-1\). 4. \(x^2-2x-8=0\).

2. Irrational Numbers and Surds

A rational number can be written as \(p/q\), where p and q are integers and \(q\ne0\). An irrational number cannot be written as such a fraction; its decimal continues without terminating or recurring. Examples include \(\sqrt2\) and \(\pi\).

Surds and simplifying

A surd is an irrational root expressed exactly, such as \(\sqrt3\). To simplify, take out the largest perfect-square factor.

Example: \(\sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\times2}=6\sqrt2\). Also \(\sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2\).

Operations with surds

  • Add or subtract like surds only: \(3\sqrt2+5\sqrt2=8\sqrt2\).
  • Multiply: \(\sqrt a\,\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}\) for non-negative a,b.
  • Divide: \(\sqrt a/\sqrt b=\sqrt{a/b}\), where b is positive.
Example: \(2\sqrt3\times3\sqrt6=6\sqrt{18}=18\sqrt2\).

Conjugate surds and rationalising

The conjugate of \(a+\sqrt b\) is \(a-\sqrt b\). Their product is a difference of squares: \((a+\sqrt b)(a-\sqrt b)=a^2-b\). Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate to remove a surd from a denominator.

Example: \(\frac{1}{\sqrt5+2}\times\frac{\sqrt5-2}{\sqrt5-2}=\frac{\sqrt5-2}{5-4}=\boxed{\sqrt5-2}\).
Common mistake: \(\sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt a+\sqrt b\) in general. Simplify each surd before collecting like terms.

Practice

  1. Simplify \(\sqrt{108}\).
  2. Simplify \(4\sqrt3-\sqrt{27}\).
  3. Evaluate \(\sqrt8\times\sqrt{18}\).
  4. Rationalise \(\frac{3}{\sqrt7}\).
Answers: \(6\sqrt3\); \(\sqrt3\); 12; \(\frac{3\sqrt7}{7}\).

3. Circle Geometry: Chord Properties

A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance (radius) from its centre. A chord is a straight line segment joining two points on the circumference. A diameter is a chord passing through the centre and is the longest chord.

Important chord theorems

  • A perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.
  • A line from the centre that bisects a chord is perpendicular to the chord.
  • Equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre and are equidistant from the centre.
  • Chords equidistant from the centre are equal.
Worked example: A chord is 16 cm long. The perpendicular distance from the centre to the chord is 6 cm. The perpendicular bisects the chord, so half the chord is 8 cm. By Pythagoras, \(r^2=6^2+8^2=100\), hence \(\boxed{r=10\text{ cm}}\).

Using the chord theorem

When solving a diagram, mark the right angle from the centre to the chord, halve the chord if the perpendicular is drawn, then use Pythagoras. State the theorem that justifies each step.

Remember: The perpendicular bisects the chord, not necessarily the radius or the whole diagram.

Practice

  1. A chord is 10 cm long and 12 cm from the centre. Find the radius.
  2. A radius of 13 cm meets a chord at right angles 5 cm from its centre. Find the chord length.
Answers: 13 cm; 24 cm.

4. Algebraic Fractions

An algebraic fraction has an algebraic expression in its numerator or denominator. Factorise first, cancel common factors and state restrictions that make a denominator zero.

Simplifying

Example: \(\frac{x^2-9}{x^2+5x+6}=\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{(x+2)(x+3)}=\boxed{\frac{x-3}{x+2}}\), provided \(x\ne-3,-2\).

Adding and subtracting

Find the lowest common denominator (LCD), rewrite each fraction with that denominator, combine the numerators and simplify.

Example: \(\frac1x+\frac1{x+1}=\frac{x+1+x}{x(x+1)}=\boxed{\frac{2x+1}{x(x+1)}}\).

Multiplication and division

For multiplication, factorise and cancel common factors before multiplying. For division, change the second fraction to its reciprocal and multiply. Never cancel terms separated by addition.

Example: \(\frac{x}{3}\div\frac{x}{6}=\frac{x}{3}\times\frac6x=2\), for \(x\ne0\).

Equations involving algebraic fractions

Record excluded values, multiply every term by the LCD, solve the resulting equation and check that no solution is excluded.

Common mistake: \(\frac{x+2}{x}\) cannot be reduced by cancelling x with part of a sum. Only common factors can be cancelled.

Practice

  1. Simplify \(\frac{x^2-4}{x^2+x-2}\).
  2. Evaluate \(\frac2x+\frac3{2x}\).
  3. Simplify \(\frac{x}{5}\div\frac{2x}{15}\).
Answers: Factor carefully: \(\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{(x+2)(x-1)}=\frac{x-2}{x-1}\); \(\frac7{2x}\); \(\frac32\) for \(x\ne0\).

5. Sets

A set is a well-defined collection of distinct objects. The objects are elements. If 3 belongs to A, write \(3\in A\); if not, write \(3\notin A\).

Ways of describing sets

Roster form lists elements, e.g. \(A=\{2,4,6,8\}\). Set-builder form describes a rule, e.g. \(A=\{x:x\text{ is an even integer and }2\le x\le8\}\).

Set operations

  • Union \(A\cup B\): elements in A or B or both.
  • Intersection \(A\cap B\): elements common to both.
  • Complement \(A'\): elements in the universal set but not in A.
  • Empty set \(\varnothing\): a set with no elements.
  • Subset \(A\subseteq B\): every element of A is in B.
Example: If \(A=\{1,2,3,4\}\) and \(B=\{3,4,5\}\), then \(A\cup B=\{1,2,3,4,5\}\) and \(A\cap B=\{3,4\}\).

Venn diagrams and number of elements

Draw a rectangle for the universal set and overlapping circles for sets. Put the intersection in the overlap first. For two sets:

\[n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)\]
Example: 30 learners study Maths, 22 study English and 10 study both. The number studying at least one is \(30+22-10=\boxed{42}\).

Practice

  1. If \(n(A)=18,n(B)=15,n(A\cap B)=6\), find \(n(A\cup B)\).
  2. List the even integers between 1 and 9 in set-builder and roster forms.
Answers: 27; \(\{x:x=2n, n=1,2,3,4\}=\{2,4,6,8\}\).

6. Mapping and Functions

A relation connects elements of one set to elements of another. A function is a relation in which every input has exactly one output. An input cannot have two different outputs.

Domain, codomain and range

The domain is the set of permitted inputs. The codomain is the target set. The range is the set of outputs actually produced. A mapping diagram uses arrows to show the correspondence.

Types of mapping

  • One-to-one: different inputs have different outputs.
  • Many-to-one: two or more inputs share an output.
  • Onto: every element of the codomain is reached.
  • Into: at least one element of the codomain is not reached.

Function notation and finding range

\(f(x)=2x+3\) means substitute the input wherever x appears.

Example: If domain is \(\{-2,0,2,4\}\) and \(f(x)=2x+3\), outputs are −1, 3, 7, 11. Therefore range is \(\{-1,3,7,11\}\).

Real-life functions

If a taxi charges a fixed fee of MK 1,000 plus MK 500 per kilometre, the cost for d kilometres is \(C(d)=1000+500d\). State units and sensible restrictions on inputs.

Check: A function must assign one and only one output to each domain element.

Practice

  1. For \(f(x)=3x-2\), find \(f(4)\).
  2. Find the range for domain \(\{1,2,3\}\) when \(f(x)=x^2\).
Answers: 10; \(\{1,4,9\}\).

7. Circle Geometry: Angle Properties

Circle theorems allow unknown angles to be found without measuring. A central angle has its vertex at the centre; an angle at the circumference has its vertex on the circle.

Core theorems

  • Angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc.
  • Angles in the same segment are equal.
  • Angle in a semicircle is 90°.
  • Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°.
  • An exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral equals the opposite interior angle.

Concyclic points

Points are concyclic if they lie on one circle. To prove four points are concyclic, show that a pair of opposite angles add to 180°, or show that two angles standing on the same chord are equal.

Example: In cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, \(\angle A=108^\circ\). Opposite angle C is \(180^\circ-108^\circ=\boxed{72^\circ}\).
Exam method: Mark equal angles and right angles on the diagram, then write the theorem beside each calculation. Check whether angles stand on the same chord or arc.

Practice

  1. An angle at the circumference is 37°. Find the angle at the centre on the same arc.
  2. One angle of a cyclic quadrilateral is 94°. Find its opposite angle.
Answers: 74°; 86°.

8. Transformation

A transformation changes the position, orientation or size of a shape. The original is the object and the result is the image. Label corresponding vertices consistently, for example A maps to A′.

Rotation

A rotation is described by its centre, angle and direction (clockwise or anticlockwise). Every point stays the same distance from the centre. A 90° anticlockwise rotation about the origin maps \((x,y)\) to \((-y,x)\); a 180° rotation maps it to \((-x,-y)\).

Translation

A translation slides every point by the same vector. For vector \(\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\), \((x,y)\mapsto(x+a,y+b)\).

Example: Translate P(2,−1) by \(\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}\). New point is \((2+3,-1+4)=\boxed{(5,3)}\).

Enlargement

An enlargement has a centre and scale factor k. Distances from the centre are multiplied by k. If k is negative, the image is on the opposite side of the centre. A fractional scale factor produces a reduction.

Example: A point 4 units from the centre is enlarged by scale factor 2.5. Its image is 10 units from the centre along the same ray.

Describing transformations

For rotation state centre, angle and direction. For translation give the vector. For enlargement give centre and scale factor. Do not describe a transformation using only “moved” or “bigger”.

Practice

  1. Translate (−2,5) by \(\begin{pmatrix}4\\-3\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Rotate (3,−2) 180° about the origin.
  3. A length 6 cm is enlarged by scale factor 1.5. Find its image length.
Answers: (2,2); (−3,2); 9 cm.

9. Change of Subject of a Formula

Changing the subject means rearranging a formula so that a chosen variable stands alone on one side. Use inverse operations and perform the same operation on both sides.

Linear example

Make x the subject of \(y=3x+5\).

Subtract 5: \(y-5=3x\). Divide by 3: \(\boxed{x=\frac{y-5}{3}}\).

Formula involving powers

Make r the subject of \(A=\pi r^2\).

Divide by π: \(r^2=A/\pi\). Take the square root: \(\boxed{r=\sqrt{A/\pi}}\) when r represents a positive radius.

Fractions and brackets

Clear denominators by multiplying both sides by the denominator, expand brackets if necessary, collect terms containing the required subject and factor it out.

Example: From \(v=u+at\), make t the subject: \(v-u=at\), so \(\boxed{t=(v-u)/a}\), where \(a\ne0\).

Exponential and logarithmic forms

If \(y=a^x\), then \(x=\log_a y\) for \(a>0,a\ne1,y>0\). Use logarithms to bring an exponent down as a multiplier.

Check: Substitute your rearranged expression back into the original formula to see whether it is equivalent.

Practice

  1. Make h the subject: \(V=\frac13\pi r^2h\).
  2. Make x the subject: \(y=\frac{x-2}{5}\).
Answers: \(h=\frac{3V}{\pi r^2}\); \(x=5y+2\).

10. Exponential and Logarithmic Equations

An exponential equation has the unknown in the exponent, for example \(2^x=16\). A logarithm answers the question: “To what power must the base be raised to obtain this number?”

Index laws used in exponential equations

\[a^m a^n=a^{m+n},\quad \frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n},\quad (a^m)^n=a^{mn},\quad a^0=1\ (a\ne0)\]
Example: \(3^{x+1}=81=3^4\), so \(x+1=4\) and \(\boxed{x=3}\).

Meaning and laws of logarithms

\[\log_a b=c\iff a^c=b\] where \(a>0,a\ne1,b>0\).
  • \(\log_a(MN)=\log_aM+\log_aN\)
  • \(\log_a(M/N)=\log_aM-\log_aN\)
  • \(\log_a(M^k)=k\log_aM\)
  • \(\log_a1=0\), and \(\log_a a=1\).
Example: \(\log_2x=5\) means \(x=2^5=\boxed{32}\).

Solving logarithmic equations

Use log laws to combine terms, convert to exponential form or equate logarithm arguments when bases match. Check every solution in the original equation because log arguments must be positive.

Example: \(\log_3(x-1)=2\). Then \(x-1=3^2=9\), so \(x=10\), which satisfies \(x-1>0\).
Important: \(\log(A+B)\ne\log A+\log B\). The product law applies to multiplication, not addition.

Practice

  1. Solve \(5^{x}=125\).
  2. Evaluate \(\log_4 64\).
  3. Solve \(\log_2(x+3)=4\).
Answers: 3; 3; 13.

11. Trigonometry

Trigonometry connects angles and side lengths. In a right-angled triangle, identify the hypotenuse (opposite the right angle), opposite side and adjacent side relative to the chosen angle.

SOH–CAH–TOA
\[\sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad\cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\]
Worked example: A right triangle has opposite side 6 cm and hypotenuse 10 cm. \(\sin\theta=6/10=0.6\), so \(\theta=\sin^{-1}(0.6)=\boxed{36.9^\circ}\) (to 1 d.p.).

Finding missing sides and angles

Choose the ratio containing the known side and the required side. For an angle, use inverse trigonometric keys: \(\sin^{-1},\cos^{-1},\tan^{-1}\). Set the calculator to degrees when the question uses degrees.

Special angles

Know common exact values: \(\sin30^\circ=1/2\), \(\cos60^\circ=1/2\), \(\tan45^\circ=1\), \(\sin90^\circ=1\), \(\cos0^\circ=1\).

Elevation, depression and bearings

Angle of elevation is measured upwards from the horizontal; angle of depression is measured downwards from the horizontal. A bearing is measured clockwise from north and written using three digits, e.g. 065°. Draw a north line at the starting point before measuring a bearing.

Example: A tree is 12 m high and the horizontal distance to its foot is 5 m. The angle of elevation θ satisfies \(\tan\theta=12/5\), so \(\theta\approx67.4^\circ\).

Practice

  1. Find the hypotenuse if the opposite side is 9 cm and angle is 30°.
  2. Find θ if adjacent = 8 and hypotenuse = 10.
Answers: 18 cm; \(36.9^\circ\).

12. Similarity

Similar shapes have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding sides. They have the same shape but may have different sizes.

Scale factors

If the linear scale factor from a smaller shape to a larger shape is k, then lengths multiply by k, areas by \(k^2\) and volumes by \(k^3\).

\[\text{Length factor}=k,\quad\text{Area factor}=k^2,\quad\text{Volume factor}=k^3\]
Worked example: Two similar triangles have corresponding sides 4 cm and 10 cm. The enlargement factor is \(10/4=2.5\). If the smaller area is 12 cm², the larger area is \(12(2.5)^2=\boxed{75\text{ cm}^2}\).

Similar solids

For similar solids, use the cube of the linear scale factor to compare volumes. If volume ratio is known, take its cube root to obtain the linear ratio.

Common mistake: Do not use the length scale factor directly for area or volume. Square it for area and cube it for volume.

Practice

  1. Two similar shapes have length ratio 3:5. Find their area ratio.
  2. Two similar solids have linear ratio 2:3. Find their volume ratio.
Answers: 9:25; 8:27.

13. Coordinate Geometry

Coordinate geometry uses ordered pairs \((x,y)\) to describe points on a Cartesian plane. The gradient measures the steepness and direction of a straight line.

Gradient

\[m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\quad(x_2\ne x_1)\]
Example: For A(1,2) and B(5,10), \(m=(10-2)/(5-1)=\boxed2\).

Equation of a straight line

The slope-intercept form is \(y=mx+c\), where m is gradient and c is the y-intercept. Through a known point, use \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\).

Example: A line has gradient 3 and passes through (2,5). \(y-5=3(x-2)\), so \(\boxed{y=3x-1}\).

Parallel lines and midpoint

Parallel non-vertical lines have equal gradients. The midpoint of A(x₁,y₁) and B(x₂,y₂) is \(\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\). A vertical line has undefined gradient.

Gradient and angle

If a line makes angle θ anticlockwise with the positive x-axis, \(m=\tan\theta\), where the gradient is defined.

Practice

  1. Find the gradient through (−1,4) and (3,−4).
  2. Find the midpoint of (2,7) and (8,−1).
  3. Find the equation with gradient −2 through (1,3).
Answers: −2; (5,3); \(y=-2x+5\).

14. Variations

Variation describes how one quantity changes when another quantity changes. The constant of variation is usually represented by k and is found using the given values.

Direct variation

If y varies directly as x, \(y\propto x\), so \(y=kx\). The ratio y/x remains constant.

Example: y=18 when x=6. Then k=3 and when x=10, y=\(3(10)=\boxed{30}\).

Inverse variation

If y varies inversely as x, \(y\propto1/x\), so \(y=k/x\) or \(xy=k\). As x increases, y decreases when k is positive.

Example: y=4 when x=6, so k=24. When x=8, y=\(24/8=\boxed3\).

Joint and partial variation

If y varies jointly as x and z, \(y=kxz\). Partial variation combines a fixed amount and a varying amount, for example \(y=a+bx\). Use the information given to determine constants.

Practice

  1. y varies directly as x. If y=21 when x=7, find y when x=12.
  2. y varies inversely as x. If y=5 when x=4, find y when x=10.
Answers: 36; 2.

15. Graphs of Quadratic Functions

A quadratic function \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) produces a parabola. If a is positive it opens upwards and has a minimum; if a is negative it opens downwards and has a maximum.

Drawing a quadratic graph

  1. Choose a useful range of x-values.
  2. Calculate y for each x accurately.
  3. Plot points using a consistent scale.
  4. Join with a smooth curve, not straight segments.

Roots, intercepts and turning point

The roots are x-values where y=0, where the graph crosses or touches the x-axis. The y-intercept is c. The turning point has x-coordinate \(-b/(2a)\); substitute this x-value to find y.

Example: For \(y=x^2-4x+3\), factorise to get roots x=1 and x=3. The turning point x-coordinate is 2; y=4−8+3=−1. The minimum is \(\boxed{(2,-1)}\).

Maximum/minimum and simultaneous equations

Read the turning point from the graph or calculate it. A line and a parabola intersect where their equations are both true. Solve algebraically by substitution or estimate intersection coordinates from a graph.

Graph tip: Include a table of values and label axes, scale, intercepts and turning point. Read graph solutions to the accuracy requested.

Practice

  1. Find the roots of \(y=x^2-5x+6\).
  2. Find the turning point of \(y=x^2+2x-3\).
Answers: 2 and 3; (−1,−4), minimum.

16. Inequalities

An inequality compares quantities without requiring equality. Symbols: \(<\) less than, \(>\) greater than, \(\le\) less than or equal to and \(\ge\) greater than or equal to.

Solving linear inequalities

Solve like an equation, but reverse the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative number.

Example: \(-2x+3>7\). Subtract 3: \(-2x>4\). Divide by −2 and reverse the sign: \(\boxed{x<-2}\).

Number lines and graphs

Use an open circle for a strict inequality (< or >) and a filled circle for an inclusive inequality (≤ or ≥). Shade in the direction of the solutions.

Regions represented by inequalities

For two variables, draw each boundary line. Use a solid line for ≤ or ≥ and a dashed line for < or >. Test a point not on the line, often (0,0), to decide which side to shade. The feasible region is the overlap of all required half-planes.

Example: For \(y\ge2x+1\), draw the solid line \(y=2x+1\) and shade above it. The boundary is included.

Practice

  1. Solve \(3x-5\le10\).
  2. Solve \(-4x\ge12\).
Answers: \(x\le5\); \(x\le-3\).

17. Statistics

Statistics involves collecting, organising, presenting and interpreting data. Grouped data are arranged into class intervals with corresponding frequencies.

Class intervals, boundaries and midpoints

For whole-number inclusive classes such as 10–19 and 20–29, class boundaries are 9.5–19.5 and 19.5–29.5. The class midpoint is \((\text{lower limit} + \text{upper limit})/2\).

Frequency tables and graphs

  • A histogram uses adjoining bars; use class boundaries and frequency density if class widths differ.
  • A frequency polygon joins plotted points at class midpoints and frequencies.
  • A pie chart sector angle is \((f/\text{total})\times360^\circ\).
Example: If 15 out of 60 learners choose a subject, its pie-chart angle is \((15/60)\times360^\circ=\boxed{90^\circ}\).

Measures of central tendency for grouped data

Estimated mean: \(\bar x=\frac{\sum fm}{\sum f}\), where m is the class midpoint. Median estimate: \(L+\frac{(N/2-CF)}{f}w\), where L is the lower class boundary of the median class, CF the cumulative frequency before it, f the median-class frequency and w its width. Estimated mode: \(L+\frac{f_1-f_0}{2f_1-f_0-f_2}w\), where f₁ is modal-class frequency and f₀,f₂ are frequencies before and after it.

Range

For grouped data in this course, estimate the range by subtracting the lower limit of the first class from the upper limit of the last class. It describes spread but is sensitive to extreme values.

Practice

  1. Find the midpoint of class 20–29.
  2. A category has frequency 12 from total 48. Find its pie-chart angle.
  3. Find the range estimate for classes 5–9 through 30–34 using class boundaries.
Answers: 24.5; 90°; 29 (34−5).
