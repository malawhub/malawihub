1. Matrices

A matrix is a rectangular arrangement of numbers in rows and columns. The order is written rows × columns. For example, a matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns has order 2 × 3. A row matrix has one row; a column matrix has one column; a square matrix has the same number of rows and columns.

Equality, addition and subtraction

Matrices are equal when they have the same order and corresponding entries are equal. Addition and subtraction are possible only for matrices of the same order. Work entry by entry.

Example: \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\3&4\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}7&1\\2&6\end{pmatrix}\).

Scalar multiplication

Multiply every entry by the scalar.

Example: \(3\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\0&4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-6\\0&12\end{pmatrix}\).

Matrix multiplication

For AB to exist, the number of columns of A must equal the number of rows of B. Multiply each row of the first matrix by each column of the second and add the products. If A is m × n and B is n × p, AB has order m × p. In general AB is not equal to BA.

Example: \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4&10\\10&20\end{pmatrix}\). First entry: \(1(2)+2(1)=4\).

Finding unknown entries

Equate corresponding entries in equal matrices. If a matrix equation produces simultaneous linear equations, solve them together.

Check: Confirm the matrix orders before multiplying. Do not multiply matching positions for matrix multiplication; use row-by-column products.

Practice

  1. State the order of a matrix with 3 rows and 2 columns.
  2. Find \(2\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\4&0\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Can a 2×3 matrix multiply a 2×2 matrix on the right?
Answers: 3×2; \(\begin{pmatrix}6&-2\\8&0\end{pmatrix}\); no, inner dimensions 3 and 2 do not match.

2. Tangents to Circles

A tangent is a straight line that touches a circle at exactly one point, called the point of contact. The radius drawn to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent.

Using the right angle

Join the centre to the point of contact and to any external point. This creates a right-angled triangle, so use Pythagoras or trigonometry.

Example: Radius = 5 cm and distance from centre to an external point = 13 cm. Tangent length t satisfies \(t^2+5^2=13^2\), so \(t=\sqrt{144}=\boxed{12\text{ cm}}\).

Two tangents from an external point

Tangents drawn from the same external point to a circle are equal in length. The line joining the centre to the external point bisects the angle between the tangents.

Alternate segment theorem

The angle between a tangent and a chord through the point of contact equals the angle subtended by that chord in the alternate segment of the circle.

Constructing tangents

To construct a tangent at a point P on a circle: join the centre O to P, then construct a perpendicular to OP at P. To construct tangents from an external point T: join OT, construct its perpendicular bisector to find the midpoint M, draw the circle with centre M and radius MO; its intersections with the original circle locate the tangent contact points. Join each contact point to T.

Proof tip: State “radius is perpendicular to tangent at the point of contact” and use RHS congruence when proving the two tangent lengths are equal.

Practice

  1. A tangent is 15 cm and radius 8 cm. Find the distance from centre to external point.
  2. Two tangents from T have lengths 3x−2 and x+6. Find x.
Answers: 17 cm; x=4.

3. Statistics III: Measures of Dispersion

Measures of dispersion describe how spread out observations are. Two data sets can have the same mean but different variation.

Range

Range = largest value − smallest value. It is quick to calculate but uses only two observations.

Example: For 4, 6, 7, 10, 13, range = 13−4 = 9.

Variance

Variance is the mean of the squared deviations from the mean. For a population of N values:

\[\sigma^2=\frac{\sum(x-\bar{x})^2}{N}=\frac{\sum x^2}{N}-\bar{x}^{,2}\]

For a frequency table, use \(\bar x=\sum fx/\sum f\) and population variance \(\sigma^2=\sum f(x-\bar x)^2/\sum f\). Follow the question if it specifies a sample formula.

Example: Data 2, 4, 6 have mean 4. Squared deviations are 4, 0, 4. Population variance = 8/3 ≈ 2.67.

Standard deviation

Standard deviation is the positive square root of variance. It is measured in the same units as the original data. A small standard deviation means values cluster near the mean; a large one indicates greater spread.

\[\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\]
Do not confuse: Variance is in squared units; standard deviation is in the original units. Do not round intermediate values too early.

Practice

  1. Find the range of 8, 11, 15, 17, 19.
  2. Find the population variance and standard deviation of 2, 4, 6.
Answers: 11; variance = 8/3 ≈ 2.67, standard deviation ≈ 1.63.

4. Simultaneous Linear and Quadratic Equations

These problems contain one linear equation and one quadratic equation. A common method is substitution: make one variable the subject in the linear equation and substitute it into the quadratic equation.

Worked example: Solve \(y=x+1\) and \(y=x^2-3x+1\). Equate: \(x+1=x^2-3x+1\). Thus \(x^2-4x=0\), so \(x=0\) or \(x=4\). Using y=x+1 gives solutions \(\boxed{(0,1),(4,5)}\).

Graphical meaning

The solutions are the coordinates where the straight line and parabola intersect. There may be two intersections, one repeated/tangent intersection or no real intersection.

Practical problems

Define variables, translate each statement into an equation, solve simultaneously and interpret the pair of values in the context. Reject values that do not make sense in the situation.

Exam tip: Give both coordinates (x,y), not only the x-values, unless the question asks for x alone.

Practice

  1. Solve \(y=2x+1\) and \(y=x^2-2x+1\).
Answer: \(x^2-4x=0\), so x=0 or 4; points (0,1) and (4,9).

5. Progressions and Series

A progression is an ordered sequence following a rule. A series is the sum of terms of a sequence.

Arithmetic progression (AP)

In an AP, the difference between consecutive terms is constant, called the common difference d. The nth term is:

\[T_n=a+(n-1)d\]

Here a is the first term. The sum of the first n terms is:

\[S_n=\frac n2[2a+(n-1)d]=\frac n2(a+l)\]
Example: AP 3, 7, 11, … has a=3 and d=4. The 10th term is \(3+9(4)=39\). Sum of first 10 terms is \(10/2(3+39)=\boxed{210}\).

Geometric progression (GP)

In a GP, each term is obtained by multiplying the previous term by a constant ratio r.

\[T_n=ar^{n-1},\qquad S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}\quad(r\ne1)\]
Example: GP 2, 6, 18, … has a=2 and r=3. The 5th term is \(2(3^4)=\boxed{162}\).

Applications

AP models a quantity that increases or decreases by a fixed amount each period, such as a regular weekly saving increase. GP models repeated multiplication, such as compound growth or repeated halving. Identify whether the change is by addition or multiplication.

Check: For GP sums, use the correct formula and ensure r is not 1. If r=1, the sum is simply na.

Practice

  1. Find the 20th term of 5, 8, 11, …
  2. Find the sum of the first 12 terms of 2, 5, 8, …
  3. Find the 6th term of 3, 6, 12, …
Answers: 62; 222; 96.

6. Travel Graphs

Travel graphs describe motion. Read the axes and units before interpreting a graph. Distance–time graphs show how distance changes with time; velocity–time graphs show how velocity changes with time.

Distance–time graphs

Gradient = change in distance ÷ change in time = speed. A horizontal section means the object is stationary. A steeper line means greater speed.

Velocity–time graphs

Gradient = acceleration. A positive gradient indicates increasing velocity; a negative gradient indicates deceleration if velocity remains positive. A horizontal section indicates constant velocity.

\[a=\frac{v-u}{t}\]

Distance from a velocity–time graph

The area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement. If velocity is always positive, this is also distance travelled. For a rectangle use base × height; for a triangle use ½ × base × height; split compound shapes into simple areas.

Example: A vehicle accelerates uniformly from 0 to 20 m/s in 5 s. Acceleration = (20−0)/5 = 4 m/s². Distance in this interval is triangular area = ½×5×20 = 50 m.

Drawing and interpreting

Use a clear scale, label axes with units, plot points accurately and join them according to the type of motion described. Convert units consistently, e.g. km/h to m/s by multiplying by 5/18.

Practice

  1. A car travels 150 m in 10 s at constant speed. Find speed.
  2. Velocity increases from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 6 s. Find acceleration.
  3. Find distance represented by a 4 s rectangle at 8 m/s.
Answers: 15 m/s; 2 m/s²; 32 m.

7. Polynomials

A polynomial is an expression made from constants and non-negative integer powers of a variable. Its degree is the highest power with a non-zero coefficient.

Polynomial long division

Arrange terms in descending powers, inserting zero coefficients for missing powers. Divide the leading term, multiply back, subtract, bring down the next term and repeat until the remainder has lower degree than the divisor.

Remainder theorem

When polynomial f(x) is divided by \(x-a\), the remainder is \(f(a)\).
Example: Find the remainder when \(f(x)=x^3-2x+5\) is divided by x−2. Remainder = f(2) = 8−4+5 = \(\boxed9\).

Factor theorem and roots

\(x-a\) is a factor of f(x) exactly when f(a)=0. To factorise a cubic, test possible integer roots, divide by the corresponding linear factor, then factorise the remaining quadratic. A root is a value that makes the polynomial zero.

Identical polynomials

Two polynomials are identical when corresponding coefficients are equal after like powers are collected. Equate coefficients to find unknown constants.

Example: If \(2x^2+ax+3\equiv2x^2+5x+b\), then \(a=5\) and \(b=3\).

Practice

  1. Find the remainder when \(x^2+3x+1\) is divided by x+2.
  2. Show that x−2 is a factor of \(x^3-3x^2-4x+12\).
Answers: −1; f(2)=0, so x−2 is a factor.

8. Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is. For equally likely outcomes:

\[P(A)=\frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}}\]

Probability lies between 0 and 1. An impossible event has probability 0 and a certain event has probability 1.

Sample spaces and compound events

List all possible outcomes systematically using a table, ordered pairs or a tree diagram. Favourable outcomes are the outcomes that satisfy the event.

Example: Toss two coins. Sample space: HH, HT, TH, TT. Probability of exactly one head = 2/4 = 1/2.

Independent events

Events are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent A and B, \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\).

Mutually exclusive events

Events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time. Then \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\). For events that can overlap, subtract the intersection: \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\).

Experimental probability and tree diagrams

Experimental probability = number of times event occurs ÷ number of trials. On a tree, multiply probabilities along a path and add probabilities of separate paths that meet the event. Probabilities on branches from the same point sum to 1.

Important: With replacement, probabilities usually stay the same on the next draw. Without replacement, the total and composition change.

Practice

  1. A fair die is rolled. Find P(even).
  2. A coin is tossed twice. Find P(two tails).
  3. If P(A)=0.4 and P(B)=0.3 for mutually exclusive events, find P(A or B).
Answers: 1/2; 1/4; 0.7.

9. Vectors

A vector has magnitude and direction. A scalar has magnitude only. A column vector \(\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\) represents a horizontal movement a and vertical movement b.

Position vectors and zero vector

The position vector of point P(x,y) from origin O is \(\overrightarrow{OP}=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\). The zero vector has magnitude zero and no defined direction.

Magnitude

\[\left|\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\right|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\]
Example: Magnitude of \(\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}\) is \(\sqrt{9+16}=5\).

Operations and parallel vectors

Add or subtract corresponding components. Multiply every component by a scalar. Vectors are parallel if one is a scalar multiple of the other.

Example: \(\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\3\end{pmatrix}\).

Midpoint and collinearity

Midpoint position vector of A and B is half the sum of their position vectors. Points are collinear if their direction vectors are parallel or one vector is a scalar multiple of another.

Triangle and parallelogram laws

Triangle law: place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first; the resultant goes from the first tail to the final head. Parallelogram law: the diagonal from the common starting point represents the sum of two vectors drawn as adjacent sides.

Practice

  1. Find the magnitude of \(\begin{pmatrix}5\\12\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Find \(2\begin{pmatrix}-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Are (2,4) and (3,6) parallel?
Answers: 13; \(\begin{pmatrix}-2\\6\end{pmatrix}\); yes, second is 1.5 times first.

10. Trigonometry II

For non-right-angled triangles, use the sine rule, cosine rule or area rule. Label sides with lowercase letters opposite their matching angles.

Sine rule

\[\frac a{\sin A}=\frac b{\sin B}=\frac c{\sin C}\]

Use when you know an opposite side-angle pair and another side or angle.

Example: A=30°, B=60° and a=8 cm. Then \(b=8\sin60°/\sin30°=\boxed{13.86\text{ cm}}\) (2 d.p.).

Cosine rule

\[a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\]

Use when two sides and the included angle are known, or all three sides are known and an angle is required.

Example: b=5, c=7 and A=60°. \(a^2=25+49-70(0.5)=39\), so \(a=\boxed{6.24}\) (2 d.p.).

Area rule

\[\text{Area}=\frac12 ab\sin C\]

a and b are the two sides enclosing angle C. Ensure the angle is between the two given sides.

Choosing a rule: Use sine rule for an opposite side-angle pair; cosine rule for SAS or SSS; area rule when two sides and included angle are known and area is required.

Practice

  1. Find c if C=90°, A=30° and a=6 cm.
  2. Find area when sides are 8 cm and 5 cm with included angle 30°.
Answers: 12 cm; 10 cm².

11. Linear Programming

Linear programming finds the maximum or minimum value of a linear objective function subject to linear constraints.

Formulating constraints

Define variables clearly. Translate limits into inequalities. For example, if x and y are quantities and total capacity is at most 40, write \(x+y\le40\). Include non-negativity constraints \(x\ge0,y\ge0\) when quantities cannot be negative.

Feasible region

Draw each boundary line accurately. Use a solid boundary for ≤ or ≥ and a dashed line for strict inequalities. Test a point to determine the correct side. The feasible region is the common overlap.

Vertices and objective function

Find coordinates of every corner (vertex), including intersections of boundary lines. Substitute each vertex into the objective function, such as \(P=5x+3y\). Compare the values to identify the maximum or minimum.

Example: For feasible vertices (0,0), (4,0), (0,3), maximise \(P=2x+3y\). Values are 0, 8 and 9. The maximum is \(\boxed9\) at (0,3).
Do not: Choose a point that merely looks best on the graph. Evaluate the objective function at every feasible vertex.

Practice

  1. Evaluate P=4x+2y at (3,2) and (1,5).
  2. Which gives the maximum?
Answers: 16 and 14; maximum is 16 at (3,2).

12. Mensuration: Three-Dimensional Shapes

Surface area is the total area covering the outside of a solid. Volume is the space occupied. Surface area is measured in square units and volume in cubic units.

Common surface-area formulae

  • Cube: total surface area \(6a^2\); volume \(a^3\).
  • Cuboid: surface area \(2(lb+lh+bh)\); volume \(lbh\).
  • Prism: total surface area = 2 × area of cross-section + perimeter of cross-section × length.
  • Cylinder: curved area \(2\pi rh\); total area \(2\pi r(h+r)\); volume \(\pi r^2h\).
  • Cone: curved area \(\pi rl\); total area \(\pi r(l+r)\); volume \(\frac13\pi r^2h\).
  • Sphere: surface area \(4\pi r^2\); volume \(\frac43\pi r^3\).
  • Pyramid: volume \(\frac13\times\text{base area}\times h\).

Worked examples

Cuboid: l=5 cm, b=3 cm, h=2 cm. Volume = 5×3×2 = 30 cm³. Surface area = 2(15+10+6) = 62 cm².
Cylinder: r=3 cm, h=7 cm. Volume = \(\pi(3)^2(7)=63\pi\) cm³.

Composite solids and hidden faces

Split a composite solid into familiar shapes. Add volumes of joined parts; subtract cut-out parts. For exposed surface area, count only faces that can be seen from outside. Do not include faces where two solids are joined.

Angles and lengths in solids

Draw a suitable right-angled triangle on the solid, identify the perpendicular height, slant height and base radius/length, then apply Pythagoras or trigonometry. A slant height is not automatically the vertical height.

Units: Convert all lengths to the same unit before calculating. Include cm², m², cm³ or m³ as appropriate.

Practice

  1. Find volume of a cube of side 4 cm.
  2. Find total surface area of a sphere of radius 3 cm.
  3. Find volume of a cone with radius 3 cm and perpendicular height 4 cm.
Answers: 64 cm³; 36π cm²; 12π cm³.

13. Graphs of Cubic Functions

A cubic function has the form \(y=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\), where a is non-zero. Its graph is usually a smooth S-shaped curve, though its exact shape depends on the coefficients.

Drawing a cubic graph

  1. Choose a suitable range of x-values.
  2. Calculate y-values carefully in a table.
  3. Plot the points and draw a smooth curve.
  4. Label axes, scale, intercepts and important features.

Roots and solving cubic equations graphically

The roots are x-values where the curve meets the x-axis. To solve \(x^3-2x-3=0\) graphically, draw \(y=x^3\) and \(y=2x+3\) on the same axes; the x-coordinate of their intersection is the solution.

Simultaneous linear and cubic equations

Draw both graphs on the same axes. The coordinates of intersection satisfy both equations. The number of solutions is the number of intersection points.

Example: To solve \(x^3=2x+3\), plot the cubic \(y=x^3\) and straight line \(y=2x+3\). Their intersection gives an approximate root; check it by substitution if a numerical estimate is available.
Graph accuracy: Use enough x-values near turning points and intersections. Read the graph to the requested accuracy and state that graphical answers are approximate.

Practice

  1. For \(y=x^3\), find y when x=−2, −1, 0, 1, 2.
  2. What does an intersection of a cubic and a straight line represent?
Answers: −8, −1, 0, 1, 8; a simultaneous solution satisfying both equations.
How to prepare for the examination
  1. Memorise formulas with the meaning and units of each symbol.
  2. Practise showing every algebraic and numerical step.
  3. Draw clear graphs and diagrams with labelled axes and points.
  4. Check whether answers are exact or approximate and round only as requested.
