1. Arithmetic Sequence

An arithmetic sequence is a list of numbers in which the difference between consecutive terms is constant. This fixed difference is called the common difference, d. The first term is a.

Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d

To use the formula, identify the first term, subtract consecutive terms to find d, then substitute the required term number n. A negative common difference means the sequence decreases.

Example: 12, 9, 6, … has a = 12 and d = −3. T₁₇ = 12 + 16(−3) = −36.

Finding an unknown term or the number of terms

The general term is Tₙ=a+(n−1)d. If a term is known, substitute its term number and value to form an equation in d. Once d is known, substitute it back to find another term or solve for n.

Source example: The seventh term is 80 and the first term is 98. 80=98+6d, so 6d=−18 and d=−3. The fourth term is 98+3(−3)=89.

Check that consecutive terms really have the same difference before using the arithmetic-sequence formula. A sequence such as 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 is not arithmetic because its differences are not constant.

Practice: Find the 20th term of 7, 11, 15, …

2. Quadratic Expressions and Equations

A quadratic expression has the form ax² + bx + c, where a ≠ 0. Factorising means writing it as a product of simpler factors. For x² + bx + c, find two numbers whose product is c and sum is b. For ax² + bx + c, split the middle term into two terms and factor by grouping.

The difference of two squares follows a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b). A quadratic equation is solved by setting the expression equal to zero, factorising and using the zero-product rule: if AB = 0, then A = 0 or B = 0.

Example: x² + 5x + 6 = 0 → (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0 → x = −2 or x = −3.

Factorisation by grouping

For four terms, group them in pairs so that each pair has a common factor and the same bracket remains. Then take that common bracket as a factor.

Example: 3ab+2b+3ac+2c = b(3a+2)+c(3a+2) = (3a+2)(b+c).

Quadratic equations and word problems

For ax²+bx+c=0, factorise and use the zero-product rule. In a word problem, define the unknown and form the quadratic equation from the given relationship. Reject a negative length or other value that cannot make sense in the context.

Source-style rectangle: If the width is x metres and the length is x+4 metres and area is 12 m², x(x+4)=12. Thus x²+4x−12=0=(x+6)(x−2). The positive width is 2 m and the length is 6 m.
Practice: Factorise x² − 7x + 12 and solve it when equal to zero.

3. Pythagoras Theorem

In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (the side opposite the 90° angle) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

a² + b² = c²

To find the hypotenuse, add the squares of the shorter sides and take the square root. To find a shorter side, subtract the square of the known shorter side from the square of the hypotenuse, then take the square root. Always identify the hypotenuse first.

Example: If the shorter sides are 6 cm and 8 cm, c² = 36 + 64 = 100, so c = 10 cm.
Practice: A right triangle has hypotenuse 13 cm and one shorter side 5 cm. Find the other side.

4. Congruency in Triangles

Congruent triangles have exactly the same shape and size. Their corresponding sides and angles are equal. The order in which vertices are written matters because it shows which vertices correspond.

  • SSS: all three corresponding sides are equal.
  • SAS: two corresponding sides and the included angle are equal.
  • AAS/ASA: two corresponding angles and a corresponding side are equal.
  • RHS: for right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one corresponding side are equal.

In a proof, state each equality and its reason (given information, a property of a shape or a common side), then name the congruency test.

Congruency tests used in the source

SAS: two sides and their included angle are equal. AAS: two angles and a corresponding side are equal. RHS: in right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one corresponding side are equal. SSS: all three corresponding sides are equal.

When writing a proof, list the equal sides and angles with their reasons before naming the test. Corresponding vertices must be written in matching order. Congruency proves exact equality in size and shape; similarity, by contrast, permits different sizes.

Practice: Two right triangles have equal hypotenuses and one equal leg. Which test proves them congruent?

5. Plane Shapes

A quadrilateral is a four-sided polygon and its interior angles add to 360°. Know the properties of common quadrilaterals:

  • Parallelogram: opposite sides parallel and equal; opposite angles equal; diagonals bisect each other.
  • Rectangle: all angles 90°; diagonals equal and bisect each other.
  • Rhombus: all sides equal; diagonals are perpendicular and bisect opposite angles.
  • Square: all sides equal and all angles 90°; it has properties of both rectangle and rhombus.
  • Kite: two pairs of adjacent equal sides; diagonals are perpendicular.
  • Trapezium: one pair of opposite sides is parallel.

Quadrilateral theorems and proof strategy

The source develops these results: a diagonal of a parallelogram divides it into two congruent triangles; opposite sides and opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal; its diagonals bisect each other. Conversely, if both pairs of opposite sides are equal, if both pairs of opposite angles are equal, or if the diagonals bisect each other, the quadrilateral is a parallelogram. A quadrilateral is also a parallelogram if one pair of opposite sides is both equal and parallel.

For a proof, draw a diagonal where useful, identify alternate angles from parallel lines, identify a common side, apply a congruency test and then state the corresponding equal sides or angles.

Remember the special properties: rectangle diagonals are equal; rhombus diagonals are perpendicular and bisect opposite angles; square diagonals have both sets of properties. A kite has perpendicular diagonals and one diagonal bisects the other.

Practice: State three properties shared by a square and a rhombus.

6. Mensuration

Mensuration deals with lengths, areas, surface areas and volumes. A solid is a three-dimensional object. Common solids include cube, cuboid, prism, pyramid, cylinder, cone, frustum and sphere. Use consistent units: area is in square units and volume in cubic units.

Important formulae

  • Rectangle area = length × width.
  • Triangle area = ½ × base × perpendicular height.
  • Circle area = πr²; circumference = 2πr.
  • Cuboid volume = lwh; cube volume = a³.
  • Cylinder volume = πr²h.
  • Pyramid volume = ⅓ × base area × perpendicular height.
  • Cylinder curved surface area = 2πrh; total surface area = 2πr² + 2πrh.
Good practice: Write the formula first, substitute values with units, calculate and state the final unit.

Solids and surface-area methods in the source

Know the named solids: cuboid, cube, pyramid, tetrahedron, prism, cone, frustum, cylinder, sphere and hemisphere. A prism has identical parallel end faces; it is named by the shape of its cross-section. A pyramid has triangular sloping faces meeting at an apex above a polygonal base.

For a cylinder, curved surface area is 2πrh. A closed cylinder has total surface area 2πr²+2πrh; an open cylinder with one end open has πr²+2πrh. Volume is πr²h. For a pyramid, volume is one-third × base area × perpendicular height. Surface area is base area plus the areas of all triangular side faces.

Worked cylinder example: radius 4 cm and height 10 cm gives V=π×4²×10=160π≈502.7 cm³. Volume must be in cubic units, not square units.
Practice: Find the volume of a cylinder of radius 7 cm and height 10 cm. Use π = 22/7.

Three-dimensional solids

Names of common solids

A tetrahedron is a triangular pyramid with four triangular faces. A prism has matching parallel ends. A frustum is the portion of a solid left after cutting it by a plane parallel to its base. A sphere has a curved surface and no flat faces.

Circle formulae used in mensuration

Area of a circle = πr². Circumference = 2πr = πd. Radius is half the diameter. Keep square units for area and cubic units for volume.

7. Simultaneous Equations

Simultaneous equations are two or more equations involving the same unknowns. Their solution is the set of values that satisfies every equation. The elimination method removes one variable by adding or subtracting equations. The substitution method makes one variable the subject and substitutes it into the other equation. A graphical solution is the intersection of two straight lines.

Example: x + y = 10 and x − y = 2. Add the equations: 2x = 12, so x = 6. Substitute: y = 4.

For word problems, define the unknowns, form two equations from the information and solve using a method that keeps the working clear.

Choosing the simultaneous-equation method

Elimination: multiply one or both equations to make a variable's coefficients equal, then add or subtract to remove it. Substitution: make one variable the subject in the simpler equation and substitute into the other. Graphical: draw both lines; the coordinates of their intersection solve both equations.

Source application: Let x be the cost of one skirt and y the cost of one blouse. 2x+3y=600 and x+2y=350. Doubling the second equation gives 2x+4y=700. Subtract the first: y=100. Then x+200=350, so x=150. A skirt costs K150 and a blouse K100.
Practice: Solve 2x + y = 11 and x + y = 7.

Graphical simultaneous equations

Make a table of values for each equation, plot both straight lines on the same axes and read the coordinates of their intersection. The intersection satisfies both equations at the same time.

The source example graphs y=5−2x and y=x−4; their intersection is (3,−1), so x=3 and y=−1.

Word problems

For age problems, write each person's age at the stated time before forming equations. For two-digit number problems, represent the number as 10x+y and the reversed number as 10y+x.

8. Rates, Ratio, Proportion and Percentages

A rate compares quantities with different units, such as kilometres per hour or cost per kilogram. A ratio compares quantities of the same kind. Simplify a ratio by dividing all parts by their highest common factor. To share a quantity in a ratio, add the ratio parts, divide the total by that sum, then multiply by each part.

Two ratios are in proportion if they are equal. Direct proportion means both quantities change in the same ratio; inverse proportion means one increases as the other decreases so that their product remains constant. Percentage means “out of 100”.

Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100%

Example: Share K600 in the ratio 2:3. Total parts = 5; one part = K120. Shares are K240 and K360.

Ratio changes and proportional reasoning

When a quantity changes in the ratio new:old, multiply the old value by new/old. To compare two ratios, express them as fractions with a common denominator. To share a total in a:b:c, add the parts and give each share its fraction of the total.

Source example: Increase 20 in the ratio 5:4: new value = 20×5/4=25. Decrease 45 in the ratio 7:9: new value=45×7/9=35.

Direct proportion: both quantities change in the same ratio. Inverse proportion: when one quantity increases, the other decreases so their product remains constant. For a fixed journey, speed and time are inversely proportional.

Sharing example: Divide 72 hectares in the ratio 2:3:4. There are 9 parts, so the shares are 16 ha, 24 ha and 32 ha.
Practice: Increase K800 by 15%. Divide 84 in the ratio 3:4.

Direct and inverse proportion

In direct proportion, the ratio of corresponding values remains constant. If one quantity doubles, the other doubles. In inverse proportion, the product of corresponding values remains constant; if one quantity doubles, the other halves.

Percentages

To convert a fraction to a percentage, multiply by 100%. To convert a decimal to a percentage, multiply by 100%. Percentage change = (change ÷ original amount)×100%.

9. Density and Mixtures

Density is mass per unit volume. It tells how closely packed the matter is. Use compatible units, such as g/cm³ or kg/m³. Relative density compares the density of a substance with the density of water and has no unit.

Density = Mass ÷ Volume

Mass = Density × Volume

Volume = Mass ÷ Density

A mixture contains two or more substances physically combined. For mixture-cost or mixture-density problems, calculate the total mass and total volume (or total cost and total quantity) before finding the overall value. Do not simply average values unless the amounts are equal.

Example: A substance has mass 200 g and volume 50 cm³. Density = 200/50 = 4 g/cm³.

Density units and relative density

Density is mass per unit volume. If mass is in grams and volume in cm³, density is g/cm³. If mass is in kilograms and volume in m³, density is kg/m³. To convert g/cm³ to kg/m³, multiply by 1,000; to convert back, divide by 1,000. Relative density compares a substance's density with water and has no unit.

Source example: A tank has volume 1,800 cm³ and contains fuel of density 0.7 g/cm³. Mass = density×volume = 0.7×1,800 = 1,260 g.

For a mixture, calculate total mass and total volume, then divide total mass by total volume. Do not take an unweighted average of the component densities unless the volumes or masses make that valid.

Practice: Find the mass of 300 cm³ of a liquid with density 0.8 g/cm³.

Relative density and mixtures

Relative density = density of substance ÷ density of water at the same conditions. It is a ratio and has no unit. For a mixture, add the masses and add the volumes, then divide total mass by total volume to obtain the mixture density.

For example, if 100 cm³ of a liquid has mass 80 g, its density is 0.8 g/cm³. For two liquids, use total mass divided by total volume rather than simply averaging their densities.

Mixtures and ratio of costs

For a mixture made from two commodities, use the total cost divided by total quantity. The quantities must be in the ratio that makes the weighted average equal to the mixture price.

Source example: White beans cost K146/kg and red beans K170/kg. To obtain a mixture costing K152/kg, compare the differences: 170−152=18 and 152−146=6. Therefore white beans:red beans = 18:6 = 3:1.

10. Indices and Logarithms

An index (power) tells how many times a number is multiplied by itself. For non-zero a, the main laws are:

  • aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
  • aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
  • (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
  • a⁰ = 1
  • a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ
  • a¹/ⁿ = ⁿ√a

A logarithm is the inverse of a power. logₐN = x means aˣ = N. The base must be positive and not equal to 1, and N must be positive.

Example: 2³ = 8, therefore log₂8 = 3. Also, 10² = 100, so log₁₀100 = 2.

Negative and fractional indices

For a non-zero base x, x⁰=1, xᵃ×xᵇ=xᵃ⁺ᵇ, xᵃ÷xᵇ=xᵃ⁻ᵇ, (xᵃ)ᵇ=xᵃᵇ, x⁻ᵃ=1/xᵃ and xᵃ/ᵇ=ᵇ√(xᵃ), where the real root is defined.

Common logarithms and log tables

logₐx=y means aʸ=x. Common logarithms use base 10. The antilogarithm reverses a logarithm. To multiply numbers using logs, add their logarithms; to divide, subtract; to raise a number to a power, multiply its logarithm by the power; to find a root, divide the logarithm by the root index.

Example: log₁₀1000=3 because 10³=1000. For a number such as 456, write it in standard form 4.56×10² before using a log table; the characteristic determines the power of ten and the mantissa gives the significant digits.
Practice: Simplify 3² × 3⁴ and evaluate log₁₀1000.

Index laws summary

Using common logarithms

A logarithm is the power to which a base must be raised to give a number. For common logarithms the base is 10. For example, log₁₀100=2 because 10²=100. In a log table, the characteristic gives the power of ten and the mantissa gives the decimal part of the logarithm.

Logarithm operations: log(ab)=log a+log b; log(a/b)=log a−log b; log(aⁿ)=n log a. The antilogarithm reverses the logarithm operation.

11. Similarity I

Similar shapes have the same shape but may have different sizes. Corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are in the same ratio. The scale factor is the ratio of a length in the image to its corresponding length in the original.

If the scale factor is k, corresponding lengths multiply by k, areas by k² and volumes by k³. Match corresponding vertices and sides before forming ratios.

Similarity of solids and triangles

The source begins with similar solids: corresponding angles are equal and corresponding lengths are in a constant ratio. If the linear scale factor is k, areas scale by k² and volumes by k³. Similar triangles have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding sides.

Source example: A jewel box is 30 cm long and similar to a 5 cm matchbox. The scale factor is 30/5=6. If the matchbox is 3.5 cm wide and 1.5 cm high, the jewel box is 21 cm wide and 9 cm high.

When solving a similar-triangle problem, mark corresponding angles first, match the corresponding sides and form a proportion. Do not compare sides that do not correspond.

Practice: A side of 4 cm corresponds to 10 cm. Find the scale factor and the image length corresponding to 7 cm.

12. Inequalities

An inequality compares values using <, >, ≤ or ≥. Solve a linear inequality in a similar way to an equation, but remember that multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the inequality sign.

Example: −2x > 8. Divide by −2 and reverse the sign: x < −4.

Compound inequalities combine two conditions, for example 2 < x ≤ 7. On a number line, use an open circle for a strict endpoint (< or >) and a filled circle for an included endpoint (≤ or ≥).

Compound inequalities and word statements

A compound statement joins two simple inequalities with “and” or “or”. “And” means values must satisfy both conditions; “or” means values may satisfy either condition. An open circle on a number line excludes the endpoint; a filled circle includes it.

Source-style word problem: A student spends (8y+40) kwacha on biscuits and 2y kwacha on sweets. If the total is less than K80, write (8y+40)+2y<80. Then 10y+40<80, so y<4.

Adding or subtracting the same number on both sides keeps the inequality direction. Multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the sign.

Practice: Solve 3x + 2 ≤ 14 and −5x ≥ 20.

13. Distance-Time Graphs

Distance is how far an object travels. Speed is distance travelled per unit time. On a distance-time graph, time is normally on the horizontal axis and distance on the vertical axis. A straight sloping line shows constant speed; a horizontal line shows the object is stationary; a steeper line represents greater speed.

Speed = Distance ÷ Time

The speed is the gradient of a distance-time graph: change in distance divided by change in time. Use two points on the straight section and keep units consistent.

Journey problems and interpreting graph sections

Use distance = speed×time. Convert minutes to hours before using km/h: 45 minutes = 0.75 hours. When two travellers move towards each other, their closing speed is the sum of their speeds. When one catches another moving in the same direction, use the difference in their speeds.

Source example: Two trains start 500 km apart and travel towards each other at 48 km/h and 52 km/h. In 45 minutes they travel 36 km and 39 km, leaving 500−36−39=425 km between them. Their combined speed is 100 km/h, so they meet after 500/100=5 hours, 240 km from the first town.

On a distance–time graph, a horizontal section means stopped, a rising section means moving away from the starting point and a falling section means returning towards it. The gradient gives speed; use the distance change divided by the time change.

Practice: A car travels 150 km in 3 hours at constant speed. Find its speed.

14. Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is. It ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Theoretical probability assumes outcomes are equally likely; experimental probability is based on results observed in trials.

P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total possible outcomes

The probability of an event not happening is 1 − P(event). For mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

Example: A fair die has three odd faces (1, 3, 5). P(odd) = 3/6 = 1/2.

Experimental probability and event language

In an experiment, each performance is a trial and the observed result is an outcome. Experimental probability is the number of times an outcome occurs divided by the number of trials. Theoretical probability is calculated from equally likely possible outcomes without carrying out the experiment.

For mutually exclusive outcomes, “A or B” means add their probabilities. For independent events, “A and B” means multiply. The probability of an event and its complement adds to 1. A fair coin has P(head)=P(tail)=1/2; a fair die has six equally likely outcomes.

Source-style example: A basket contains 5 red, 4 green and 3 blue balls. There are 12 balls. P(blue)=3/12=1/4 and P(not red)=(4+3)/12=7/12.
Practice: A bag has 4 red and 6 blue balls. Find the probability of selecting a red ball and of not selecting a red ball.

Probability scale

Experimental probability display

Record the number of trials and the frequency of each outcome in a tally or frequency table. Experimental probability is relative frequency and may change as more trials are performed. Theoretical probability assumes equally likely outcomes.

15. Geometric Constructions II

An inscribed circle (incircle) lies inside a triangle and touches all three sides. Its centre, the incentre, is where the angle bisectors meet. To construct it, bisect two angles, mark their intersection, draw a perpendicular from that point to a side to obtain the radius, then draw the circle.

A circumscribed circle passes through all three vertices of a triangle. Its centre, the circumcentre, is where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet. Use the distance from the circumcentre to any vertex as the radius.

Remember: Angle bisectors locate the incentre; perpendicular bisectors locate the circumcentre. Do not confuse the two constructions.

Detailed construction procedures from the source

Incircle: construct the triangle using the given side lengths. Bisect two or all three interior angles; the bisectors meet at the incentre I. Draw a perpendicular from I to a side to obtain radius IM. With centre I and radius IM, draw the circle. It touches all three sides.

Circumcircle: construct the triangle. Draw the perpendicular bisectors of its three sides; they meet at circumcentre O. Set the compass to OA (or OB or OC) and draw the circle. It passes through all three vertices.

Source construction tasks: Form 2 asks for an incircle in a triangle with sides 9 cm, 11 cm and 7 cm, and a circumcircle around an equilateral triangle with sides 5 cm. Leave construction arcs visible and label the centre and radius.

Practice: Construct a triangle with sides 6 cm, 7 cm and 8 cm, then construct its incircle and circumcircle.
