1. Introduction

Probability is the study of how likely an event is to occur. It is used when dealing with uncertainty, chance and random experiments.

Learning objectives

  • Understand probability and sample spaces.
  • Calculate probabilities of simple events.
  • Use complementary events.
  • Understand mutually exclusive events.
  • Use probability addition rules.
  • Use tree diagrams and tables.
  • Understand independent events.
  • Solve examination problems involving probability.

2. Probability Scale

Probability lies between 0 and 1.

\[ 0\leq P(A)\leq1 \]
Probability Meaning
0 Impossible
Between 0 and 1 Possible
1 Certain

Probability can also be expressed as a fraction, decimal or percentage.

3. Sample Space

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.

When a fair die is rolled: \[ S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\} \] Therefore, there are 6 possible outcomes.
When a coin is tossed: \[ S=\{H,T\} \] where \(H\) represents heads and \(T\) represents tails.

4. Simple Probability

When all outcomes are equally likely:

\[ P(A)= \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}} {\text{total number of possible outcomes}} \]
A fair die is rolled. Find the probability of obtaining a 4. There is one favourable outcome and six possible outcomes. \[ P(4)=\frac16 \] \[ \boxed{\frac16} \]
Find the probability of obtaining an even number when a fair die is rolled. Even numbers are: \[ 2,4,6 \] Therefore: \[ P(\text{even})=\frac36 \] \[ \boxed{\frac12} \]

5. Complementary Events

The complement of event \(A\) means that \(A\) does not occur.

\[ P(A')=1-P(A) \]
If the probability that a student passes an examination is \(0.8\), find the probability that the student does not pass. \[ P(\text{not pass})=1-0.8 \] \[ \boxed{0.2} \]

6. Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time.

When a die is rolled, obtaining an odd number and obtaining an even number are mutually exclusive events.

For mutually exclusive events:

\[ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B) \]

7. Addition Rule

For general events:

\[ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) \]

The subtraction prevents the overlap from being counted twice.

Suppose: \[ P(A)=0.5,\qquad P(B)=0.4,\qquad P(A\cap B)=0.2 \] Then: \[ P(A\cup B)=0.5+0.4-0.2 \] \[ \boxed{0.7} \]

8. Independent Events

Two events are independent when the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B) \]
A fair coin is tossed twice. Find the probability of getting two heads. \[ P(H)=\frac12 \] Therefore: \[ P(H\text{ and }H) = \frac12\times\frac12 \] \[ \boxed{\frac14} \]

9. Tree Diagrams

Tree diagrams show possible sequences of outcomes.

Multiply probabilities along a branch. Add probabilities of separate branches when finding the probability of alternative outcomes.

10. Probability from Tables

Probability can be obtained from frequency tables.

A class contains 30 students. 18 are girls and 12 are boys. The probability of selecting a girl at random is: \[ P(\text{girl})=\frac{18}{30} \] \[ \boxed{\frac35} \]

11. Experimental Probability

Experimental probability is based on results obtained from an actual experiment.

\[ P(A)= \frac{\text{number of times A occurs}} {\text{number of trials}} \]
A coin is tossed 100 times and heads occurs 56 times. \[ P(H)=\frac{56}{100} \] \[ \boxed{0.56} \]

Experimental probability may differ from theoretical probability because of random variation.

12. Expected Frequency

Expected frequency estimates how many times an event should occur.

\[ \text{Expected frequency} = \text{number of trials}\times P(A) \]
A die is rolled 120 times. Find the expected number of sixes. \[ 120\times\frac16 \] \[ \boxed{20} \]

13. MANEB-style Practice

  1. A fair die is rolled. Find the probability of obtaining a number greater than 4.
  2. A bag contains 5 red balls and 3 blue balls. Find the probability of selecting a blue ball.
  3. If \(P(A)=0.35\), find \(P(A')\).
  4. A coin is tossed twice. Find the probability of obtaining exactly one head.
  5. Two independent events have probabilities \(\frac23\) and \(\frac34\). Find the probability that both occur.
  6. A die is rolled 180 times. Find the expected number of sixes.
  7. A class contains 25 boys and 15 girls. Find the probability of selecting a girl at random.
  8. Explain the difference between mutually exclusive and independent events.

Answers

  1. \[ \frac26=\boxed{\frac13} \]
  2. \[ \frac38 \]
  3. \[ 1-0.35=\boxed{0.65} \]
  4. \[ \frac14+\frac14=\boxed{\frac12} \]
  5. \[ \frac23\times\frac34=\boxed{\frac12} \]
  6. \[ 180\times\frac16=\boxed{30} \]
  7. \[ \frac{15}{40}=\boxed{\frac38} \]
  8. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together, while independent events do not affect each other's probabilities.

14. Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting that probability must lie between 0 and 1.
  • Counting the sample space incorrectly.
  • Adding probabilities when multiplication is required.
  • Multiplying probabilities when alternatives should be added.
  • Confusing mutually exclusive and independent events.
  • Forgetting to subtract the overlap in the general addition rule.
  • Failing to simplify the final fraction.
Examination tip: For probability questions, clearly identify the possible outcomes first. For tree diagrams, multiply along branches and add separate successful branches.
