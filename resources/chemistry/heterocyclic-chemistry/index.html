1. Introduction to heterocyclic chemistry

Heterocyclic chemistry studies cyclic organic molecules in which at least one atom in a ring is not carbon. Such a non-carbon ring atom is called a heteroatom. Nitrogen (N), oxygen (O) and sulfur (S) are especially common, although phosphorus, boron, silicon and selenium can also occur.

A ring is described as saturated when it contains no carbon–carbon or carbon–heteroatom multiple bond in the ring framework, and unsaturated when one or more multiple bonds are present. Some unsaturated heterocycles are aromatic: their π electrons are delocalised around a planar cyclic framework.

Quick distinction: cyclohexane is a carbocycle because every ring atom is carbon. Piperidine is a heterocycle because one ring carbon is replaced by nitrogen. Pyridine is also a nitrogen heterocycle, but it is unsaturated and aromatic.
Figure 1.1. A conceptual comparison of a carbocycle and two nitrogen heterocycles. The labels, rather than the schematic bond placement, identify each example.

Why these compounds matter

  • Medicines: many drugs contain heterocyclic rings. Examples include quinine, chloroquine, isoniazid and indomethacin.
  • Genetic material: adenine, guanine, cytosine and thymine contain nitrogen heterocycles and form the bases of DNA; uracil occurs in RNA.
  • Biochemistry: heterocyclic units occur in coenzymes such as NAD⁺/NADH and NADP⁺/NADPH and in molecules involved in electron transfer.
  • Agriculture and materials: heterocyclic compounds are used in crop-protection chemicals, dyes, polymers and functional materials.
Figure 1.2. Purine and pyrimidine are the parent fused and single-ring frameworks from which the nucleobases are derived; this is a family-level sketch, not a full atom-by-atom structure.

2. Naming heterocyclic compounds

Three naming approaches are commonly encountered: traditional (trivial) names, replacement nomenclature and systematic Hantzsch–Widman nomenclature. Traditional names are often short and widely recognised, but they may not reveal the full structure. Systematic methods encode information about ring size, heteroatoms and unsaturation.

Figure 2.1. Parent structures of furan, thiophene and pyrrole. Each ring contains four carbon atoms and one heteroatom.

2.2 Numbering rules

  1. Begin numbering at a heteroatom. When different heteroatom types occur, the usual priority sequence is O before S before N.
  2. Choose the direction that gives the other heteroatoms the lowest possible set of locants.
  3. When equivalent choices remain, apply the relevant systematic nomenclature rules and give substituents the lowest possible positions.
  4. Identify substituent positions with numbers and list prefixes alphabetically (ignoring multiplicative prefixes such as di- when alphabetising).
  5. Use indicated-hydrogen and hydrogenation prefixes where needed to distinguish different unsaturation patterns, for example 1,2-dihydropyridine.
Figure 2.2. Schematic numbering examples. In imidazole, the two nitrogens occupy positions 1 and 3; in isoxazole, oxygen is position 1 and nitrogen is position 2.

2.3 Replacement nomenclature

In replacement nomenclature, a heteroatom is indicated by a prefix that tells the reader which carbon atom in a parent ring framework has been replaced. Common replacement prefixes include oxa for oxygen, thia for sulfur and aza for nitrogen. The locant identifies the position of the replacement atom. This approach is useful for complex or saturated ring systems.

Worked naming approach: To name a substituted five-membered heterocycle, first identify ring size, then locate and prioritise the heteroatoms, number the ring, identify each substituent and finally check the indicated hydrogen/unsaturation pattern. Do not guess a name from the appearance alone.

3. Aromaticity and electron counting

Aromaticity is a special stabilisation associated with cyclic delocalisation of π electrons. It is not enough for a molecule to contain alternating double bonds. The ring must satisfy a set of structural and electronic requirements.

Conditions for aromaticity

  1. The system must be cyclic.
  2. It must be approximately planar so the orbitals can overlap effectively.
  3. Every atom in the conjugated ring must have a p orbital, giving continuous cyclic conjugation.
  4. The delocalised system must contain (4n + 2) π electrons, where n is 0, 1, 2, ... (Hückel's rule).
Figure 3.1. Aromaticity requires both a suitable orbital framework and the correct π-electron count; the ring sketches are simplified.

Counting π electrons in heterocycles

Each ring double bond contributes two π electrons. A heteroatom lone pair contributes only when it occupies a p orbital and participates in the cyclic conjugated system. In pyridine, nitrogen contributes one electron from its p orbital, while its lone pair lies in an in-plane sp² orbital and does not join the aromatic sextet. In pyrrole, the nitrogen lone pair occupies a p orbital and supplies two electrons to the aromatic system.

Ringπ-electron contributionWhy?
Pyridine6 π electronsThree π bonds contribute six; the N lone pair is outside the π system.
Pyrrole6 π electronsTwo π bonds contribute four; one N lone pair contributes two.
Furan6 π electronsTwo π bonds contribute four; one oxygen lone pair contributes two. The second O lone pair remains in-plane.
Thiophene6 π electronsTwo π bonds and one sulfur lone pair form the aromatic sextet.
Common examination error: do not count both lone pairs on oxygen or sulfur in furan/thiophene. Only one lone pair enters the π system; the other remains in an orbital in the plane of the ring.

4. Pyridine: structure, electron distribution and basicity

Pyridine is a six-membered aromatic ring in which one CH unit of benzene is replaced by a nitrogen atom. Its formula is C₅H₅N. All six ring atoms are approximately sp² hybridised. Each atom supplies one electron to the delocalised π system, giving six π electrons.

Figure 4.1. Pyridine positions relative to ring nitrogen: α = 2/6, β = 3/5 and γ = 4.

Why pyridine is basic

The nitrogen atom has a lone pair in an sp² orbital. This pair is not required to maintain the aromatic sextet, so it can accept a proton and form pyridinium ion without destroying the ring's aromatic π system. Pyridine is therefore a weak base, but a stronger base than pyrrole.

C₅H₅N + H⁺ ⇌ C₅H₆N⁺   (pyridinium ion)

The conjugate-acid pKₐ of pyridinium is about 5.2. Piperidinium has a pKₐ near 11.2, reflecting the much greater basicity of the saturated amine piperidine. The lone pair in an sp³ amine is generally more available for protonation than the lone pair in pyridine's sp² orbital.

Comparing pyridine, pyrrole and alkylamines

CompoundWhere is the N lone pair?Basicity trendReason
Alkylamine / piperidineLocalised on sp³ nitrogenStrongest of these examplesLone pair is relatively available to bind H⁺.
PyridineLocalised in an sp² orbital outside the π sextetModerate/weak baseCan accept H⁺ while retaining aromaticity, but the sp² orbital holds the pair relatively tightly.
PyrroleContributes to aromatic π sextetVery weak baseProtonation at nitrogen removes the lone pair from the aromatic sextet and is unfavourable.
How to explain pyridine basicity in an exam:
  1. Identify the nitrogen lone pair.
  2. Show that it occupies an sp² orbital in the plane of the ring.
  3. State that it is not part of the six π electrons.
  4. Show protonation to pyridinium and note that the aromatic π sextet remains intact.

Imidazole as a base

Imidazole contains two chemically different nitrogens: one pyridine-like nitrogen whose lone pair is available for protonation, and one pyrrole-like N–H whose lone pair contributes to aromaticity. Protonation gives an imidazolium ion whose positive charge is stabilised by resonance over the ring. This stabilisation makes imidazole more basic than pyridine in water (conjugate-acid pKₐ approximately 7).

5. Electrophilic aromatic substitution in pyridine

Pyridine is less reactive than benzene towards electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS). Nitrogen withdraws electron density by both inductive and resonance effects, making the ring electron-poor. Strongly acidic reaction conditions can also protonate nitrogen to form pyridinium, which is even less reactive towards electrophiles.

Why substitution is mainly at the β position

For attack at C-2 (α) or C-4 (γ), important resonance contributors of the σ-complex place positive charge on nitrogen. Such contributors are highly destabilised. Attack at C-3 (β) avoids this particularly unfavourable contributor, so β substitution is favoured when electrophilic substitution can be achieved.

Figure 5.1. The β positions (C-3 and C-5) are favoured for direct electrophilic substitution on pyridine.

Examples and practical implications

  • Nitration: harsh conditions are needed and the yield of 3-nitropyridine is low. This illustrates the strong deactivation of the ring.
  • Sulfonation and halogenation: also require forcing conditions compared with benzene.
  • N-substitution: pyridine can react at nitrogen to give pyridinium salts. This is distinct from substitution on a ring carbon.
  • Activation by substituents: electron-donating groups can increase ring reactivity and influence the position of incoming electrophiles. Steric effects also matter.
Do not write that pyridine never undergoes EAS. It does, but it is strongly deactivated and usually requires more forcing conditions than benzene. When it occurs on the parent ring, β substitution is generally favoured.

6. Nucleophilic substitution in pyridine

The ring nitrogen withdraws electron density and can stabilise some anionic intermediates formed when a nucleophile attacks the ring. Consequently, pyridine is more receptive to nucleophilic aromatic substitution (NAS) than to EAS, especially when a suitable leaving group is present at C-2 or C-4.

Addition–elimination pathway

Figure 6.1. General addition–elimination sequence. Ar represents the aromatic heterocyclic ring, X is the leaving group and Nu is the incoming nucleophile.
  1. The nucleophile attacks an electron-poor ring carbon bearing a suitable leaving group.
  2. A non-aromatic σ-adduct forms; negative charge is delocalised through the ring and can be stabilised by the ring nitrogen.
  3. The leaving group departs and aromaticity is restored.

For halopyridines, the rate depends strongly on the position of the leaving group. Substitution is usually easier at C-2 and C-4 than at C-3 because the anionic intermediate from α/γ attack can be stabilised by resonance involving nitrogen. The identity of the nucleophile, leaving group and other substituents also affects the outcome.

2-chloropyridine + NaOEt → 2-ethoxypyridine + NaCl

Substitution through a pyridyne intermediate

Under very strong basic conditions, elimination of a neighbouring hydrogen and halide can generate a highly reactive pyridyne intermediate. A nucleophile can add to either end of the formal triple-bond region, so more than one regioisomer may form. Pyridynes are too reactive to isolate under ordinary conditions.

Pyridinium salts

Converting pyridine to an N-alkylpyridinium salt makes the ring more electron-deficient and can greatly accelerate nucleophilic addition/substitution. The position preference changes with the N-substituent and the ring substituents; do not automatically transfer the exact rate order from neutral pyridines to pyridinium salts.

7. Pyridine N-oxide and other derivatives

Pyridine N-oxide is commonly prepared by oxidation of pyridine with a peroxy acid or hydrogen peroxide under suitable conditions. Its bonding is often represented as a charge-separated N⁺–O⁻ structure. Resonance donation from oxygen increases electron density at the 2- and 4-positions, which can facilitate electrophilic substitution at those positions.

Pyridine + peroxy acid (RCO₃H) → pyridine N-oxide + carboxylic acid

The N-oxide can be deoxygenated back to pyridine using a suitable reducing or oxygen-transfer reagent. It can also undergo functionalisation on the ring. The exact outcome depends on the reagent and reaction conditions.

DerivativeFormationKey point
Pyridinium chlorideProtonation of pyridine with HClSalt contains pyridinium cation and chloride anion.
N-methylpyridinium iodideAlkylation with CH₃IAlkylation occurs at nitrogen.
Pyridine N-oxideOxidation of pyridineOxygen is bonded to ring nitrogen; resonance changes ring reactivity.

8. Synthesis and reduction of pyridines

8.1 The Hantzsch 1,4-dihydropyridine synthesis: a 5 + 1 construction

The Hantzsch approach joins two β-ketoester molecules, an aldehyde and ammonia. A sequence of condensation, carbon–carbon bond formation, cyclisation and dehydration produces a 1,4-dihydropyridine. Oxidation can then give the corresponding aromatic pyridine derivative.

Figure 8.1. Conceptual outline of the Hantzsch 5 + 1 synthesis. The exact substituents depend on the chosen aldehyde and β-ketoester.

8.2 Other ring-construction strategies

  • 5 + 1: a five-atom carbon framework is combined with a one-atom nitrogen source, commonly ammonia, followed by cyclisation.
  • 3 + 3: two three-atom fragments are joined to construct the six-membered ring.
  • 4 + 2: a four-atom component and a two-atom component combine, often through a cycloaddition strategy followed by appropriate adjustment of oxidation state.

A useful route to 1,5-dicarbonyl compounds is Michael addition of an enolate or enamine donor to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl acceptor. The resulting chain can undergo condensation with ammonia and cyclisation to form a pyridine framework.

8.3 Reduction of pyridine

Hydrogenation of pyridine can produce piperidine when the aromatic ring is fully reduced. Partial reduction gives dihydropyridine isomers. Birch-type reduction conditions can produce a 1,4-dihydropyridine pattern, whereas other reducing systems may favour different products. Always identify whether the reaction reduces one π bond or the entire ring.

TransformationTypical outcomeStructural change
Catalytic hydrogenationPiperidineAll three ring π bonds are reduced.
Controlled hydride reductionDihydropyridine productsPartial reduction; product depends on conditions.
Birch-type reduction1,4-Dihydropyridine frameworkTwo ring positions become saturated while a conjugated diene remains.

9. Five-membered heterocycles: pyrrole, furan and thiophene

Pyrrole, furan and thiophene are five-membered aromatic heterocycles containing nitrogen, oxygen and sulfur respectively. These compounds are important in natural products and synthetic chemistry. Pyrrole-derived units occur in haem and chlorophyll-related structures; furan rings occur in many natural products; sulfur heterocycles are found in natural and synthetic molecules.

CompoundHeteroatomRepresentative physical featureImportant structural point
PyrroleNHBoiling point about 130 °CN–H can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds.
FuranOBoiling point about 31 °COne oxygen lone pair contributes to aromaticity.
ThiopheneSBoiling point about 84 °CSulfur is less electronegative than oxygen; π donation differs.

The higher boiling point of pyrrole is partly explained by intermolecular hydrogen bonding between N–H groups. Furan and thiophene do not have an equivalent N–H donor.

10. Aromaticity and resonance in five-membered rings

In pyrrole, furan and thiophene, two π bonds contribute four electrons and one heteroatom lone pair contributes two more. The result is a six-electron aromatic π system. The second lone pair on oxygen or sulfur remains outside this system.

Figure 10.1. Each ring has an aromatic sextet; the heteroatom lone-pair contribution is essential to the electron count.

Resonance contributors place increased electron density at the carbon atoms, especially the α positions (C-2 and C-5). This helps explain why these rings react readily with electrophiles and why substitution usually occurs at C-2.

Relative reactivity and aromatic character

In typical electrophilic substitution conditions, pyrrole and furan are highly reactive, and thiophene is also more reactive than benzene. A commonly taught qualitative order is pyrrole and furan above thiophene above benzene, although the precise comparison depends on the electrophile, solvent and reaction conditions. The stability of the σ-complex and the ease of retaining or restoring aromaticity must be considered together.

Aromaticity is a balance. A heteroatom may donate a lone pair to stabilise the ring, but that same donation reduces the availability of the lone pair for protonation. This is why aromatic pyrrole is much less basic than saturated pyrrolidine.

11. Synthesis of pyrrole, furan and thiophene

11.1 Paal–Knorr synthesis

A 1,4-dicarbonyl compound can be converted into a five-membered heterocycle by cyclisation and loss of water. The heteroatom source determines the product family: ammonia or a primary amine gives a pyrrole; acidic dehydration gives a furan; and a sulfurising reagent such as phosphorus pentasulfide can give a thiophene.

Figure 11.1. Paal–Knorr route selection: the reagent controls which heteroatom is incorporated into the five-membered ring.

11.2 Knorr pyrrole synthesis

The Knorr method combines an α-amino carbonyl compound with a β-dicarbonyl compound or β-ketoester. Condensation and cyclisation generate a substituted pyrrole. It is useful when substituents need to be introduced at defined ring positions.

α-Aminoketone + β-dicarbonyl compound → substituted pyrrole + small molecules (often H₂O)

11.3 Other pyrrole preparations

  • Heating succinimide with zinc dust can produce pyrrole by reductive deoxygenation.
  • Furan can be converted to pyrrole by reaction with ammonia and steam over a suitable alumina catalyst.
  • Acetylene and ammonia can form pyrrole under high-temperature conditions; this is a historical high-temperature route rather than a routine laboratory preparation.

11.4 Furan and thiophene

Furan is commonly prepared through acid-catalysed cyclodehydration of suitable 1,4-dicarbonyl precursors. Thiophene can be formed by replacing oxygen functionality in a 1,4-dicarbonyl precursor with sulfur using sulfurising reagents. The specific reagent and temperature must be selected for the substrate.

12. Pyrrole: acidity, sensitivity to acids and electrophilic substitution

12.1 Why pyrrole is a very weak base

The nitrogen lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet. Protonating nitrogen would remove this pair from the delocalised system and disrupt aromatic stabilisation. As a result, pyrrole is much less basic than an ordinary secondary amine.

12.2 Why pyrrole can show N–H acidity

Removal of the N–H proton gives a conjugate base in which the negative charge can be delocalised over the ring. Strong bases, alkali metals or suitable organometallic reagents can deprotonate pyrrole. The resulting pyrrolide salts are useful intermediates for N-alkylation and N-acylation.

Pyrrole–NH + Base⁻ → pyrrolide anion + H–Base

12.3 Sensitivity to strong acids

Strong acids can protonate pyrrole at carbon. The resulting electrophilic species may react with another pyrrole molecule, leading to oligomerisation or polymerisation. Therefore, conventional strongly acidic nitrating and sulfonating conditions can destroy or polymerise pyrrole rather than give a clean substitution product.

12.4 Electrophilic substitution: why C-2?

Pyrrole is electron-rich because nitrogen donates a lone pair into the ring. Electrophilic substitution is therefore easier than in benzene and can often be performed under mild conditions. Attack at C-2 (α) gives a σ-complex with more important resonance contributors than attack at C-3 (β). The greater stabilisation of this intermediate explains the usual preference for C-2 substitution.

Figure 12.1. Electrophilic substitution normally occurs at C-2 when that position is available.

12.5 Second substitution and substituent effects

After one substituent has entered the ring, its electronic character and steric size influence the next substitution. An electron-withdrawing substituent generally deactivates the ring and may direct a further electrophile away from positions where resonance or steric interactions are unfavourable. Electron-donating groups usually activate the ring and can favour positions ortho/para to the directing group within the heterocycle's numbering framework. N-substituted pyrroles can show different orientation patterns because the substituent on nitrogen changes both electron distribution and steric accessibility.

For a directing-effect question, draw the relevant σ-complexes rather than relying only on a memorised label. Compare the number and stability of resonance contributors, and check whether any contributor places an unfavourable charge on the heteroatom.

13. Condensation of pyrrole and formation of porphyrins

Unsubstituted pyrrole can condense with aldehydes or ketones at an α carbon in acidic medium. Initial coupling gives a dipyrromethane (two pyrrole units connected through a carbon bridge). Further condensation can produce a tetrapyrrolic macrocycle known as a porphyrinogen.

Figure 13.1. Simplified sequence from pyrrole condensation to a porphyrin framework.

Oxidation converts porphyrinogen into porphyrin, producing an extended conjugated macrocycle. Porphyrin systems are important because their central cavity can bind metal ions. Haem contains an iron–porphyrin unit; chlorophyll contains a magnesium-containing tetrapyrrole-derived macrocycle.

14. Fused heterocycles

A fused heterocycle contains two rings that share a pair of adjacent atoms and the bond between them. Fusion can extend conjugation and create new physical, chemical and biological properties. Important examples include quinoline, isoquinoline and indole.

Figure 14.1. Ring-fusion concept for quinoline and indole; full structures are shown in the synthesis discussions below.

15. Quinoline and its synthesis

Quinoline is a bicyclic aromatic heterocycle consisting of a benzene ring fused to a pyridine ring. Its molecular formula is C₉H₇N. It is a benzopyridine framework and is the core structure of several biologically active compounds.

15.1 Skraup synthesis

The Skraup synthesis produces quinoline by heating aniline with glycerol in the presence of a strong acid and an oxidising agent. Glycerol is dehydrated to an unsaturated carbonyl compound (acrolein or its equivalent), which reacts with aniline. Cyclisation and oxidation then generate the aromatic quinoline system.

Figure 15.1. Conceptual sequence of the Skraup quinoline synthesis.

15.2 Friedländer synthesis

The Friedländer method combines an o-aminoaryl aldehyde or ketone with a carbonyl compound containing an α-methylene group. Condensation forms a carbon–carbon bond, followed by cyclisation and dehydration to build the second ring of quinoline. It is especially useful when the required carbonyl partners are available.

o-Aminoaryl carbonyl compound + carbonyl compound with α-H → substituted quinoline + H₂O

15.3 Conrad–Limpach synthesis

Aniline reacts with a β-ketoester to form an anilino-enone/enamine intermediate. Depending on conditions, intramolecular cyclisation followed by loss of alcohol and further tautomerisation gives a 4-hydroxyquinoline/quinolone framework. The method is widely used to prepare quinolone-type ring systems.

Aniline + β-ketoester → anilino-enone → cyclised quinolone derivative

16. Isoquinoline and the Bischler–Napieralski reaction

Isoquinoline is a structural isomer of quinoline. Both have the formula C₉H₇N and contain fused benzene and pyridine rings, but the nitrogen atom occupies a different position in the six-membered heterocyclic ring.

Bischler–Napieralski cyclisation

The Bischler–Napieralski reaction converts suitable β-arylethyl amides into 3,4-dihydroisoquinolines. A dehydrating/activating reagent such as POCl₃, P₂O₅ or ZnCl₂ activates the amide carbonyl. The electron-rich aromatic ring then attacks intramolecularly, closing the ring. Subsequent dehydrogenation can produce isoquinoline derivatives.

Figure 16.1. Simplified Bischler–Napieralski pathway. The exact substrate determines the substitution pattern.

17. Indole and Fischer indole synthesis

Indole is a bicyclic aromatic compound formed by fusion of a benzene ring with a pyrrole ring. Its nitrogen is pyrrole-like: the lone pair participates in the extended aromatic system. Indole units occur in natural products and are common building blocks in pharmaceutical chemistry.

Fischer indole synthesis

The Fischer method begins with a phenylhydrazine and an aldehyde or ketone. They form a hydrazone, which under acidic conditions undergoes tautomerisation and rearrangement. Intramolecular ring closure and loss of ammonia produce the indole framework.

Figure 17.1. Fischer indole synthesis shown as a sequence of key transformations.

Applications of the indole scaffold

Indole is found in tryptophan and in many natural products. The indole framework is also present in medicinal compounds such as indomethacin. In synthesis questions, identify the hydrazone formation, rearrangement, cyclisation and ammonia loss as the major conceptual stages.

18. Heterocycles in medicinal and applied chemistry

Heterocyclic rings are not merely structural decorations. Their heteroatoms can change solubility, basicity, hydrogen bonding, electronic distribution and the way a molecule binds to a biological target. A ring may also improve metabolic stability or provide a convenient site for further chemical modification.

CompoundHeterocyclic featureContext
QuinineQuinoline-containing alkaloidHistorically important antimalarial compound.

Other important examples include nucleobases, porphyrins, vitamin-derived heterocycles and compounds used in crop protection. The chemistry of the ring often determines how the molecule behaves under acidic/basic conditions and which reactions can be used to synthesise it.

Clinical note: the examples above illustrate chemical structures and historical/therapeutic contexts for study. They are not instructions for choosing or using medicines.

19. Revision exercises

Attempt the questions before opening the suggested answers. Draw structures and mechanisms on paper where appropriate; a written explanation alone is not enough for a mechanism question.

Exercise 1 — Definitions and classification
  1. Define a heterocyclic compound.
  2. Classify pyridine, piperidine, furan and thiophene by ring size, heteroatom and saturation.
  3. Give two examples of biological molecules that contain heterocyclic rings.
Suggested answer: A heterocycle is a cyclic compound with at least one non-carbon atom in the ring. Pyridine is an unsaturated six-membered N-heterocycle; piperidine is a saturated six-membered N-heterocycle; furan is an unsaturated five-membered O-heterocycle; thiophene is an unsaturated five-membered S-heterocycle. Examples include nucleobases and haem/chlorophyll-related porphyrin systems.
Exercise 2 — Nomenclature
  1. Distinguish pyridazine, pyrimidine and pyrazine by the positions of their two nitrogen atoms.
  2. Explain the difference between quinoline and isoquinoline.
  3. What information does the prefix 1,2-dihydro convey?
Suggested answer: Pyridazine is 1,2-diazine; pyrimidine is 1,3-diazine; pyrazine is 1,4-diazine. Quinoline and isoquinoline are fused benzopyridine isomers with nitrogen in different positions. 1,2-dihydro indicates added hydrogenation at positions 1 and 2 relative to the fully unsaturated parent system.
Exercise 3 — Aromaticity
  1. State the four requirements for aromaticity.
  2. Calculate the π-electron count of pyrrole.
  3. Explain why only one lone pair on oxygen contributes to the aromaticity of furan.
  4. Explain why the nitrogen lone pair in pyridine is not counted in its aromatic sextet.
Suggested answer: The ring must be cyclic, planar, continuously conjugated and contain 4n+2 π electrons. Pyrrole has four electrons from two π bonds plus two from the nitrogen lone pair, giving six. In furan, one oxygen lone pair occupies a p orbital and the other is in an in-plane orbital. Pyridine nitrogen contributes one p electron; its lone pair occupies an in-plane sp² orbital.
Exercise 4 — Basicity
  1. Arrange piperidine, pyridine and pyrrole in decreasing basicity.
  2. Why is pyridine more basic than pyrrole?
  3. Why is imidazole more basic than pyrrole?
Suggested answer: Piperidine > pyridine > pyrrole. Pyridine can use its in-plane lone pair to bind H⁺ without removing the aromatic sextet; pyrrole would lose aromatic stabilisation if its N lone pair were protonated. Imidazole has a pyridine-like nitrogen available for protonation, and its conjugate acid is resonance-stabilised.
Exercise 5 — Pyridine reactions
  1. Why is electrophilic substitution difficult on pyridine?
  2. Which position is usually favoured in electrophilic substitution of pyridine?
  3. Why are 2- and 4-halopyridines generally more susceptible to nucleophilic substitution than 3-halopyridine?
  4. Describe the steps of addition–elimination NAS.
Suggested answer: Nitrogen withdraws electron density and destabilises σ-complexes. β/C-3 is generally favoured in EAS because α/γ attack gives a resonance contributor with positive charge on nitrogen. In NAS, attack at C-2/C-4 permits stabilisation of the anionic intermediate by the ring nitrogen. The nucleophile adds, a σ-adduct forms, the leaving group departs and aromaticity is restored.
Exercise 6 — Pyrrole substitution
  1. Why does pyrrole react with electrophiles under milder conditions than benzene?
  2. Why does electrophilic substitution normally occur at C-2?
  3. Why must strong acids be avoided in many pyrrole substitution reactions?
Suggested answer: Nitrogen donates electron density into the ring. C-2 attack gives a more resonance-stabilised σ-complex than C-3 attack. Strong acids can protonate pyrrole and promote oligomerisation/polymerisation.
Exercise 7 — Named syntheses
  1. What starting material pattern is used in Paal–Knorr pyrrole synthesis?
  2. What are the principal reactants in the Knorr pyrrole synthesis?
  3. Distinguish Skraup and Friedländer quinoline syntheses.
  4. What product class is formed in the Bischler–Napieralski reaction?
  5. What are the starting materials in Fischer indole synthesis?
Suggested answer: Paal–Knorr uses a 1,4-dicarbonyl plus ammonia/amine. Knorr uses an α-amino carbonyl compound plus a β-dicarbonyl or β-ketoester. Skraup uses aniline and glycerol under acidic oxidative conditions; Friedländer combines an o-aminoaryl carbonyl compound with a carbonyl partner containing an α-hydrogen. Bischler–Napieralski gives 3,4-dihydroisoquinolines. Fischer uses phenylhydrazine and an aldehyde/ketone through a hydrazone intermediate.
Exercise 8 — Mechanism drawing
  1. Draw protonation and resonance contributors for pyridine forming pyridinium.
  2. Draw the addition–elimination mechanism for a nucleophile replacing chloride at C-2 of a chloropyridine.
  3. Draw the Paal–Knorr cyclisation of a 1,4-dicarbonyl to a pyrrole.
  4. Draw the Fischer indole sequence and label hydrazone formation, rearrangement, cyclisation and loss of ammonia.
Marking guide: Show curved arrows from electron pairs or bonds to the correct electron-poor centre, include charges and proton transfers, identify the leaving group and show where aromaticity is restored. Do not draw arrows from an atom without identifying the electron pair or bond that moves.
Exercise 9 — Applied chemistry
  1. Identify the heterocyclic family in quinine, papaverine and indomethacin.
  2. Explain why porphyrins are able to bind metal ions.
  3. Give two ways a heterocyclic ring can influence the properties of a drug molecule.
Suggested answer: Quinine contains a quinoline unit; papaverine is an isoquinoline alkaloid; indomethacin contains an indole unit. Porphyrins have a central cavity with inward-facing nitrogen donor atoms that coordinate a metal. A heterocycle can change basicity, polarity, hydrogen bonding, solubility, electron distribution and metabolic behaviour.

How to study this course

For each heterocycle, practise four linked skills: (1) draw and number the ring, (2) count π electrons and identify the lone pair involved, (3) predict the major reaction position, and (4) explain the mechanism using curved arrows. For named syntheses, memorise the starting-material pattern and the key bond-forming step rather than only the reaction name.
