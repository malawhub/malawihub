1. Principles of NMR spectroscopy

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is an analytical method used to obtain information about the chemical environments of magnetically active nuclei. In organic chemistry, the most frequently studied nuclei are hydrogen-1 (1H) and carbon-13 (13C).

1.1 Nuclear spin and magnetic behaviour

Some nuclei possess a property called nuclear spin. A nucleus with non-zero spin has a magnetic moment and behaves like a tiny magnet. When such a nucleus is placed in an external magnetic field, its allowed orientations have different energies.

Conceptual energy-level diagram for a spin-½ nucleus such as 1H.

For a spin-½ nucleus such as 1H, two energy states are possible in a magnetic field: a lower-energy state aligned with the field and a higher-energy state opposed to it. The energy difference increases with magnetic-field strength.

When radiofrequency energy of the correct frequency is supplied, the nucleus can absorb energy and undergo a transition between the two spin states. This condition is resonance.

Resonance relationship
ΔE = hν
where ΔE is the energy difference, h is Planck’s constant and ν is the resonance frequency. The resonance frequency is proportional to the applied magnetic field strength.

1.2 Why different nuclei give different signals

Electrons surrounding a nucleus circulate in response to the external field and generate a small induced magnetic field. This changes the effective field experienced by the nucleus. Because different atoms and functional groups have different electron distributions, nuclei in different chemical environments resonate at different frequencies.

1.3 Which nuclei can be observed?

NMR-active nuclei have non-zero nuclear spin. Common examples include 1H, 13C, 19F and 31P. The common isotope 12C is not observed by ordinary NMR because its nuclear spin is zero. Although 13C is NMR-active, it has a low natural abundance of about 1.1%, so its signals are much weaker than proton signals.

2. Reading an NMR spectrum

An NMR spectrum plots signal intensity against chemical shift. Chemical shift is represented by the Greek letter δ and is measured in parts per million (ppm).

Schematic only: a proton NMR spectrum is conventionally displayed with higher chemical shift at the left.

The scale normally runs from higher ppm on the left to lower ppm on the right. A signal at higher ppm is described as downfield, while one at lower ppm is upfield. These terms describe position on the spectrum, not the physical location of a proton in a molecule.

2.1 Tetramethylsilane (TMS)

Tetramethylsilane, Si(CH3)4, is commonly used as a reference standard and assigned a chemical shift of 0 ppm. It gives one sharp signal because its twelve protons are chemically equivalent. Its signal is usually found upfield of most organic proton signals.

2.2 Chemical shift equation

The chemical shift is the difference between the sample resonance frequency and the reference frequency, expressed relative to the spectrometer operating frequency.

δ (ppm) = [(νsample − νreference) / νspectrometer] × 106

Using ppm makes chemical shifts broadly comparable between instruments operating at different magnetic-field strengths.

3. Number of signals in 1H NMR

The number of distinct proton signals generally corresponds to the number of chemically different proton environments. Chemically equivalent protons resonate at the same chemical shift under the measurement conditions.

3.1 Recognising equivalent protons

  • Protons related by a symmetry operation are often chemically equivalent.
  • The three protons of a freely rotating methyl group are usually equivalent on the NMR timescale.
  • Protons in different functional groups normally have different chemical environments.
  • In rigid rings or restricted systems, apparently similar hydrogens may be nonequivalent.
The equivalence of protons must be judged from the molecular structure and its symmetry.

3.2 A practical equivalence test

Replace one proton at a time with a hypothetical label such as D. If replacement at either position produces the same molecule, the protons are homotopic and chemically equivalent. If it produces enantiomers, they are enantiotopic and usually equivalent in an achiral NMR environment. If it produces diastereomers, they are diastereotopic and can give separate signals.

Important: Do not count every hydrogen separately. First identify groups of equivalent protons. Draw all C–H bonds in rings and alkenes where stereochemistry may affect equivalence.

4. Chemical shifts, shielding and deshielding

Shielding occurs when surrounding electron density reduces the effective magnetic field experienced by a nucleus. A shielded proton generally resonates at a lower chemical shift. Deshielding occurs when the nucleus experiences a greater effective field and generally moves to a higher chemical shift.

Conceptual illustration of the effect of local electron density on a proton.

4.1 Factors affecting chemical shift

  • Electronegativity: electronegative atoms such as oxygen, nitrogen and halogens withdraw electron density from nearby bonds and often deshield nearby protons.
  • Hybridisation: the chemical shift depends on the bonding environment and hybridisation of the carbon bearing the proton.
  • π systems: aromatic rings, alkenes and alkynes generate anisotropic induced magnetic fields.
  • Hydrogen bonding: exchangeable O–H and N–H proton shifts can vary with solvent, concentration and temperature.
  • Conjugation and resonance: electron distribution in conjugated systems changes local shielding.

4.2 Approximate 1H chemical-shift guide

4.3 Aromatic and alkenic anisotropy

When an aromatic ring is placed in a magnetic field, its π electrons circulate and create an induced magnetic field. Around the outer edge of the ring, where aromatic hydrogens are located, this induced field usually reinforces the applied field. Aromatic protons are therefore deshielded. Alkene protons are also commonly deshielded by the anisotropic effect of the double bond.

For an alkyne, the induced field has a different spatial pattern. The acetylenic proton lies along the bond axis in a region that is relatively shielded, so its shift is often lower than that of an alkene proton.

4.4 Chemical-shift estimation by additivity

Some introductory methods estimate proton shifts by starting with a base value for a proton type and adding substituent corrections. The method can be useful for simple saturated structures, but empirical tables and the exact structural environment must be used consistently.

Illustrative approach:
Estimated δ = base shift for the proton type + sum of applicable substituent corrections.
Identify the carbon bearing the proton (α carbon), then identify substituents on that carbon and on neighbouring β carbons. Avoid applying a correction twice or mixing tables developed for different structural classes.

5. Integration: how many protons give each signal?

The integrated area under a 1H NMR signal is proportional to the number of protons contributing to that signal. An integration trace is often drawn as a step-like curve. The height increase across a signal represents its relative proton count.

Schematic spectrum: integration is based on peak area, not simply peak height.

5.1 Converting integral ratios into proton numbers

Suppose three signals have integral values 1.5, 3.0 and 4.5.
Divide each value by the smallest value (1.5):
1.5 : 3.0 : 4.5 = 1 : 2 : 3.
The relative proton ratio is therefore 1 : 2 : 3.

To convert a ratio into actual proton numbers, use the molecular formula and chemical structure. The integral alone gives a relative ratio; it does not automatically give the absolute number of hydrogens.

6. Spin–spin splitting

Spin–spin splitting, also called scalar coupling, occurs when the spin states of nearby nonequivalent NMR-active nuclei influence one another. In ordinary introductory 1H NMR, the most familiar examples involve coupling between nonequivalent protons separated by a small number of bonds.

6.1 The n + 1 rule

For a simple first-order proton signal split by n equivalent neighbouring protons, the signal is divided into n + 1 lines.

Equivalent neighbouring H (n)Number of linesPatternRelative intensity
01Singlet1
12Doublet1 : 1
23Triplet1 : 2 : 1
34Quartet1 : 3 : 3 : 1
45Quintet1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1
Simplified line patterns; line spacing within one multiplet is equal for simple first-order coupling.

6.2 Pascal’s triangle and peak intensities

The relative line intensities for simple first-order multiplets follow the coefficients of Pascal’s triangle. A triplet has intensities 1:2:1 and a quartet has 1:3:3:1. The central lines are taller because more combinations of neighbouring nuclear spin orientations produce those transitions.

6.3 Coupling constant, J

The distance between adjacent lines in a multiplet is the coupling constant, J, measured in hertz (Hz). Coupled signals share the same J value. Unlike chemical shift in ppm, J is generally independent of the spectrometer field strength for a given coupling interaction.

6.4 Common coupling relationships and limitations

  • Equivalent protons do not split one another.
  • Coupling is commonly observed across two or three bonds, although longer-range coupling can occur in suitable systems.
  • Rapid rotation can average some conformational effects.
  • Exchangeable O–H and N–H protons may show broad signals and variable coupling depending on conditions.
  • The simple n + 1 rule assumes equivalent neighbours and first-order behaviour. Complex or strongly coupled systems may show more complicated multiplets.
Ethyl-group recognition: a simple CH3–CH2 fragment often gives a three-proton triplet and a two-proton quartet. The methyl group is split by two CH2 protons, while the methylene group is split by three methyl protons.

7. Structure determination using 1H NMR

Do not assign a structure from one peak alone. Combine the number of signals, chemical shifts, integration and splitting patterns with the molecular formula and other analytical evidence.

7.1 Worked example: an ethyl fragment

A common ethyl fragment gives a recognisable 3H/2H integration and splitting pattern.

Suppose a spectrum contains a three-proton triplet and a two-proton quartet. The integration ratio 3:2 and reciprocal splitting are consistent with a CH3CH2– fragment. Their chemical shifts then help identify what is attached to the ethyl group.

7.2 A systematic method

  1. Check the molecular formula and calculate the degree of unsaturation if the formula is available.
  2. Count the distinct proton signals and consider molecular symmetry.
  3. Use integration to determine the relative number of protons in each environment.
  4. Use chemical shifts to identify likely functional-group environments.
  5. Use splitting and coupling relationships to connect neighbouring proton groups.
  6. Construct possible fragments and join them without violating the molecular formula or valence.
  7. Check whether every proton and every signal is accounted for.

7.3 Degree of unsaturation

For a typical formula containing carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and halogens, the degree of unsaturation (also called the index of hydrogen deficiency) can be estimated by:

DBE = (2C + 2 + N − H − X) / 2
where X is the total number of monovalent halogen atoms. Oxygen and sulphur are omitted from this expression.

Each ring or double bond contributes one degree of unsaturation; each triple bond contributes two.

8. Carbon-13 NMR spectroscopy

13C NMR provides information about the number and types of chemically distinct carbon atoms. Because 13C has low natural abundance, its signals are weaker than proton signals, but modern instruments and pulse methods make carbon spectra highly useful.

8.1 Number of carbon signals

Each chemically distinct carbon environment generally gives one signal. Molecular symmetry can make multiple carbon atoms equivalent, reducing the number of observed signals. In a routine broadband proton-decoupled 13C spectrum, each carbon environment commonly appears as a single line.

8.2 Chemical-shift range

Carbon environmentApproximate δ (ppm)
Simple saturated alkyl carbon0–50
Carbon attached to O, N or halogenAbout 40–90
Alkyne carbonAbout 65–90
Alkene and aromatic carbonsAbout 100–160
Carboxylic acid derivatives and ester carbonylsAbout 160–185
Aldehyde and ketone carbonylsAbout 190–220

These ranges are approximate and may overlap. The carbonyl region is especially useful because carbonyl carbons occur at substantially higher chemical shifts than most saturated carbons.

8.3 Proton-decoupled carbon spectra

In a broadband proton-decoupled 13C experiment, interactions between carbon nuclei and attached protons are removed from the displayed spectrum. This usually gives one simplified line per chemically distinct carbon environment. Signal intensity in a routine 13C spectrum is not normally directly proportional to the number of carbons because relaxation and nuclear Overhauser effects influence intensities.

9. DEPT: identifying CH₃, CH₂ and CH groups

DEPT means Distortionless Enhancement by Polarisation Transfer. It is a carbon NMR experiment that uses proton-to-carbon magnetisation transfer to distinguish carbon types according to the number of attached hydrogens. Quaternary carbons, which have no directly attached hydrogen, do not appear in the usual DEPT spectra.

ExperimentCH₃CH₂CHQuaternary C
DEPT-45PositivePositivePositiveAbsent
DEPT-90AbsentAbsentPositiveAbsent
DEPT-135PositiveNegativePositiveAbsent
DEPT-135 distinguishes CH₂ from CH and CH₃ by the phase of the signal.

Compare the normal proton-decoupled carbon spectrum with DEPT-45, DEPT-90 and DEPT-135. A carbon present in the normal spectrum but absent from DEPT experiments is likely quaternary. A signal present in DEPT-90 is a methine carbon. A negative DEPT-135 signal identifies a methylene carbon.

10. Two-dimensional NMR spectroscopy

Two-dimensional NMR experiments provide correlation information between nuclei. A 2D spectrum has two frequency axes and displays cross-peaks that reveal coupling or spatial proximity, depending on the experiment. It can help connect fragments that are difficult to assign from one-dimensional spectra alone.

10.1 COSY: proton–proton correlation

COSY (Correlation Spectroscopy) is a homonuclear experiment that shows correlations between spin-coupled protons, usually protons connected through a small number of bonds. The diagonal contains the proton signals; off-diagonal cross-peaks identify coupled proton pairs.

Illustrative only: paired off-diagonal cross-peaks indicate a proton–proton correlation.

10.2 HSQC: one-bond proton–carbon correlation

HSQC (Heteronuclear Single Quantum Coherence) correlates a proton with the carbon to which it is directly attached. The cross-peak therefore identifies one-bond C–H connections. This is especially useful when assigning which carbon signal belongs to a particular proton signal.

10.3 HMBC: long-range proton–carbon correlation

HMBC (Heteronuclear Multiple Bond Correlation) detects longer-range correlations, commonly over two or three bonds and sometimes farther in suitable structures. It helps connect fragments and locate quaternary or carbonyl carbons that have no directly attached proton.

TechniqueNuclei correlatedMain information
COSY1H–1HWhich protons are spin-coupled
HSQC1H–13CDirect, one-bond C–H connections
HMBC1H–13CLonger-range connectivity, often two or three bonds

Do not confuse COSY and NOESY: COSY mainly reports through-bond scalar coupling. NOESY reports through-space proximity between nuclei and can provide stereochemical information.

11. Complete strategy for interpreting NMR data

  1. Begin with the molecular formula and calculate DBE.
  2. Use 13C NMR to count carbon environments and identify carbonyl, alkene, aromatic and saturated carbon regions.
  3. Use DEPT data to classify protonated carbons as CH₃, CH₂ or CH.
  4. Use the number of 1H signals to identify distinct proton environments.
  5. Use integration to establish proton ratios.
  6. Use chemical shifts to assign likely functional-group environments.
  7. Use splitting and J values to identify coupled proton fragments.
  8. Use COSY to build proton coupling networks, HSQC to connect each proton to its directly attached carbon and HMBC to connect more distant fragments.
  9. Draw a structure and check that every carbon, hydrogen, signal and correlation is explained.
Final consistency check: the proposed structure must agree with the molecular formula, DBE, carbon count, proton count, integrations, chemical shifts, splitting patterns and 2D correlations. If one important signal remains unexplained, reconsider the structure.

13. Revision exercises and self-assessment

Attempt the questions before opening the suggested answers. These questions are newly written for this learning page.

Exercise 1 — Nuclear spin

Explain why a proton can interact with an external magnetic field and why two energy states are observed.

Suggested answer: A proton has spin ½ and a magnetic moment. In an external field its allowed orientations have different energies. The lower-energy state is aligned with the field and the higher-energy state is opposed to it.

Exercise 2 — Chemical shift

Define chemical shift and explain why it is reported in ppm.

Suggested answer: Chemical shift expresses the resonance frequency of a nucleus relative to a reference, normally TMS. Dividing the frequency difference by the spectrometer frequency and multiplying by one million gives ppm, making values comparable across instruments.

Exercise 3 — Shielding

Explain why a proton attached to a carbon next to oxygen often appears at a higher chemical shift than a simple alkyl proton.

Suggested answer: Oxygen withdraws electron density through the inductive effect. The nearby proton is deshielded and generally resonates downfield at a higher δ value.

Exercise 4 — Integration

Three proton signals have integral values 2, 6 and 4. Determine the simplest whole-number proton ratio.

Suggested answer: Divide each value by 2. The ratio is 1:3:2.

Exercise 5 — Splitting

Predict the splitting pattern and relative intensities for a proton group coupled to two equivalent neighbouring protons.

Suggested answer: n + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3 lines, a triplet with relative intensities 1:2:1.

Exercise 6 — Ethyl group

A spectrum contains a 3H triplet and a 2H quartet. What structural fragment is suggested?

Suggested answer: A CH₃CH₂– fragment. The three methyl protons are split by two methylene protons and the two methylene protons are split by three methyl protons.

Exercise 7 — Carbon NMR

Why can a molecule containing ten carbon atoms show fewer than ten signals in a 13C NMR spectrum?

Suggested answer: Symmetry or identical chemical environments can make multiple carbon atoms chemically equivalent, so they produce the same signal.

Exercise 8 — DEPT-135

Which carbon types give positive and negative peaks in a DEPT-135 spectrum?

Suggested answer: CH and CH₃ normally give positive peaks, CH₂ gives a negative peak and quaternary carbons are absent.

Exercise 9 — 2D NMR

Distinguish the main information obtained from COSY, HSQC and HMBC.

Suggested answer: COSY identifies coupled proton pairs; HSQC links a proton to its directly attached carbon; HMBC provides longer-range proton–carbon correlations, commonly over two or three bonds.

Exercise 10 — Structure strategy

Why should a proposed molecular structure be checked against both integration and the molecular formula?

Suggested answer: Integration establishes relative numbers of protons in each environment, while the molecular formula sets the total atom count and possible unsaturation. The structure must satisfy both as well as the observed shifts and splitting.
