1. Nuclear spin, energy levels and resonance

Nuclear magnetic resonance is used to investigate the chemical environments of magnetically active nuclei. In organic chemistry, the two nuclei most frequently examined are ¹H and ¹³C. The energy source is radiofrequency radiation, which has a long wavelength and relatively low energy compared with ultraviolet or visible radiation.

1.1 Why a proton behaves like a tiny magnet

A proton has a nuclear spin and a magnetic moment. It can therefore be treated as a tiny bar magnet. In the absence of an external magnetic field, the magnetic moments have random orientations. When a strong external magnetic field, B₀, is applied, the allowed orientations of a spin-½ nucleus are quantised. The lower-energy orientation is parallel to B₀ (often called α); the higher-energy orientation is opposed to B₀ (β). A small excess of nuclei occupies the lower-energy state.

Energy transition

When radiofrequency energy matches the energy gap, the nucleus absorbs energy and changes spin state. This is resonance.

ΔE = hν

ΔE = energy separation; h = Planck constant; ν = resonance frequency.

1.2 Relationship between magnetic field and frequency

The resonance frequency is proportional to the strength of the applied magnetic field. A stronger magnet creates a larger separation between the nuclear spin energy levels and requires a higher resonance frequency. Thus instruments are commonly described by their proton frequency, for example 300 MHz or 500 MHz. One MHz is 10⁶ Hz.

Only nuclei with non-zero nuclear spin are NMR-active. Common examples include ¹H, ¹³C, ¹⁹F and ³¹P. The common isotope ¹²C has zero nuclear spin and is not observed in ordinary NMR. The ¹³C isotope is NMR-active but has a natural abundance of only about 1.1%.

1.3 Why chemically different nuclei resonate differently

Electrons around a nucleus circulate in response to the applied field and generate a small induced magnetic field. This changes the effective magnetic field at the nucleus. Since the electron distribution varies with bonding and functional group, protons and carbons in different chemical environments experience different effective fields and resonate at different frequencies.

2. The proton NMR spectrum and TMS

A proton NMR spectrum is a plot of signal intensity against chemical shift, δ, in parts per million (ppm). In the conventional display, higher chemical shift is on the left and lower chemical shift is on the right. Most ordinary organic proton signals occur between about 0 and 10 ppm, although exchangeable protons and unusual structures can fall outside common ranges.

2.1 Tetramethylsilane as reference

Tetramethylsilane, Si(CH₃)₄, is commonly used as the reference and assigned δ = 0 ppm. Its twelve protons are equivalent, so it gives one signal. It is volatile and relatively inert, and its resonance generally appears upfield from most organic proton signals.

2.2 Chemical shift calculation

δ (ppm) = [(νsample − νreference) / νspectrometer] × 10⁶

The frequency difference is divided by the operating frequency and multiplied by one million. This expresses the shift as a relative quantity, making reported ppm values comparable between instruments with different operating frequencies.

3. Counting chemically equivalent protons

The number of signals corresponds to the number of distinct proton environments. Equivalent protons give the same resonance; nonequivalent protons generally give separate signals.

3.1 Examples of signal counting

  • Dimethyl ether, CH₃OCH₃: the two methyl groups are symmetry-equivalent; one proton signal is expected.
  • Chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl: the methyl and methylene groups are different; two signals are expected.
  • Methoxyethane, CH₃OCH₂CH₃: the methoxy methyl, OCH₂ group and terminal methyl are three different environments.
  • 1,2-dichloroethane, ClCH₂CH₂Cl: the two ends are equivalent in the symmetric molecule; one carbon-bound proton environment is expected.
  • Methyl acetate, CH₃COOCH₃: the acetyl methyl and methoxy methyl are different; two signals are expected.
  • Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH: methyl, methylene and hydroxyl hydrogens are three different environments under conditions where the OH resonance is observed separately.

3.2 Alkenes and cycloalkanes

For alkenes, draw every bond to hydrogen and compare whether each hydrogen is cis or trans to the same substituent. Hydrogens on the same alkene carbon are not automatically equivalent. If one hydrogen is cis to chlorine and another is cis to bromine, their environments differ. If two hydrogens are each cis to the same groups because of molecular symmetry, they can be equivalent.

For rings, draw the ring and all C–H bonds, including their orientation. A simple cyclopropane has equivalent hydrogens because of its symmetry. Substitution, as in chlorocyclopropane, removes equivalence and can give several proton environments. A skeletal triangle without hydrogen bonds can hide these differences.

Exam reminder: count proton environments, not individual hydrogen atoms. Use symmetry and molecular motion, and draw explicit C–H bonds for alkenes and rings.

4. Chemical shifts, shielding and anisotropy

4.1 Shielding

The electron cloud around a nucleus generates an induced field that generally opposes the applied field at the nucleus. A proton with greater local electron density is shielded. It experiences a smaller effective field, requires a lower resonance frequency and appears upfield at a lower δ value.

4.2 Deshielding

When electron density is reduced, the nucleus experiences more of the applied field. It requires a higher resonance frequency and appears downfield at a higher δ value. Electronegative atoms such as oxygen, nitrogen and halogens withdraw electron density through sigma bonds. Protons on carbons attached to these atoms therefore tend to appear downfield from simple alkyl protons.

The lecture notes a trend for simple saturated C–H protons as alkyl substitution increases: primary RCH₂–H around 0.9 ppm, secondary R₂CH–H around 1.3 ppm and tertiary R₃C–H around 1.7 ppm.

4.4 Anisotropy and π systems

Benzene: its six π electrons circulate in the magnetic field and create a ring current. At the positions of the ring hydrogens, the induced field reinforces B₀. The hydrogens are deshielded and appear around 6.5–8 ppm.

Alkene: the induced field from the C=C π electrons reinforces the applied field near the vinylic hydrogens. These protons are deshielded and usually appear around 4.5–6 ppm.

Alkyne: the spatial orientation of the induced field around C≡C is different. At the terminal alkyne proton, the induced field opposes B₀, giving relative shielding and a signal near 2.5 ppm.

5. Chemical-shift additivity calculations

The lecture describes an additivity method for estimating saturated C–H chemical shifts. Start with a base value and add the contributions from nearby substituents.

Hydrogen typeBase chemical shift
CH₃0.87 ppm
CH₂1.20 ppm
CH1.20 ppm

The carbon bearing the hydrogen being considered is the α carbon. A substituent attached to this same carbon is an α substituent. The next carbon is the β carbon; groups attached to it are β substituents.

Estimated δ = base value + α contributions + β contributions

Worked example 1: chloroethane terminal methyl

For CH₃CH₂Cl, consider the CH₃ hydrogens. Base value = 0.87 ppm. There are no α substituents. The chlorine is on the β carbon and the lecture's table gives a β-chloro contribution of 0.63 ppm for this methyl case. Estimated δ = 0.87 + 0.63 = 1.50 ppm.

Worked example 2: chloromethylene hydrogens

For the CH₂Cl hydrogens, base value = 1.20 ppm and the α-chloro contribution is 2.30 ppm. Estimated δ = 1.20 + 2.30 = 3.50 ppm.

Worked example 3: methyl acetate

For OCH₃, base value 0.87 ppm plus the α ester-oxygen contribution 2.88 ppm gives about 3.75 ppm. For CH₃CO–, base value 0.87 ppm plus the α carbonyl contribution 1.23 ppm gives about 2.10 ppm. This predicts two separate methyl signals.

Worked example 4: 2,3-dimethyl-2-butene

For its methyl protons, the lecture adds 0.78 ppm to the 0.87 ppm base value, giving an estimated shift of 1.65 ppm. Symmetry makes the methyl groups equivalent in this molecule.

These are estimates based on the lecture's additivity table. They should be compared with the observed spectrum rather than treated as exact universal values.

6. Signal intensity and integration

The area under a proton NMR signal is proportional to the number of protons responsible for that signal. The spectrometer displays an integral as a stepped curve. The vertical height of each step represents the integrated area. The ratio of step heights gives the relative proton ratio, not automatically the absolute number of protons.

Worked integration example

A molecule has formula C₉H₁₀O₂ and integral values 54, 23 and 33. Total integral = 110 units. The molecular formula contains 10 hydrogens, so 110 ÷ 10 = 11 units per proton.

  • Signal A: 54 ÷ 11 = 4.9, approximately 5 H.
  • Signal B: 23 ÷ 11 = 2.1, approximately 2 H.
  • Signal C: 33 ÷ 11 = 3.0, approximately 3 H.

The resulting proton count is 5:2:3. In general, divide each integral by the common units-per-proton factor and round to a chemically sensible whole number.

Methyl α,α-dimethylpropionate

The structure contains one methoxy methyl group (3 H) and three equivalent methyl groups on a quaternary carbon (9 H). The expected integral ratio is 3:9, simplified to 1:3. This example shows why integral step heights, rather than peak heights, must be compared.

7. Spin–spin splitting and coupling constants

Spin–spin splitting occurs when a proton interacts magnetically with nonequivalent neighbouring protons. The neighbouring nuclear spin can be aligned with or against B₀, changing the effective field experienced by the observed proton. This produces multiple resonance lines.

7.1 Rules in the lecture

  • Equivalent protons do not split one another.
  • Splitting is commonly observed between nonequivalent protons on the same carbon or adjacent carbons.
  • In the simple cases considered, splitting is generally not observed when protons are separated by more than three sigma bonds.
  • Protons separated by four sigma bonds generally do not show ordinary splitting in the examples.

7.2 How a doublet forms

With one adjacent proton, there are two possible orientations for that neighbouring spin. The observed proton experiences two slightly different effective fields and absorbs at two frequencies. The signal is a doublet with a 1:1 intensity ratio.

7.3 How a triplet forms

With two equivalent adjacent protons, the neighbouring spins can give three distinguishable net field arrangements. There are two equivalent ways to have one spin up and one down, so the middle line is twice as intense as either outer line. The resulting triplet has relative intensities 1:2:1.

The frequency separation between adjacent lines is the coupling constant, J, measured in hertz. Coupled proton signals share the same J for the coupling interaction. J is expressed in Hz, not ppm.

The intensity ratios are the coefficients of Pascal's triangle. The simple n + 1 rule applies when the neighbouring protons are equivalent. If there are two nonequivalent neighbouring sets, the signal is split successively by each set. It can be treated as one combined pattern only when the coupling constants are identical or the lines are unresolved. Otherwise use descriptions such as doublet of doublets.

7.5 Ethyl group recognition

An ethyl fragment CH₃CH₂– often gives a methyl triplet (three protons split by two methylene protons) and a methylene quartet (two protons split by three methyl protons). The two signals have the same coupling constant for their mutual coupling. This pattern is a useful clue, but chemical shift is needed to identify what the ethyl group is attached to.

8. Worked ¹H NMR structure determination: C₄H₈O₂

The lecture's unknown compound X has formula C₄H₈O₂ and an IR absorption indicating C=O. The supplied proton data are: A, triplet at 1.1 ppm, integral 15; B, quartet at 2.3 ppm, integral 11; C, singlet at 3.7 ppm, integral 14.

  1. Count signals: three signals mean three proton environments.
  2. Convert integrals: 15 + 11 + 14 = 40 units. The formula has 8 H, so 40 ÷ 8 = 5 units per proton. A = 15 ÷ 5 = 3 H; B = 11 ÷ 5 ≈ 2 H; C = 14 ÷ 5 ≈ 3 H.
  3. Interpret splitting: the 3H triplet and 2H quartet indicate CH₃CH₂–, an ethyl group.
  4. Interpret the singlet: a 3H singlet at 3.7 ppm is consistent with OCH₃.
  5. Use formula and IR: the molecule contains four carbons, eight hydrogens, two oxygens and a carbonyl. The fragments fit an ester.
  6. Compare ester isomers: methyl propanoate, CH₃CH₂COOCH₃, has an OCH₃ singlet and an ethyl group attached to the carbonyl. Ethyl acetate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃, would instead have an OCH₂ quartet at the downfield position and a carbonyl methyl singlet. The observed downfield 3H singlet supports methyl propanoate.
Conclusion: the most consistent structure is methyl propanoate, CH₃CH₂COOCH₃. The assignment explains the molecular formula, carbonyl evidence, three proton environments, integration and splitting patterns.

9. Carbon-13 NMR

¹³C NMR gives information about the number and types of carbon environments. Its signal is much weaker than a proton signal because ¹³C has a natural abundance of about 1.1%. The probability that two directly bonded carbon atoms are both ¹³C is very small, so ordinary spectra do not normally show carbon–carbon splitting.

Each nonequivalent carbon gives a different signal. The number of signals therefore gives the number of different carbon environments, not necessarily the total number of carbon atoms. Symmetry can make several carbons equivalent.

9.1 Proton coupling and decoupling

A proton-coupled carbon signal can be split by attached hydrogens according to the n + 1 relationship. Carbon–hydrogen coupling constants are commonly about 100–250 Hz, so multiplets can overlap. Routine ¹³C spectra are commonly recorded with broadband proton decoupling, which removes the displayed C–H splitting and simplifies the spectrum.

Routine ¹³C peak heights are not normally proportional to the number of carbon atoms. Therefore ordinary carbon spectra are not interpreted by proton-style integration.

9.2 Approximate carbon chemical-shift regions

Carbon shifts are affected by resonance, electronegativity and hybridisation. The broad 0–220 ppm range makes it easier to distinguish many carbon environments than in the narrower proton spectrum.

10. DEPT: Distortionless Enhancement by Polarisation Transfer

DEPT is a one-dimensional carbon experiment that transfers magnetisation from attached protons to carbon. Different pulse angles cause different signal phases depending on the number of hydrogens attached to the carbon. Compare the DEPT spectra with the ordinary carbon spectrum.

Carbon typeDEPT-45DEPT-90DEPT-135
CH₃PositiveAbsentPositive
CH₂PositiveAbsentNegative
CHPositivePositivePositive
Quaternary carbonAbsentAbsentAbsent
  • DEPT-45 shows protonated carbons: CH₃, CH₂ and CH.
  • DEPT-90 shows methine (CH) carbons only.
  • DEPT-135 shows CH and CH₃ positive, while CH₂ is negative.
  • Quaternary carbons have no attached hydrogen and do not appear in these DEPT spectra.

A carbon signal in the ordinary spectrum that is absent from all DEPT spectra is a candidate quaternary carbon. A carbonyl carbon without an attached proton is also absent from DEPT.

11. Two-dimensional NMR: correlations and connectivity

Two-dimensional NMR experiments use pulse sequences with an evolution period, an incremented delay and signal detection. After two-dimensional Fourier transformation, the data are displayed against two frequency axes, often as a contour plot. Cross-peaks provide correlations between nuclei. Some experiments show through-bond connectivity; others show through-space proximity.

11.1 Homonuclear and heteronuclear experiments

Homonuclear experiments correlate the same nucleus type, for example ¹H–¹H. Heteronuclear experiments correlate different nuclei, for example ¹H–¹³C. The lecture lists COSY, TOCSY, NOESY, ROESY and INADEQUATE among autocorrelation methods and HSQC and HMBC among cross-correlation methods.

11.2 COSY — Correlation Spectroscopy

¹H–¹H COSY identifies spin-coupled proton pairs. The two axes both represent proton chemical shift. The diagonal contains the individual proton resonances. Off-diagonal contours are cross-peaks and show which proton resonances are coupled. A cross-peak is accompanied by a corresponding symmetric cross-peak on the other side of the diagonal.

Reading procedure: locate a diagonal resonance, follow across to an off-diagonal cross-peak and read the proton shift on the second axis. This identifies the coupled proton. Repeat for other cross-peaks to assemble the proton coupling network. The lecture's methyl ethyl ketone and longer-chain ketone examples illustrate how neighbouring proton groups can be linked along a chain.

11.3 HSQC — Heteronuclear Single Quantum Coherence

HSQC correlates a proton with the carbon to which it is directly attached. One axis is ¹H chemical shift and the other is ¹³C chemical shift. Each cross-peak identifies a direct, one-bond C–H connection. This allows a proton signal to be assigned to its attached carbon. Quaternary carbons do not give HSQC cross-peaks because they have no directly attached hydrogen.

11.4 HMBC — Heteronuclear Multiple Bond Correlation

HMBC shows longer-range proton–carbon couplings, commonly across two or three bonds. It often gives more cross-peaks than HSQC. It is useful for connecting fragments across carbons that have no directly attached hydrogen, including quaternary and carbonyl carbons.

11.5 Other 2D methods

  • TOCSY: shows protons belonging to the same coupled spin system.
  • NOESY: reports through-space proximity between protons; it does not simply mean that two nuclei are bonded.
  • ROESY: another through-space correlation experiment.
  • INADEQUATE: can reveal carbon–carbon connectivity, but is difficult because ¹³C has low natural abundance.

12. Full structure-elucidation workflow

  1. Start with the molecular formula. Calculate the degree of unsaturation when the formula is available.
  2. Read the ¹³C spectrum. Count unique carbon environments and identify alkyl, heteroatom-substituted, alkene, aromatic and carbonyl regions.
  3. Use DEPT-45, DEPT-90 and DEPT-135 to distinguish CH₃, CH₂, CH and quaternary carbons.
  4. Count the ¹H signals to identify proton environments.
  5. Use integration to determine the number of hydrogens represented by each signal.
  6. Use chemical shifts to assign likely proton environments.
  7. Use splitting and J values to identify neighbouring proton fragments.
  8. Use COSY to connect coupled protons, HSQC to link protons to their directly attached carbons and HMBC to connect more distant fragments.
  9. Use NOESY or ROESY when spatial proximity or stereochemical information is required.
  10. Draw a candidate structure and verify that it accounts for every atom, carbon signal, proton signal, integral, multiplicity and correlation.
Most important principle: a correct structure must explain all the available evidence. Do not assign a molecule from one chemical shift or one multiplet alone.
