Chapter 6: IUPAC Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds

Nomenclature provides a systematic way to communicate the composition and charge of a coordination entity. A correct name identifies the ligands, the central metal, its oxidation state and whether the complex ion is positive, negative or neutral.

1. Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Apply the standard order for naming ions and coordination entities.
  • Name common neutral, anionic and cationic ligands.
  • Use multiplicative prefixes correctly, including bis-, tris- and tetrakis-.
  • Calculate the oxidation state of the central metal from the overall charge.
  • Distinguish the metal-name endings used for cationic/neutral and anionic complexes.
  • Write a coordination formula from a systematic name and check its charge balance.

2. Basic principles of coordination nomenclature

A coordination compound may contain a complex ion and counter-ions outside the coordination sphere. In naming an ionic coordination compound, name the cation first and the anion second, just as for ordinary ionic salts. Within a coordination entity, name the ligands before the central metal.

The oxidation state of the metal is written in Roman numerals in parentheses immediately after the metal name. It is not the same as the overall charge on the complex ion. For example, the superscript charge on [Co(NH3)6]3+ is +3, and the oxidation state of cobalt is also +3 because ammonia is neutral. In [Fe(CN)6]4−, the metal oxidation state is +2, not −4.

Reliable naming sequence: (1) identify the ions, (2) name the cation before the anion, (3) within each complex name ligands alphabetically, (4) add ligand-count prefixes, (5) name the metal, (6) calculate and add its oxidation state in Roman numerals.

3. Order and naming of ligands

Ligand names are arranged alphabetically according to the ligand name itself. The multiplicative prefix di-, tri-, tetra- and so on is not used to decide alphabetical order. For example, ammine is listed before aqua because “ammine” begins with A and “aqua” begins with A but m comes before q.

4. Prefixes for the number of ligands

For simple ligand names, use di-, tri-, tetra-, penta- and hexa- to show the number of identical ligands. For example, two ammonia ligands are named diammine and four water ligands are named tetraaqua.

When the ligand name already contains a numerical prefix or is a complicated name, enclose the ligand name in parentheses and use bis-, tris-, tetrakis- or pentakis-. This avoids confusing the internal parts of the ligand name with the number of ligand groups.

5. Determining the oxidation state of the metal

Use the total charge of the coordination entity and the charges of all ligands. Let x represent the oxidation state of the central metal:

x + (sum of ligand charges) = charge on the complex ion
Worked example 1: [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]²⁺

Let the oxidation state of cobalt be x. Ammonia is neutral and coordinated chloride has charge −1.

x + 5(0) + (−1) = +2

x − 1 = +2; therefore x = +3.

The metal is cobalt(III).

Worked example 2: [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻

Let the oxidation state of iron be x. Each cyanido ligand has charge −1.

x + 6(−1) = −4; x − 6 = −4; therefore x = +2.

The metal is iron(II).

Worked example 3: [Cr(H₂O)₄Cl₂]⁺

Water is neutral and each chlorido ligand has charge −1.

x + 4(0) + 2(−1) = +1; x − 2 = +1; therefore x = +3.

The metal is chromium(III).

6. Naming cationic, anionic and neutral complexes

6.1 Cationic complexes

For a positively charged coordination entity, the metal name remains the normal metal name. Examples include cobalt, chromium, nickel and copper, followed by the oxidation state in Roman numerals.

  • [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃: hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.
  • [Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃: hexaaquachromium(III) chloride.
  • [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄: tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate.

6.2 Anionic complexes

When the coordination entity is negatively charged, the central metal name takes the ending “-ate”. Some metals use traditional Latin-derived forms.

6.3 Neutral complexes

Neutral coordination compounds are named as coordination entities, without naming a counter-ion. Examples include [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], diamminedichloridoplatinum(II), and [Ni(CO)₄], tetracarbonylnickel(0).

7. Naming an ionic coordination compound

Name the positive ion first and the negative ion second. If a complex ion is the anion, apply the “-ate” ending to the metal name inside that ion.

Worked example 4: [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃

The cation is [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ and the counter-ion is chloride. Ammonia is neutral, so cobalt is +3. Six ammonia ligands give hexaammine.

Name: hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.

Worked example 5: K₄[Fe(CN)₆]

Four potassium ions contribute +4, so the complex ion has charge −4. Six cyanido ligands contribute −6. Iron must therefore be +2. The complex is anionic, so iron becomes ferrate.

Name: potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II).

8. Writing formulae from names

To write a formula from a name, reverse the naming process. Identify the metal and oxidation state, translate each ligand name into its formula and charge, apply the ligand count, calculate the total charge on the coordination entity, then add counter-ions in the smallest whole-number ratio that gives an electrically neutral compound.

  1. Write the central metal symbol and ligand formulae inside square brackets.
  2. Use parentheses around polyatomic ligands where needed.
  3. Calculate the complex charge from the metal oxidation state and ligand charges.
  4. Add counter-ions outside the square brackets to balance the charge.
  5. Check that the complete compound is electrically neutral.
Worked example 6: Write the formula of hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.

Cobalt(III) is Co³⁺. Ammine is NH₃ and is neutral. Six ammine ligands give [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺. Three chloride ions balance the +3 charge.

Formula: [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃.

Worked example 7: Write the formula of potassium hexachloridoplatinate(IV).

Platinum(IV) is Pt⁴⁺. Six chlorido ligands contribute −6. The complex ion has charge −2, [PtCl₆]²⁻. Two K⁺ ions balance it.

Formula: K₂[PtCl₆].

9. Ambidentate and bridging ligands in names

An ambidentate ligand can coordinate through different donor atoms. The name should identify the donor atom when the coordination mode could be ambiguous. For example, NO₂⁻ may bind through nitrogen (nitro) or oxygen (nitrito). SCN⁻ may bind through sulfur or nitrogen; names such as thiocyanato-S and isothiocyanato-N are used to make the attachment atom clear.

A bridging ligand connects two or more metal centres. The Greek letter μ (mu) is used to indicate a bridging ligand, followed by the ligand name. For example, a chloride ligand bridging two metal centres may be written μ-chlorido. With multiple bridging ligands, an appropriate multiplicative prefix is used, such as di-μ-chlorido.

For polynuclear complexes, metal centres are identified and the bridging ligands are described before the metal names. Detailed naming can become complex, so always draw or inspect the connectivity before assigning the name.

10. Geometrical descriptors and stereochemistry

Where needed, stereochemical descriptors are placed at the beginning of the name in italics or enclosed in the accepted format. Common descriptors include cis-, trans-, fac- and mer-. Optical configurations may be identified by Δ (delta) and Λ (lambda) for suitable octahedral chelate complexes. These descriptors distinguish arrangements that have the same formula and ligand connectivity but different spatial structures.

For example, cis-diamminedichloridoplatinum(II) and trans-diamminedichloridoplatinum(II) describe different square-planar geometric isomers. The descriptor must match the actual structure; it cannot be inferred from the formula alone.

11. Common naming errors

  • Using the overall charge of the complex as the metal oxidation state without accounting for ligand charges.
  • Alphabetising ligands according to di-, tri- or tetra- rather than the ligand name.
  • Forgetting that an anionic complex uses the “-ate” form of the metal name.
  • Writing the counter-ion inside the square brackets instead of outside the coordination sphere.
  • Using ordinary “amine” for coordinated NH₃ instead of “ammine”.
  • Confusing aqua (H₂O) with hydroxido (OH⁻).
  • Leaving out stereochemical or donor-atom descriptors when they are needed to distinguish structures.

12. A systematic exam method

  1. Separate the coordination sphere from the counter-ions.
  2. Determine the charge of the complex ion using the full compound's charge balance.
  3. Calculate the metal oxidation state using ligand charges.
  4. Name the ligands and put them in alphabetical order.
  5. Choose simple multiplicative prefixes or bis-/tris-/tetrakis- where appropriate.
  6. Name the metal and add the oxidation state in Roman numerals.
  7. If the complex is anionic, change the metal name to its “-ate” form.
  8. Write cation before anion and check the final name against the formula.

13. Practice questions

1. Name [Cr(NH₃)₆]Cl₃.
2. Name K₃[Fe(CN)₆].
3. Determine the oxidation state of cobalt in [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺.
4. Name [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂].
5. Write the formula for tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate.
6. Write the formula for potassium tetracyanonickelate(II).
7. Explain why bis(ethane-1,2-diamine) is used instead of diethane-1,2-diamine.
8. State the difference between an ambidentate ligand and a bridging ligand.

14. Answers and explanations

1. Hexaamminechromium(III) chloride. Chromium is +3 because NH₃ is neutral and the complex cation has charge +3.

2. Potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III). Three K⁺ ions mean the complex charge is −3; six CN⁻ ligands give iron oxidation state +3.

3. +3. Let x − 2 = +1, so x = +3.

4. Diamminedichloridoplatinum(II). The complex is neutral and two chlorido ligands contribute −2, so Pt is +2.

5. [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄. The complex cation is +2 and sulfate is −2.

6. K₂[Ni(CN)₄]. Nickel(II) plus four cyanido ligands gives a −2 complex ion.

7. Ethane-1,2-diamine is a complex ligand name containing its own numerical locants; bis- clearly indicates two whole ligand molecules.

8. An ambidentate ligand can attach through either of two different donor atoms, usually one at a time. A bridging ligand links two or more metal centres at the same time.

15. Chapter summary

  • Ligands are named before the central metal and are arranged alphabetically by ligand name.
  • Simple ligand multiplicity uses di-, tri-, tetra- and related prefixes; complex ligand names use bis-, tris- and related forms.
  • The metal oxidation state is calculated from the overall complex charge and the charges of all ligands.
  • Anionic coordination entities use metal names ending in “-ate”, including ferrate, cuprate and argentate.
  • The cation is named before the anion in an ionic coordination compound.
  • Formula writing reverses the naming procedure and requires charge balance.
  • Ambidentate, bridging and stereochemical descriptors identify important structural details.
