1.1 What coordination chemistry studies

Coordination chemistry is the branch of inorganic chemistry concerned with compounds in which a central metal atom or ion is surrounded by molecules or ions that donate electron pairs to it. The surrounding species are called ligands. The metal–ligand arrangement is called a coordination entity or complex.

Many familiar substances are coordination compounds. Examples include the haem group in haemoglobin, chlorophyll, vitamin B12, metal-containing catalysts and analytical reagents used to identify metal ions. Their properties depend not only on the metal but also on the ligands, their arrangement and the metal–ligand bonding.

Coordination compound: a substance containing a central metal atom or ion bonded to one or more ligands through coordinate (donor–acceptor) bonds.

A coordination compound may be neutral or ionic. For example, [Ni(CO)4] is a neutral complex molecule, while [Co(NH3)6]Cl3 is an ionic compound made of the complex cation [Co(NH3)6]3+ and three chloride counter-ions.

1.2 The language of coordination compounds

TermMeaningExample
Central metalThe metal atom or ion to which ligands attach.Co3+ in [Co(NH3)6]3+
LigandAn ion or molecule that supplies a lone pair to the metal.NH3, H2O, Cl−, CN−
Donor atomThe atom in a ligand that directly forms the bond to the metal.N in NH3; O in H2O
Coordination sphereThe metal and ligands written inside square brackets.[Co(NH3)6]3+
Counter-ionAn ion outside the brackets that balances the charge of the complex ion.Cl− in [Co(NH3)6]Cl3
Coordination numberThe number of donor atoms directly bonded to the central metal.6 in [Co(NH3)6]3+
Oxidation stateThe formal charge assigned to the metal after applying ionic bookkeeping rules.+3 for Co in [Co(NH3)6]3+

Do not confuse oxidation state with coordination number. Oxidation state is a charge-accounting value. Coordination number is a count of directly attached donor atoms. They answer different questions and are often numerically different.

Coordination sphere and ionisation sphere

In [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2, the cobalt ion, five ammonia molecules and one chloride ligand are inside the square brackets. Two chloride ions are outside the brackets. The outside chloride ions act as counter-ions and can separate as ions when the salt dissolves in water. The chloride ligand inside the brackets is directly bonded to cobalt and is not simply a free chloride ion.

Figure 1.2. Square brackets identify the coordination sphere; ions written outside are counter-ions.

1.3 How the metal–ligand bond forms

A ligand must have an available electron pair that it can donate. The metal centre must be able to accept electron density into a suitable orbital or set of orbitals. In the Lewis acid–base description, the ligand is the Lewis base (electron-pair donor) and the metal centre is the Lewis acid (electron-pair acceptor).

The bond is called a coordinate covalent bond or dative bond because both electrons in the shared pair originate from the donor ligand at the moment the bond is formed. Once formed, it behaves as a covalent metal–ligand bond; the arrow is a useful way of showing the direction of donation, not a claim that the bond remains fundamentally different from other covalent bonds.

Figure 1.3. A ligand lone pair is donated to an electron-deficient metal centre.

Metal–ligand bonding is more fully described using molecular orbital theory and crystal field approaches. Those models are developed in Chapter 4. The Lewis picture is the starting point because it makes the direction of electron-pair donation clear.

1.4 Werner's coordination theory

Alfred Werner proposed his coordination theory in 1893 to explain why certain metal salts containing ammonia and chloride did not behave like ordinary ionic salts. His experiments showed that a metal could satisfy two different kinds of valency.

Primary valency

Primary valency corresponds broadly to the modern oxidation state of the metal. In Werner's model it was ionisable and non-directional. It was commonly satisfied by negative ions. Ions satisfying only primary valency were written outside the coordination sphere.

Secondary valency

Secondary valency corresponds to the modern coordination number. It is satisfied by a fixed number of ligands or donor atoms directly attached to the metal. Werner recognised that secondary valencies have definite spatial directions, so they help determine the shape of a complex.

Modern translation: Werner's primary valency ≈ oxidation state; Werner's secondary valency ≈ coordination number. This is a useful historical correspondence, not a complete modern theory of bonding.

The cobalt(III)–ammonia–chloride series

Werner studied compounds formed from cobalt(III) chloride and ammonia. Their formulas, colours and reactions with silver nitrate indicated that different numbers of chloride ions were free in solution.

1.5 Experimental evidence for Werner's theory

1.5.1 Precipitation with silver nitrate

Aqueous silver nitrate provides Ag+ ions, which react with free chloride ions to form white silver chloride precipitate:

Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s)

Only chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are immediately available as free chloride ions in solution. The coordinated chloride is bonded to cobalt and is not precipitated as though it were already a free chloride ion.

Complex saltImmediate AgCl per formula unitReason
[Co(NH3)6]Cl33 molThree counter-ion chlorides
[Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl22 molTwo counter-ion chlorides
[Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl1 molOne counter-ion chloride
[Co(NH3)3Cl3]0 molNo counter-ion chloride

1.5.2 Electrical conductance

When an ionic compound dissolves, its mobile ions carry electric current. Under comparable conditions, a solution that produces more ions generally has a greater molar conductivity than one producing fewer ions, although ion charge, size, mobility and concentration also affect the measured value.

The cobalt series produces different numbers of ions on dissolution. For example:

  • [Co(NH3)6]Cl3 → [Co(NH3)6]3+ + 3Cl−
  • [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2 → [Co(NH3)5Cl]2+ + 2Cl−
  • [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl → [Co(NH3)4Cl2]+ + Cl−
  • [Co(NH3)3Cl3] is a neutral molecular complex and does not dissociate into counter-ions in this simple representation.

The conductance trend therefore supports the proposed number of ions and the distinction between the inner and outer coordination spheres.

1.5.3 Cryoscopic measurements

Freezing-point depression is a colligative property: it depends on the number of dissolved particles rather than their chemical identity. For dilute solutions, the relationship is expressed as ΔTf = iKfm, where i is the van ’t Hoff factor, Kf is the solvent's cryoscopic constant and m is molality. A larger number of independently moving solute particles gives a larger freezing-point depression under comparable conditions. This provided another way to estimate how many ions a salt produced in solution.

Interpretation: silver nitrate tests free chloride directly; conductivity measures the behaviour of mobile ions; cryoscopy estimates the number of dissolved particles. Together, these methods supported Werner's structural proposals.

1.5.4 Geometrical evidence

Werner also considered the number of possible arrangements of ligands around a metal. The observed number of isomers for suitable coordination compounds helped distinguish possible geometries. Coordination number six is commonly octahedral, while coordination number four commonly gives tetrahedral or square-planar arrangements. The detailed treatment of isomerism is reserved for Chapter 2.

1.6 Sidgwick's effective atomic number (EAN) concept

Sidgwick proposed that a metal in a stable complex may attain an electron count resembling that of a nearby noble gas. This is called the effective atomic number (EAN) rule. It is a useful electron-counting guide for some complexes, but it is not a universal law and does not by itself prove that a complex is stable.

EAN = atomic number of metal − oxidation state + electrons donated by ligands

In the simple counting convention used here, each donor atom contributes one electron pair, or two electrons, to the metal's count.

Worked example 1: [Co(NH3)6]3+
  1. Atomic number of cobalt = 27.
  2. Oxidation state of cobalt = +3.
  3. Six NH3 ligands donate 6 × 2 = 12 electrons.
  4. EAN = 27 − 3 + 12 = 36.
Thirty-six is the atomic number of krypton. The complex satisfies the EAN count.
Worked example 2: [Fe(CN)6]4−
  1. Let the oxidation state of iron be x: x + 6(−1) = −4, so x = +2.
  2. Atomic number of Fe = 26.
  3. Six cyanide ligands donate 12 electrons in this counting method.
  4. EAN = 26 − 2 + 12 = 36, the krypton count.
Limit of the EAN rule: many stable coordination compounds do not attain a noble-gas EAN. Modern explanations of bonding and stability require orbital energies, electron configuration, ligand field effects, metal–ligand covalency and thermodynamics.

1.7 Ligands and their classification

A ligand is an ion or molecule that binds to a central metal through one or more donor atoms. A donor atom usually possesses a lone pair that can be donated to the metal. Ligands may be neutral or negatively charged; some positively charged ligands also exist.

1.7.1 Classification by denticity

Denticity is the number of donor atoms from one ligand that attach to the same central metal ion. It is not the total number of atoms in the ligand.

Figure 1.5. Denticity counts the donor atoms from a single ligand that bind to one metal centre.

1.7.2 Classification by charge

  • Neutral ligands: NH3 (ammine), H2O (aqua), CO (carbonyl) and NO (nitrosyl; bonding can be more complex).
  • Anionic ligands: Cl− (chlorido), OH− (hydroxido), CN− (cyanido), C2O42− (oxalato).
  • Cationic ligands: less common examples include protonated nitrogen-containing donor species in specialised complexes.

1.7.3 Ambidentate ligands

An ambidentate ligand has two different possible donor atoms but attaches through only one of them at a time in a given bonding mode. Examples include NO2−, which can bind through nitrogen (nitro) or oxygen (nitrito), and SCN−, which can bind through sulfur or nitrogen.

Figure 1.6. Two possible attachment modes of nitrite. The drawing is schematic; actual bond orders and geometry depend on the complex.

1.7.4 Bridging ligands

A bridging ligand connects two or more metal centres. Such ligands are indicated by the prefix μ (mu), for example μ-chlorido. Bridging is different from denticity: denticity describes how one ligand attaches to one metal centre, while bridging describes a ligand shared between metal centres.

1.8 Chelation and the chelate effect

A polydentate ligand that attaches to the same metal through two or more donor atoms forms one or more rings that include the metal. This process is called chelation, and the resulting ring-containing complex is called a chelate.

For example, ethane-1,2-diamine (en), H2N–CH2–CH2–NH2, has two nitrogen donor atoms. Both can bind to one metal centre, forming a five-membered chelate ring when the metal is included in the ring.

Figure 1.7. A bidentate ligand uses two donor atoms to bind the same metal. The simplified sketch illustrates ring closure rather than exact bond angles.

The chelate effect is the general observation that complexes with suitable multidentate ligands are often more thermodynamically stable than comparable complexes containing the same number of similar monodentate donor groups. A major contribution is often entropy: replacing several separate monodentate ligands with one multidentate ligand can increase the number of free particles released to solution. Enthalpy, ring size, ligand strain, solvent and metal–donor matching also matter.

Important distinction: a complex being thermodynamically stable does not necessarily mean it forms quickly or reacts slowly. Thermodynamic stability and kinetic inertness are different ideas. They will be treated in the later chapter on stability and reaction mechanisms.

1.9 Coordination number and geometry

The coordination number (CN) is the number of donor atoms directly attached to the central metal. Count donor atoms, not the number of ligand molecules. A bidentate ligand contributes two to the coordination number; a hexadentate ligand can contribute six.

4Tetrahedral or square planarTwo common alternatives; metal identity and electronic structure influence the choice.
5Trigonal bipyramidal or square pyramidalTwo common five-coordinate arrangements.
6OctahedralSix donor atoms around the metal, commonly with 90° and 180° relationships.
7–8Various polyhedraExamples include pentagonal bipyramidal and square-antiprismatic arrangements.
Figure 1.8. Common coordination geometries. The tetrahedral and octahedral drawings are schematic projections of three-dimensional structures.

Why do coordination numbers differ?

No single factor determines coordination number. Important influences include:

  • Size of the metal ion: larger metal ions can often accommodate more donor atoms, provided other factors allow it.
  • Size of the ligand: bulky ligands occupy more space and can reduce the number that fit around a metal.
  • Metal oxidation state and electronic configuration: these influence metal–ligand attraction, orbital occupancy and preferred geometry.
  • Ligand charge and donor strength: these affect electrostatic and covalent interactions.
  • Solvent and reaction conditions: solvent, temperature, concentration and competing ligands can alter which complex forms.
  • Chelation: multidentate ligands can occupy several coordination sites at once and often favour particular arrangements.

1.10 Geometry, bonding and stability: ideas to carry forward

Coordination geometry is not simply a drawing convention. It affects orbital interactions, magnetic behaviour, colour, spectra and reactivity. For example, octahedral and tetrahedral ligand arrangements split the metal d orbitals in different ways. This is why the geometry introduced here becomes essential when studying crystal field theory and electronic spectra.

Likewise, ligand identity matters. Strong-field and weak-field ligands can produce different electron arrangements in some metal complexes. Some ligands form particularly stable chelate rings, while other ligands exchange rapidly. These ideas will be developed in the chapters on metal–ligand bonding, magnetism, colour and complex stability.

1.11 Worked examples

Example 1. Determine oxidation state and coordination number in [Cr(H₂O)₄Cl₂]⁺.
  1. Let chromium oxidation state be x.
  2. Water is neutral and each chlorido ligand is −1: x + 4(0) + 2(−1) = +1.
  3. x − 2 = +1, therefore x = +3.
  4. Four water oxygen atoms and two chloride donor atoms are attached: CN = 4 + 2 = 6.
Example 2. Find the coordination number in [Fe(en)₂Cl₂]⁺.
  1. Each en ligand is bidentate and contributes two donor atoms.
  2. Two en ligands contribute 2 × 2 = 4 donor atoms.
  3. Two chlorido ligands contribute 2 × 1 = 2 donor atoms.
  4. Total CN = 4 + 2 = 6.

Do not count this as four ligands and conclude that the coordination number is four. Count the donor atoms.

Example 3. How many chloride ions are immediately precipitated from [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl?

Only the chloride outside the brackets is a counter-ion. One mole of the salt therefore gives one mole of AgCl immediately per mole of complex salt, assuming complete precipitation and no side reactions.

Example 4. Calculate the EAN of [Ni(CO)₄].
  1. Nickel atomic number = 28.
  2. CO is neutral, so nickel oxidation state = 0.
  3. Four CO ligands donate 4 × 2 = 8 electrons in the EAN method.
  4. EAN = 28 − 0 + 8 = 36, the krypton count.

1.12 Chapter review

Key points

  • A coordination entity contains a central metal and attached ligands.
  • Ligands donate electron pairs; the metal centre accepts them.
  • The coordination sphere is written inside square brackets.
  • Oxidation state and coordination number are different quantities.
  • Werner's primary valency corresponds broadly to oxidation state; secondary valency corresponds to coordination number.
  • Silver nitrate precipitates free chloride ions as AgCl.
  • Conductivity and freezing-point depression provide evidence about the number of ions or particles in solution.
  • EAN is a useful electron-counting guideline, not a universal stability rule.
  • Denticity counts the donor atoms of one ligand attached to a metal.
  • Polydentate ligands can form chelate rings; ambidentate ligands have alternative donor atoms.
  • Coordination number counts donor atoms, not ligand molecules.

Practice questions

  1. Define a coordination compound and distinguish it from a simple ionic salt.
  2. Explain the Lewis acid–base roles of the metal centre and ligand during complex formation.
  3. For [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂, identify the coordination sphere, counter-ions, oxidation state of cobalt and coordination number.
  4. Write the ionisation equation for [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl in water and predict the immediate amount of AgCl formed by excess AgNO₃ per mole of salt.
  5. State Werner's primary and secondary valencies and give their modern equivalents.
  6. Explain how conductance measurements supported Werner's theory.
  7. Calculate the EAN of [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻. (First determine the oxidation state of iron.)
  8. Classify NH₃, oxalate, en, dien and EDTA by denticity.
  9. Explain the difference between a bidentate ligand and an ambidentate ligand.
  10. Determine the oxidation state and coordination number of [Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺.
  11. Give two common geometries for coordination number four and describe one factor that can influence which geometry is adopted.
  12. Explain why the chelate effect is often associated with an entropy advantage.
