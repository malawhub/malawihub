Chapter 3 — Theories of Metal–Ligand Bonding

This chapter explains how ligands interact with a central metal ion, how the five d-orbitals change in energy when a complex forms and how those changes help us understand geometry, magnetism, colour and stability.

3.1 Why are bonding theories needed?

A coordination compound forms when ligands donate electron pairs to a central metal atom or ion. However, simply saying that a coordinate bond forms does not fully explain the observed properties of complexes. Chemists therefore use bonding theories to interpret how the metal and ligands interact.

The reference text introduces three main approaches:

  • Valence Bond Theory (VBT): uses hybridisation and orbital overlap to describe coordinate bonding and predict possible geometries.
  • Crystal Field Theory (CFT): treats the ligand environment mainly through electrostatic interactions and explains the splitting of metal d-orbital energies.
  • Ligand Field Theory (LFT) / Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT): gives a more complete orbital description that includes covalent metal–ligand interactions.
Important distinction: VBT focuses on hybrid orbitals and bond formation; CFT focuses on the effect of the ligand field on d-orbital energies; LFT/MOT combines orbital-energy ideas with metal–ligand orbital interactions.

3.2 Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

Valence Bond Theory, developed mainly through the work of Linus Pauling, describes a coordinate bond as the overlap of a filled orbital on a ligand with a vacant hybrid orbital on the metal.

Main assumptions of VBT

  1. The central metal ion makes a number of vacant orbitals available for bonding. The number of orbitals used is equal to the coordination number.
  2. Suitable metal orbitals mix to form hybrid orbitals. These hybrid orbitals have definite orientations in space.
  3. A ligand donates a lone pair into an empty metal hybrid orbital. The overlap produces a coordinate covalent bond.
  4. The coordination number and the type of hybridisation are related to the geometry of the complex.
  5. In the traditional VBT model, strong ligands may cause pairing of metal d-electrons, while weak ligands generally do not cause pairing.
  6. The number of unpaired d-electrons is used to predict whether a complex is paramagnetic or diamagnetic.
Figure 3.1. A ligand donates a lone pair into an available metal orbital.

Strong and weak ligands in the traditional VBT model

In the VBT description used in the reference notes, ligands are broadly grouped according to their tendency to promote pairing of metal d-electrons. Strong ligands such as CN⁻ and CO are commonly associated with electron pairing. Weak-field ligands such as F⁻ and Cl⁻ are commonly associated with little or no pairing. This is a simplified model; the more systematic explanation of ligand strength is developed by CFT and LFT.

Do not confuse the models: VBT uses hybridisation labels such as d²sp³ or sp³d². CFT instead describes electrons occupying split d-orbital sets, such as t₂g and eg. These are different descriptions of bonding.

3.3 Hybridisation and coordination geometry

Hybridisation is the mixing of atomic orbitals of suitable energies on the same atom to form a new set of orbitals. In VBT, the direction of these hybrid orbitals helps determine the positions of the ligands.

An outer-orbital octahedral complex uses the outer nd orbitals to form sp³d² hybrids.

3.4 Applying VBT to coordination compounds

Example A: [Co(CN)₆]³⁻

  1. Each CN⁻ has charge −1. Let the oxidation state of cobalt be x: x + 6(−1) = −3, so x = +3.
  2. Co has atomic number 27. Co³⁺ has the d⁶ configuration.
  3. CN⁻ is treated as a strong ligand in the traditional VBT approach and promotes pairing of the six d-electrons.
  4. Two 3d orbitals become available, and they combine with one 4s and three 4p orbitals to form d²sp³ hybrid orbitals.
  5. Six ligand lone pairs occupy the six hybrid orbitals.

VBT prediction: octahedral, inner-orbital complex, with all d-electrons paired and diamagnetic.

Example B: [CoF₆]³⁻

  1. Cobalt has oxidation state +3 and therefore d⁶.
  2. F⁻ is treated as a weak ligand in the VBT model and does not promote pairing of the d-electrons.
  3. The outer 4s and 4p orbitals together with two 4d orbitals form sp³d² hybrids.
  4. The complex is described as an outer-orbital octahedral complex.

VBT prediction: high-spin d⁶ with four unpaired electrons and paramagnetism.

Example C: [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻

  1. Let the oxidation state of Ni be x: x + 4(−1) = −2, hence x = +2.
  2. Ni²⁺ has configuration d⁸.
  3. CN⁻ is a strong ligand and promotes pairing in the traditional VBT description.
  4. One 3d orbital, one 4s orbital and two 4p orbitals form dsp² hybrid orbitals.

VBT prediction: square planar and diamagnetic.

Example D: [NiCl₄]²⁻

Ni²⁺ is d⁸, but Cl⁻ is a weak ligand. The VBT model predicts sp³ hybridisation, a tetrahedral geometry and two unpaired electrons, so the complex is paramagnetic.

Reliable order for VBT questions: calculate oxidation state → write d-electron count → assess ligand strength → decide whether pairing occurs → identify hybridisation → state geometry → count unpaired electrons.

3.5 Limitations of Valence Bond Theory

  • It does not provide a satisfactory quantitative explanation of why one ligand is stronger than another.
  • It does not explain the origin or magnitude of d-orbital energy splitting.
  • It cannot adequately explain the colours and electronic spectra of coordination compounds.
  • It gives a basic account of magnetic behaviour but does not fully explain magnetic susceptibility and its temperature dependence.
  • It does not satisfactorily explain distortions from ideal geometries in many complexes.
  • It gives limited explanations of the relative stability and reaction rates of complexes.
  • Some observed structures and magnetic properties are not adequately explained by simple hybridisation assignments.

These limitations lead to the crystal field approach, which focuses on the effect of ligand arrangement on the energies of the metal d-orbitals.

3.6 Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

Crystal Field Theory was developed from the work of physicists including Hans Bethe and John Hasbrouck van Vleck. It describes the interaction between a metal ion and surrounding ligands mainly in electrostatic terms.

Main assumptions of the simple CFT model

  1. Ligands are treated as negative point charges when they are anions, or as point dipoles when they are neutral polar molecules such as H₂O or NH₃.
  2. The central metal ion is treated as a positive ion containing d-electrons.
  3. Repulsion between ligand electron density and metal d-electrons raises the energies of the metal d-orbitals.
  4. In a free metal ion, the five d-orbitals have the same energy. They are described as degenerate.
  5. When ligands approach in a particular geometry, they repel different d-orbitals by different amounts. The degeneracy is removed and the d-orbitals split into energy groups.
Crystal-field splitting, Δ: the energy difference between the d-orbital groups created when ligands surround a metal ion. The symbol Δ is given a subscript for the geometry, for example Δₒ for octahedral and Δₜ for tetrahedral splitting.

The simple CFT model is useful, but its assumption of purely electrostatic bonding is an approximation. Real metal–ligand bonds can have covalent character, which is treated more fully by ligand field theory.

3.7 The five d-orbitals and their orientations

The five d-orbitals are dxy, dxz, dyz, dx²−y² and dz². Four have four-lobed shapes. The dz² orbital has two main lobes along the z-axis and a doughnut-shaped region around its centre.

  • dx²−y²: lobes point along the x- and y-axes.
  • dz²: lobes point along the z-axis.
  • dxy: lobes lie between the x- and y-axes.
  • dxz: lobes lie between the x- and z-axes.
  • dyz: lobes lie between the y- and z-axes.
Figure 3.3. Highly simplified orbital orientation sketches; the actual d-orbital shapes are three-dimensional.

In an octahedral complex, six ligands approach along the positive and negative x-, y- and z-axes. Orbitals directed at the ligands experience greater electrostatic repulsion than orbitals directed between the axes.

3.8 Crystal-field splitting in octahedral complexes

For an octahedral complex, the metal ion is at the centre and six ligands approach along the coordinate axes. The dx²−y² and dz² orbitals point directly towards the ligands and experience greater repulsion. They form the higher-energy eg set. The dxy, dxz and dyz orbitals point between the axes and form the lower-energy t2g set.

Figure 3.4. Octahedral splitting: the eg set is higher and the t2g set is lower in energy.

The energy separation between the two sets is called Δₒ (also written 10Dq). Relative to the barycentre:

  • Each electron in t2g contributes −0.4Δₒ.
  • Each electron in eg contributes +0.6Δₒ.

The total energy of the five orbitals is conserved relative to the barycentre: three lower orbitals each move down by 0.4Δₒ, giving −1.2Δₒ; two upper orbitals each move up by 0.6Δₒ, giving +1.2Δₒ. The changes cancel.

3.9 Crystal-field splitting in tetrahedral complexes

In a tetrahedral complex, four ligands approach the metal from directions between the Cartesian axes. The d-orbitals therefore split in the opposite order to the octahedral case.

  • The dxy, dxz and dyz orbitals form the higher-energy t2 set.
  • The dz² and dx²−y² orbitals form the lower-energy e set.
Figure 3.5. Tetrahedral splitting has the reverse ordering to octahedral splitting.

Relative to the barycentre, each electron in e contributes −0.6Δₜ and each electron in t2 contributes +0.4Δₜ. For comparable metal–ligand systems, Δₜ is much smaller than Δₒ. A commonly used approximation is:

Approximate relationship: Δₜ ≈ 4/9 Δₒ for comparable metal ions and ligands.

Because tetrahedral splitting is usually small compared with the energy needed to pair electrons, tetrahedral complexes are generally high spin.

3.10 Spectrochemical series and factors affecting splitting

Ligands do not all produce the same size of d-orbital splitting. The experimentally observed order of increasing ligand-field strength is called the spectrochemical series. A simplified sequence from weaker to stronger ligands is:

Illustrative spectrochemical series:
I⁻ < Br⁻ < S²⁻ < SCN⁻ (S-bound) < Cl⁻ < NO₃⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ (N-bound) < py < NH₃ < en < bpy/phen < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO.

The exact position of some ligands can depend on the metal ion, oxidation state and bonding description. Treat this as a useful general order, not an absolute rule for every possible complex.

Factors that affect the magnitude of Δ

  1. Nature of the ligand: strong-field ligands produce a larger splitting than weak-field ligands.
  2. Oxidation state of the metal: a higher positive charge generally strengthens metal–ligand attraction and often increases splitting.
  3. Identity and period of the metal: for analogous complexes, 4d and 5d metals commonly show larger splitting than 3d metals.
  4. Geometry: octahedral, tetrahedral and square-planar environments produce different splitting patterns.
  5. Metal–ligand distance: shorter distances generally strengthen the interaction and increase splitting.

3.11 High-spin and low-spin complexes

When d-electrons occupy split orbitals, two competing energy effects matter. Electrons can occupy a higher-energy orbital rather than pair in a lower-energy orbital, or they can pair in the lower set if the splitting is sufficiently large.

Pairing energy (P): the energy cost associated with placing two electrons in the same orbital rather than keeping them in separate orbitals, including the relevant electron–electron repulsion effects.
  • High spin: occurs when the energy required to occupy the upper set is relatively small compared with the pairing energy. Electrons occupy separate orbitals as far as possible, giving the maximum number of unpaired electrons.
  • Low spin: occurs when the splitting is sufficiently large compared with the pairing energy. Electrons pair in the lower set before occupying the upper set, producing fewer unpaired electrons.

For octahedral d⁴ to d⁷ configurations, the relative size of Δₒ and P is especially important. d⁰ to d³ and d⁸ to d¹⁰ do not show the same high-spin/low-spin choice between t2g and eg in the simple octahedral filling pattern.

3.12 Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy (CFSE)

CFSE is the net change in energy of the d-electrons caused by their occupation of the split orbitals, measured relative to the barycentre. It is calculated by adding the contributions from the lower- and higher-energy sets.

Octahedral complexes

CFSE (octahedral) = (−0.4nt₂g + 0.6ne_g)Δₒ + mP

Here nt₂g is the number of electrons in t₂g, ne_g is the number in eg, m is the number of additional electron pairs formed because of the ligand-field arrangement, and P is the pairing-energy contribution. Some textbooks report CFSE separately from pairing energy, so always state which convention you are using.

Tetrahedral complexes

CFSE (tetrahedral) = (−0.6ne + 0.4nt₂)Δₜ

In the usual tetrahedral case, complexes are high spin and the electron distribution does not involve the same low-spin pairing choice found in octahedral d⁴–d⁷ complexes.

Calculation example: octahedral d³

The three electrons occupy the lower t₂g orbitals singly: t₂g³eg⁰.

Calculation:
CFSE = (−0.4 × 3 + 0.6 × 0)Δₒ
= −1.2Δₒ.
There is no pairing contribution from these three singly occupied orbitals.

Calculation example: high-spin octahedral d⁶

The configuration is t₂g⁴eg².

Calculation:
Orbital contribution = (−0.4 × 4 + 0.6 × 2)Δₒ
= (−1.6 + 1.2)Δₒ
= −0.4Δₒ.
There is one pair in t₂g in the high-spin arrangement. When comparing total energies, include the appropriate pairing-energy term according to the convention specified in the question.

Calculation example: low-spin octahedral d⁶

The configuration is t₂g⁶eg⁰.

Calculation:
Orbital contribution = (−0.4 × 6 + 0.6 × 0)Δₒ
= −2.4Δₒ.
There are three electron pairs in t₂g. Compared with the high-spin d⁶ arrangement, two additional pairs have been formed, so the extra pairing cost must be considered when comparing the two states.
Common CFSE error: do not count every electron pair as a new pairing penalty. The correct comparison counts the additional pairing caused by the ligand-field arrangement relative to the reference free-ion arrangement or the convention used in your course.

3.13 Jahn–Teller distortion

The Jahn–Teller theorem states that a non-linear complex in an electronically degenerate state can lower its energy by distorting so that the degeneracy is removed. In coordination chemistry, this often appears as a distortion of an octahedral complex.

A common case is tetragonal distortion, in which the two ligands on the z-axis move relative to the four ligands in the xy-plane.

  • Tetragonal elongation: the two axial ligands move farther from the metal. Interactions with orbitals having z-character become weaker.
  • Tetragonal compression: the two axial ligands move closer to the metal. Interactions with orbitals having z-character become stronger.
Figure 3.6. Conceptual comparison of a regular octahedron and tetragonal distortions.

Distortion is especially noticeable when the eg orbitals are unevenly occupied because they point directly towards the ligands. Uneven occupation of t₂g orbitals can also contribute, although the effect is often smaller. Octahedral Cu²⁺ (d⁹) complexes are common examples of strong tetragonal elongation.

3.14 Ligand Field Theory and Molecular Orbital Theory

Crystal Field Theory is valuable for understanding d-orbital splitting, but its simple electrostatic model treats metal–ligand bonding as purely ionic. In real complexes, metal and ligand orbitals can overlap and mix. Ligand Field Theory (LFT) extends the crystal-field picture by including covalent orbital interactions. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) provides the orbital framework used to describe this mixing.

Basic molecular-orbital idea

When metal orbitals and ligand group orbitals have suitable symmetry and comparable energies, they can combine to form molecular orbitals spread over the metal and ligand framework. Combination produces bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals. Orbitals with incompatible symmetry do not mix effectively.

  • Bonding molecular orbital: lower in energy than the combining orbitals; electron occupation stabilises the complex.
  • Antibonding molecular orbital: higher in energy; electron occupation reduces the net bonding stabilisation.
  • Non-bonding orbital: an orbital that has little or no effective interaction with the ligand group orbitals.

In an octahedral complex, ligand σ-donor orbitals combine into symmetry-adapted ligand group orbitals. These interact with metal orbitals of matching symmetry. The metal d-orbitals that point directly towards ligands have strong σ interactions, while d-orbitals between the axes may remain largely non-bonding in a σ-only model. π-donor or π-acceptor ligands can further change the relative energies of the metal-based orbitals.

Why LFT/MOT is more complete

  • It explains why metal–ligand bonds can have both ionic and covalent character.
  • It gives a framework for understanding σ donation and π bonding.
  • It helps explain why π-acceptor ligands such as CO and CN⁻ often produce strong ligand fields.
  • It gives a more complete account of electronic spectra, bonding and trends in complex stability.
Exam distinction: CFT is an electrostatic model; LFT/MOT includes orbital overlap and covalent mixing. LFT does not simply mean another name for the basic CFT model.

3.15 Worked examples

Example 1 — oxidation state and d count in [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻

Let the oxidation state of Fe be x. x + 6(−1) = −4, so x = +2. Fe²⁺ is d⁶. CN⁻ is a strong-field ligand, so the octahedral complex is low spin in the usual CFT description: t₂g⁶eg⁰. It has zero unpaired electrons and is diamagnetic.

Example 2 — unpaired electrons in high-spin octahedral d⁵

Fill t₂g and eg singly before pairing: t₂g³eg². All five d-electrons remain unpaired. The complex is paramagnetic.

Example 3 — tetrahedral d⁸ CFSE

For tetrahedral d⁸, the lower e set contains four electrons and the upper t₂ set contains four electrons: e⁴t₂⁴.

CFSE = [−0.6(4) + 0.4(4)]Δₜ = (−2.4 + 1.6)Δₜ = −0.8Δₜ.

Example 4 — spin state of [CoF₆]³⁻

Co is +3, so it is d⁶. F⁻ is weak field and the octahedral splitting is relatively small. The expected configuration is high-spin t₂g⁴eg², with four unpaired electrons. The complex is paramagnetic.

Example 5 — spin state of [Co(CN)₆]³⁻

Co is +3 and d⁶. CN⁻ is strong field, giving low-spin t₂g⁶eg⁰. All electrons are paired, so the complex is diamagnetic.
