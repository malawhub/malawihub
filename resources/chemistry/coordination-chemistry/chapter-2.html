Chapter 2 — Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

2.1 Meaning and classification of isomerism

Isomers are compounds that have the same overall chemical composition but differ in the way their atoms, ions or ligands are connected or arranged in space. Because their structures are different, isomers may also differ in colour, solubility, melting point, dipole moment, reactivity or biological activity.

Isomerism: the existence of two or more compounds with the same chemical formula but different structures or spatial arrangements.

In coordination chemistry, the central metal and the ligands may be the same while the compounds still differ. One ligand may occupy a different position, attach through a different donor atom or be located inside rather than outside the coordination sphere.

Figure 2.1. Broad classification of coordination-compound isomerism.

Structural (constitutional) isomers differ in which species are bonded to the metal or in how the coordination entity is assembled. Stereoisomers have the same metal–ligand connectivity but differ in the three-dimensional positions of the ligands.

2.2 Structural isomerism

Structural isomerism occurs when the same overall composition can be arranged with different bonding relationships. The important forms covered here are ionisation, hydrate, linkage and coordination isomerism. Some classifications also discuss coordination-position and ligand isomerism, while empirical-formula similarities are sometimes described as polymerisation isomerism.

2.3 Ionisation isomerism

Ionisation isomers arise when an anion inside the coordination sphere exchanges places with a counter-ion outside the brackets. The two compounds therefore produce different ions when dissolved in water and can give different reactions with suitable test reagents.

Example:
[Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ and [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br have the same overall composition. In the first compound, sulfate is outside the coordination sphere and can be detected in solution with Ba²⁺ as a white BaSO₄ precipitate. In the second compound, bromide is the counter-ion and can give a cream precipitate of AgBr with AgNO₃. The sulfate group in the second compound is coordinated to cobalt.
Figure 2.2. Ionisation isomers: the species inside and outside the brackets are exchanged.

How to recognise it: compare the formulae inside the square brackets. If an anion that is coordinated in one formula appears outside the brackets in the other, and the counter-ion moves inside, ionisation isomerism is indicated.

2.4 Hydrate isomerism

Hydrate isomerism is a special case in which water molecules are distributed differently between the coordination sphere and the water of crystallisation. The total number of water molecules in the formula remains the same, but the number directly bonded to the metal changes.

A classic series is represented by chromium(III) chloride hexahydrate. Commonly described forms include [Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃, [Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O and [Cr(H₂O)₄Cl₂]Cl·2H₂O. The colours and number of immediately ionisable chloride ions differ.

FormWater inside sphereChloride outside sphereExpected AgCl per formula unit
[Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃633 mol
[Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O522 mol
[Cr(H₂O)₄Cl₂]Cl·2H₂O411 mol

When a solution is treated with silver nitrate, only the chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are immediately available to precipitate as AgCl. Chloride ligands already bonded to chromium are not counted as free counter-ions in this simple test.

Exam point: water inside square brackets is coordinated water; water written after a dot is water of crystallisation. Do not count both as if they have the same role.

2.5 Linkage isomerism

Linkage isomerism occurs when an ambidentate ligand can attach to the same metal through either of two different donor atoms. The ligand composition stays the same, but the metal–donor-atom connection changes.

LigandPossible donor atomsNames used in complexes
Nitrite, NO₂⁻N or ON-bound nitro; O-bound nitrito
Thiocyanate, SCN⁻S or NS-bound thiocyanato-S; N-bound thiocyanato-N / isothiocyanato
Cyanide, CN⁻C or NC-bound cyano; N-bound isocyano

For nitrite, the N-bound and O-bound forms are often called nitro and nitrito forms respectively. For thiocyanate, the name should identify whether sulfur or nitrogen is bonded to the metal. Always inspect the donor atom rather than relying only on the ligand formula.

2.6 Coordination isomerism and other structural forms

Coordination isomerism

Coordination isomerism is possible when both the cation and anion are complex ions. Ligands are redistributed between the two metal coordination entities. A representative pair is [Co(NH₃)₆][Cr(CN)₆] and [Cr(NH₃)₆][Co(CN)₆]. The metal–ligand combinations have been exchanged between the complex cation and complex anion.

Coordination-position isomerism

In a polynuclear or bridged complex containing more than one metal centre, ligands may occupy different positions around the different metal centres while the overall composition remains unchanged. The exact structural comparison depends on the particular bridged system being considered.

Ligand isomerism

Ligand isomerism occurs when ligands themselves have the same molecular formula but different structures, and those different ligand structures are incorporated into the coordination compounds. The difference lies in the constitution of the ligand rather than a change in donor atom of one ambidentate ligand.

Polymerisation isomerism: a terminology caution

Some older coordination-chemistry classifications use the term polymerisation isomerism for compounds that have the same empirical formula but different molecular formulae or different numbers of metal-containing units. In modern usage, this is not always treated as true isomerism because the molecular formulae are not identical. Follow the terminology required by your course or examiner and state the distinction clearly.

2.7 Stereoisomerism and geometrical isomerism

Stereoisomers have the same molecular formula and the same connectivity between metal and ligands, but differ in the spatial arrangement of the ligands. In coordination chemistry, the two major forms are geometrical isomerism and optical isomerism.

Geometrical isomerism occurs when ligands occupy different relative positions around a metal centre. The terms cis and trans describe whether a selected pair of ligands are adjacent or opposite. In an octahedral complex, fac and mer describe the arrangements of three identical ligands.

Figure 2.4. Simplified square-planar cis/trans comparison. The two A ligands are next to each other in cis and opposite each other in trans.

2.8 Geometrical isomerism in square-planar complexes

Square-planar complexes have four ligand positions in one plane. A common example is a complex of the type [MA₂B₂]. It can have two arrangements:

  • cis-[MA₂B₂]: the two A ligands are adjacent (90° apart).
  • trans-[MA₂B₂]: the two A ligands are opposite (180° apart).

For example, [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] has cis and trans forms. The cis form is cisplatin, a well-known anticancer coordination compound. The trans form has a different spatial arrangement and does not have the same therapeutic use.

Not every square-planar formula has geometrical isomers. If all four positions are equivalent under rotation, as in a complex with four identical ligands, there is only one arrangement. A complex of the type [MA₃B] also has only one square-planar arrangement because the single B ligand can be placed at any one position by rotation of the whole square.

Counting strategy: for square-planar [MA₂B₂], there are two geometrical isomers, not three or four. Rotating the whole square does not create a new compound.

2.9 Geometrical isomerism in octahedral complexes

An octahedral complex has six coordination positions. A useful way to reason about it is to imagine three pairs of opposite positions. Two identical ligands are trans if they occupy opposite positions and cis if they occupy adjacent positions.

[MA₄B₂] type

This type gives cis and trans isomers. In cis-[MA₄B₂], the two B ligands are adjacent; in trans-[MA₄B₂], they are opposite. For example, [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺ can be considered in these two arrangements.

[MA₃B₃] type: facial and meridional

Octahedral [MA₃B₃] complexes can exist as fac and mer forms. In the facial (fac) isomer, the three identical A ligands occupy the corners of one triangular face. Every A–M–A angle is 90°. In the meridional (mer) isomer, the three A ligands lie around a plane passing through the metal; two A ligands are trans (180°) and the other two A–M–A relationships are cis (90°).

Figure 2.5. Conceptual comparison of facial and meridional placement in an octahedral [MA₃B₃] complex.

Complexes with chelating ligands

Bidentate ligands occupy two coordination positions through two donor atoms. In octahedral complexes containing two bidentate ligands and two monodentate ligands, the monodentate ligands may be cis or trans. The arrangement of chelate rings can also contribute to stereochemical differences. When several different ligands are present, more than two geometrical arrangements may be possible; draw the six positions and compare arrangements systematically.

Important: do not assume that every octahedral complex has only cis and trans forms. The number and type of isomers depend on the ligand pattern and whether ligands are monodentate or chelating.

2.10 Optical isomerism and chirality

Optical isomers, also called enantiomers, are pairs of structures that are mirror images but cannot be superimposed exactly. A molecule or complex with this property is described as chiral. A structure that can be superimposed on its mirror image is achiral.

Figure 2.6. The left and right hands illustrate mirror images that cannot be superimposed.

Chiral coordination complexes can rotate plane-polarised light. One enantiomer rotates the plane in one direction and the other rotates it by the same magnitude in the opposite direction under the same conditions. The signs (+) and (−) describe measured rotation; they do not by themselves identify a particular three-dimensional arrangement.

An equal mixture of two enantiomers is called a racemic mixture. Their opposite rotations cancel, so the mixture has no net optical rotation even though each enantiomer is individually optically active.

How to recognise chirality

  • Draw the three-dimensional arrangement, not only the empirical formula.
  • Construct the mirror image and test whether rotation can make the two structures coincide.
  • A plane of symmetry usually makes a structure achiral, although the safest test is whether the mirror image is superimposable.
  • Do not confuse optical activity with geometrical isomerism: a complex may have cis/trans forms and one of those forms may or may not be chiral.

Important octahedral example: [M(AA)₃]

An octahedral complex containing three identical symmetric bidentate ligands, such as [Co(en)₃]³⁺ or [Cr(ox)₃]³⁻, can exist as two enantiomeric forms. The three chelate rings wrap around the metal in a right-handed or left-handed arrangement. These forms are commonly labelled Δ (delta) and Λ (lambda).

Figure 2.7. Δ and Λ indicate opposite helical arrangements of three chelate rings in suitable octahedral complexes.

Some tetrahedral complexes can also be chiral, especially when all four attached groups are different or when unsymmetrical chelating ligands create a non-superimposable arrangement. Square-planar complexes are usually achiral because the metal and four donor atoms lie in one plane, but special ligand structures can introduce chirality elsewhere in the molecule.

2.11 Distinguishing isomers

Different types of isomerism require different experimental evidence. No single test identifies every type.

MethodWhat it can revealReasoning
Precipitation testsIonisation and some hydrate isomersDifferent amounts or identities of free counter-ions are present in solution.
Conductivity measurementsDifferences in the number and charge of ions produced in solutionComplex salts with different ionisation behaviour can have different molar conductivities.
Dipole momentSome cis/trans pairsBond dipoles may cancel in a symmetrical trans arrangement but not in a cis arrangement.
Infrared spectroscopyDifferent bonding environments and vibrational symmetryDifferent structures can have different vibrational frequencies and IR-active modes.
Optical rotation / circular dichroismChiral complexes and enantiomeric compositionEnantiomers interact differently with plane-polarised light and circularly polarised light.
X-ray crystallographyDirect structural arrangement in a crystalProvides atomic positions and bond connectivity in the solid state.

Dipole moment: in an ideal symmetrical trans-[MA₂B₂] square-planar arrangement, the relevant bond dipoles may cancel, giving zero net dipole moment. In the cis form, cancellation is incomplete and a net dipole may remain. This is a useful structural principle, but the actual value depends on the complete molecular symmetry and ligand dipoles.

Infrared spectroscopy: a vibration is IR-active when it produces a change in dipole moment. Symmetry can make a vibration inactive in one isomer but active in another. Therefore, IR spectra can help distinguish structures, but assignments should be based on the full vibrational pattern rather than one band alone.

Chemical chelation tests: a bidentate reagent may react differently with cis and trans arrangements because the donor atoms must reach two suitable coordination positions at the same time. A cis arrangement may permit ring formation where the trans arrangement cannot form the same chelate without major rearrangement.

2.12 A systematic method for solving isomer questions

  1. Read the formula carefully. Identify the metal, oxidation state, ligands, charges and coordination sphere.
  2. Check connectivity first. Ask whether a ligand changes donor atom or moves between inside and outside the brackets. If yes, consider structural isomerism.
  3. Fix the coordination geometry. Determine whether the complex is square planar, tetrahedral or octahedral from the information supplied.
  4. Place each donor atom. For chelating ligands, remember that one ligand occupies more than one coordination position.
  5. Compare positions. Identify cis/trans or fac/mer relationships where applicable.
  6. Test mirror images. For optical isomerism, check whether the mirror image can be superimposed on the original structure.
  7. Remove duplicates. Rotating the entire drawing or exchanging equivalent positions does not create a new isomer.
Strong exam habit: state the structural reason for each name. For example, write “cis because the two chloride ligands are adjacent” rather than only writing “cis-isomer”.

2.13 Worked examples and practice

Worked example 1 — identify ionisation isomers

Compare [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]SO₄ and [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Cl.

Reasoning: chloride is coordinated in the first formula but is outside the brackets in the second. Sulfate is outside in the first and coordinated in the second. The counter-ion and coordinated anion exchange places.

Answer: they are ionisation isomers.

Worked example 2 — count the geometrical forms of square-planar [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]

There are two pairs of identical ligands. The two chloride ligands may be adjacent or opposite. These arrangements are not interconverted by rotating the whole molecule.

Answer: two geometrical isomers: cis and trans.

Worked example 3 — classify [Co(en)₃]³⁺

Each en ligand is bidentate and occupies two donor positions. Three en ligands provide six donor atoms, giving an octahedral coordination environment. The three chelate rings can wrap in opposite helical directions.

Answer: the complex has a pair of optical isomers, Δ and Λ.

Worked example 4 — identify linkage isomerism

A complex contains NO₂⁻. In one compound, nitrogen is directly bonded to the metal; in another, oxygen is directly bonded to the metal.

Answer: linkage isomerism because the ambidentate nitrite ligand uses different donor atoms.

Self-test questions

  1. Define isomerism in coordination chemistry and distinguish structural isomerism from stereoisomerism.
  2. Explain why [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ and [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br are ionisation isomers.
  3. In hydrate isomers of CrCl₃·6H₂O, why does the amount of AgCl formed differ?
  4. What property of NO₂⁻ allows it to form linkage isomers?
  5. Draw cis and trans forms of square-planar [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂].
  6. Explain the difference between fac and mer arrangements in an octahedral [MA₃B₃] complex.
  7. Define chirality and explain why [Co(en)₃]³⁺ can have optical isomers.
  8. State one way dipole-moment measurements can help distinguish cis and trans isomers.
  9. Why should a vibration produce a change in dipole moment to be IR-active?
  10. Explain why rotating a complex drawing does not produce a new isomer.

Short answer guide

  1. Same composition, different connectivity or spatial arrangement; structural isomers change connectivity, stereoisomers retain connectivity but change 3-D arrangement.
  2. The coordinated anion and free counter-ion exchange positions.
  3. Only chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are immediately available as free counter-ions for precipitation.
  4. It can coordinate through either N or O.
  5. Place the two chloride ligands adjacent for cis and opposite for trans.
  6. Fac has three identical ligands on one triangular face; mer has them around a plane with one trans pair.
  7. A chiral complex has a non-superimposable mirror image; three chelate rings can form Δ and Λ arrangements.
  8. Ideal trans symmetry can cause bond dipoles to cancel, while cis arrangements may have a net dipole.
  9. IR absorption requires the vibration to change the dipole moment.
  10. A whole-object rotation changes the viewpoint, not the relative arrangement.
