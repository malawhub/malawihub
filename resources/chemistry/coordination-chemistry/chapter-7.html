Chapter 7: Stability of Complexes and Reaction Mechanisms

Coordination chemistry requires two different questions. First, how strongly does a metal ion favour a particular complex at equilibrium? Second, how quickly can that complex exchange a ligand or transfer an electron? The first question is thermodynamic stability; the second is kinetic behaviour.

1. Learning outcomes

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish thermodynamic stability from kinetic stability.
  • Use formation constants and Gibbs energy to describe complex formation.
  • Explain stepwise and overall formation constants.
  • Discuss the metal-ion and ligand factors that influence stability, including the Irving–Williams trend, chelate effect and macrocyclic effect.
  • Distinguish labile and inert complexes without confusing these terms with stable and unstable.
  • Describe dissociative, associative and interchange substitution mechanisms.
  • Explain aquation, acid hydrolysis and base hydrolysis in octahedral complexes.
  • Describe associative substitution in square-planar complexes and explain the trans effect.
  • Compare outer-sphere and inner-sphere electron-transfer mechanisms, including remote and adjacent attack.
  • Apply the Marcus–Hush relationship to estimate a cross-reaction rate constant.
  • Explain metal-to-ligand charge-transfer excitation, excited-state redox behaviour, luminescence, internal conversion and photosubstitution.

2. Two meanings of stability

2.1 Thermodynamic stability

Thermodynamic stability describes the tendency of a complex to exist at equilibrium relative to the uncomplexed metal ion and ligand under specified conditions. It is measured using an equilibrium constant, commonly a formation constant. A larger formation constant means that equilibrium favours the complex more strongly.

2.2 Kinetic stability

Kinetic stability describes how quickly a complex reacts, particularly how quickly a coordinated ligand is replaced. It depends on the reaction pathway and its activation barrier. A complex that undergoes ligand substitution rapidly is called labile; one that reacts slowly is called inert with respect to that reaction.

Do not confuse the terms: thermodynamic stability is about equilibrium; kinetic inertness is about reaction rate. A complex may be thermodynamically stable but kinetically labile, or thermodynamically less favoured yet kinetically inert. The words stable/unstable and inert/labile are not interchangeable.

Conceptual energy profiles: a lower activation barrier generally permits a faster reaction. The relative energies of reactants and products determine thermodynamic favourability, not the barrier alone.

3. Formation constants and thermodynamic stability

Consider formation of a complex from a metal ion M and ligand L:

M + nL ⇌ MLn

The overall formation constant, βn, is the equilibrium constant for formation of the complete complex:

βn = [MLn] / ([M][L]n)

Activities are used in the rigorous thermodynamic definition; concentrations are commonly used in introductory treatments and dilute-solution calculations. A larger β value indicates a greater equilibrium preference for the complex under the conditions represented by the constant.

3.1 Relationship with Gibbs free energy

ΔG° = −RT ln β

R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature in kelvin and β is the dimensionless standard equilibrium constant. A large β gives a negative standard Gibbs energy for complex formation. The logarithm is natural; when using log10, use ΔG° = −2.303RT log β.

Worked example 1: Gibbs energy from a formation constant.

At 298 K, a complex has β = 1.0 × 106. Calculate ΔG° using R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1.

ΔG° = −RT ln β = −(8.314)(298)ln(106)

ΔG° = −(8.314)(298)(13.816) = −34,230 J mol−1.

ΔG° ≈ −34.2 kJ mol−1. The negative value indicates that complex formation is favourable under standard conditions.

4. Stepwise and overall formation constants

Complexes often form by adding ligands one at a time. For a metal ion M and ligand L:

M + L ⇌ ML    K₁ = [ML]/([M][L])
ML + L ⇌ ML₂    K₂ = [ML₂]/([ML][L])
ML₂ + L ⇌ ML₃    K₃ = [ML₃]/([ML₂][L])

These are stepwise formation constants. The overall constant for formation of ML₃ is:

β₃ = K₁ × K₂ × K₃

In general, βn = K₁K₂K₃...Kn. The dissociation constant for the reverse of a formation step is the reciprocal of the corresponding formation constant, provided the same standard-state convention is used.

Worked example 2: Overall formation constant.

A complex forms with stepwise constants K₁ = 1.0 × 105, K₂ = 2.0 × 104 and K₃ = 1.0 × 103.

β₃ = (1.0 × 105)(2.0 × 104)(1.0 × 103)

β₃ = 2.0 × 1012.

4.1 Why stepwise constants often decrease

For many systems, K₁ is greater than K₂, which is greater than K₃ and so on. After each ligand binds, fewer coordination sites remain and the incoming ligand may experience greater electrostatic repulsion from ligands already attached. Statistical factors also influence the observed values. This decreasing pattern is common, not an absolute rule; exceptions occur when geometry, ligand interactions or structural changes make a later binding step especially favourable.

5. Factors affecting thermodynamic stability

5.1 Charge and size of the metal ion

For comparable metal ions and the same ligand, a higher positive charge and smaller ionic radius often produce stronger electrostatic attraction to donor atoms. This is a useful trend, but it is not the only factor: covalency, electronic configuration, solvent and ligand properties can alter the order.

5.2 Electronic configuration and crystal-field stabilisation

The d-electron configuration and ligand-field stabilisation can contribute to differences in stability across a series. The metal–ligand bonding pattern and the energy gained from the ligand field are relevant, but crystal-field stabilisation energy alone does not determine every stability constant.

5.3 Irving–Williams series

For many high-spin divalent first-row transition-metal complexes with the same donor ligand, the commonly observed stability trend is:

Mn²⁺ < Fe²⁺ < Co²⁺ < Ni²⁺ < Cu²⁺ > Zn²⁺

This is called the Irving–Williams series. The trend reflects several contributions, including decreasing ionic size across the series, changes in ligand-field stabilisation and the particular stability of d⁹ Cu(II). It is an empirical trend for many comparable systems, not a universal ranking for every ligand or condition.

5.4 Metal–ligand covalency and ligand donor ability

Greater covalent character can strengthen metal–ligand bonding in suitable systems. Donor atoms differ in basicity, polarizability and orbital-energy match with the metal. Hard and soft acid–base ideas help organise these preferences: hard metal ions often favour hard donor atoms, while softer metal ions often bind more strongly to softer, more polarizable donors. The actual stability order depends on the complete metal–ligand pair and solution conditions.

5.5 Steric effects and ligand structure

Bulky groups close to a donor atom can obstruct approach to the metal and lower the stability of a complex. However, steric crowding can also affect the rate of substitution in a different way. Electron delocalisation within a ligand can sometimes stabilise a complex by distributing charge and changing bonding interactions.

6. The chelate effect

A chelating ligand attaches to one metal ion through two or more donor atoms, producing one or more rings that include the metal. A multidentate ligand can replace several separate monodentate ligands and form a ring structure. Complexes with chelating ligands are often more thermodynamically stable than comparable complexes with equivalent monodentate donor groups. This enhanced stability is called the chelate effect.

Conceptual comparison: D represents a donor atom. In a chelate, two donor atoms from the same ligand bind the same metal centre and form a ring.

Two common explanations are useful:

  • Entropy contribution: replacing several separate ligand molecules by one multidentate ligand can increase the number of free particles in solution. This can make the entropy change more favourable.
  • Ring formation and rebinding: after one donor atom temporarily detaches, another donor atom of the same ligand can remain attached. The free donor can re-coordinate before the whole ligand escapes.

The chelate effect is not simply “a stronger bond because there are more bonds”. It is a thermodynamic effect involving the complete reaction, including entropy, enthalpy and the structure of the ligand.

6.1 Ring size and ligand structure

Ring size influences chelate stability. Five-membered chelate rings are often especially favourable, while six-membered rings can also be stable. Very small rings may be strained; larger rings may have greater conformational freedom. The preferred size depends on the metal, donor atoms and ligand geometry. For comparable bidentate ligands, donor-atom basicity, bite angle and ligand structure also matter.

7. Macrocyclic effect

A macrocyclic ligand contains a large pre-organised ring with several donor atoms. When it binds a metal ion, the donor atoms are already held in a suitable arrangement, so less structural reorganisation may be required than with a flexible open-chain ligand. Complexes of macrocyclic ligands can therefore be more stable than comparable complexes of open-chain ligands with similar donor atoms. This is called the macrocyclic effect.

Examples of naturally occurring macrocyclic coordination environments include the porphyrin-type rings in heme and the related tetrapyrrole framework in chlorophyll. The match between cavity size, donor atoms and metal-ion size is important.

8. Kinetic stability: labile and inert complexes

For a ligand-substitution reaction, a labile complex undergoes replacement relatively rapidly, whereas an inert complex undergoes replacement slowly. These are kinetic descriptions. The same complex can be labile in one reaction and less labile in another because the entering ligand, solvent, temperature and mechanism can change the rate.

Some introductory texts use a one-minute half-life as a rough boundary in examples. This is not a universal IUPAC dividing line: always interpret lability and inertness in relation to the particular reaction and experimental conditions.

9. Ligand substitution in octahedral complexes

In a substitution reaction, an entering ligand Y replaces a leaving ligand X:

[ML₅X] + Y → [ML₅Y] + X

The mechanism describes the sequence of elementary events by which the reaction occurs. Three limiting descriptions are used: dissociative (D), associative (A) and interchange (I).

9.1 Dissociative mechanism (D)

In a dissociative pathway, the metal–leaving-ligand bond breaks substantially before the entering ligand forms a bond. A five-coordinate intermediate may be produced from an octahedral six-coordinate complex. The rate-determining step is often loss of X:

[ML₅X] → [ML₅] + X    slow
[ML₅] + Y → [ML₅Y]    fast

If the first step is rate-determining, the rate law is commonly:

Rate = k[ML₅X]

The rate is first order in the complex and independent of the entering-ligand concentration in this limiting case. A five-coordinate intermediate may be square-pyramidal or trigonal-bipyramidal; the actual structure depends on the system.

9.2 Associative mechanism (A)

In an associative pathway, the entering ligand begins to bond to the metal before the leaving ligand has departed. The coordination number temporarily increases, often to seven for an octahedral starting complex:

[ML₅X] + Y → [ML₅XY] → [ML₅Y] + X

If formation of the higher-coordinate species is rate-determining, the rate depends on both the complex and entering ligand:

Rate = k[ML₅X][Y]

This is second order overall for the simple case. Associative pathways are favoured when the metal centre can accommodate the incoming ligand and steric/electronic repulsion does not prevent approach.

9.3 Interchange mechanism (I)

In an interchange pathway, bond formation to Y and bond breaking to X occur in one concerted step, without a long-lived intermediate that can be isolated. The process may have more dissociative character (Id) or more associative character (Ia).

10. Factors controlling octahedral substitution

Substitution rates depend on the metal ion, oxidation state, electronic configuration, ligand-field stabilisation, metal–ligand bond strength, steric crowding, solvent and the entering and leaving ligands. No single rule predicts every complex.

  • Metal–ligand bond strength: a stronger bond to the leaving ligand generally makes its removal more difficult.
  • Charge and radius: these affect metal–ligand attraction and solvent interactions.
  • Electronic configuration and CFSE: loss or reduction of ligand-field stabilisation can raise the activation barrier in some substitution pathways.
  • Steric crowding: crowding can destabilise a crowded starting arrangement and sometimes accelerate ligand loss, but it may hinder an associative incoming-ligand approach.
  • Chelation: multidentate ligands can reduce the rate at which the entire ligand leaves because several donor atoms must detach.

11. Water exchange and aquation

11.1 Water exchange

Water exchange is the replacement of a coordinated water molecule by a water molecule from the surrounding solvent. It is a useful way to compare the kinetic behaviour of hydrated metal ions. Exchange rates vary widely with metal-ion charge, radius, electronic configuration and the strength of the metal–oxygen interaction.

11.2 Aquation

Aquation is a substitution reaction in which a coordinated ligand is replaced by water. For a complex containing a coordinated halide X⁻, a simplified equation is:

[M(NH₃)₅X]²⁺ + H₂O → [M(NH₃)₅(H₂O)]³⁺ + X⁻

The charges shown correspond to a trivalent metal centre and five neutral ammonia ligands. Aquation is one type of hydrolysis/substitution reaction discussed for coordination complexes.

12. Acid and base hydrolysis

12.1 Acid hydrolysis

In acid hydrolysis of a suitable ammine complex, a coordinated anionic ligand such as chloride is replaced by water. A simplified reaction is:

[Co(NH₃)₅Cl]²⁺ + H₂O → [Co(NH₃)₅(H₂O)]³⁺ + Cl⁻

For many cobalt(III) ammine systems, acid hydrolysis has dissociative or interchange-dissociative character. The exact rate law and pathway depend on the complex and conditions.

12.2 Base hydrolysis and the conjugate-base pathway

Base hydrolysis of some cobalt(III) ammine complexes is more complicated than direct attack of OH⁻ on the metal. A coordinated NH₃ ligand can first lose a proton to form a coordinated amido group, NH₂⁻. This conjugate-base species can then facilitate substitution of the leaving ligand.

[Co(NH₃)₅X]²⁺ + OH⁻ ⇌ [Co(NH₃)₄(NH₂)X]⁺ + H₂O
[Co(NH₃)₄(NH₂)X]⁺ → substitution products + X⁻

The second step can involve loss of X from the conjugate-base intermediate. This is commonly called the SN1CB mechanism: substitution, nucleophilic, unimolecular, conjugate-base pathway. In such a mechanism, OH⁻ participates in the prior deprotonation equilibrium, so the observed rate can depend on hydroxide concentration even though the slow step is dissociation of X from the conjugate-base species.

Key distinction: Do not automatically describe every base hydrolysis as a simple associative attack by OH⁻. For the cobalt(III) ammine examples in this chapter, the conjugate-base route is an important mechanism to recognise.

13. Stereochemistry of octahedral substitution

Substitution can preserve or change the spatial arrangement of the remaining ligands. The outcome depends on the geometry of the intermediate and on how the entering ligand approaches. A five-coordinate intermediate can rearrange between square-pyramidal and trigonal-bipyramidal arrangements before product formation. Consequently, a reaction may retain or alter a cis/trans relationship. The stereochemical product is evidence about the pathway, but should be interpreted together with kinetic data and the structures of the reactant and product.

14. Substitution in square-planar complexes

Square-planar d⁸ complexes, especially many Pt(II) complexes, commonly undergo ligand substitution by an associative pathway. The entering ligand approaches the metal and forms a bond before the leaving ligand has completely departed. A five-coordinate transition state or short-lived intermediate is involved.

[PtL₃X] + Y → [PtL₃XY]‡ → [PtL₃Y] + X

For a simple associative pathway, the rate often has a contribution that depends on the entering-ligand concentration. In aqueous solution, solvent-assisted and direct nucleophile pathways may operate together:

Rate = k₁[complex] + k₂[complex][Y]

The first term represents a solvent-dependent pathway; the second represents a pathway assisted by the entering nucleophile. The observed rate depends on conditions and the relative sizes of the two contributions.

14.1 Factors affecting square-planar substitution

  • Nucleophilicity of the entering ligand: a more effective nucleophile often increases the rate of an associative substitution, provided other factors are comparable.
  • Nature of the leaving ligand: the metal–leaving-group bond and the stability of the departing ligand affect the rate.
  • Metal centre: Pt(II), Pd(II) and Ni(II) complexes can have different substitution rates.
  • Steric effects: bulky groups can obstruct the approach of an entering ligand and slow an associative reaction.
  • Solvent: solvent coordination and solvation of reactants or products can change the rate.

15. Trans effect in square-planar complexes

The trans effect is the kinetic effect of a coordinated ligand on the rate of substitution of the ligand positioned trans (opposite) to it in a square-planar complex. A ligand with a strong trans effect accelerates replacement of the ligand opposite to itself. It is especially important in Pt(II) chemistry and is used to guide the synthesis of particular geometric isomers.

T is the spectator ligand. X lies trans to T and is the ligand whose substitution is accelerated. The second panel shows Y in the position formerly occupied by X.

Examples of ligands that often show strong trans-directing effects include CO, CN⁻, NO and phosphines; halides also show trans effects, with their strength depending on the metal and conditions. A simplified qualitative series often places strong π-acceptor ligands toward the strong end and water/ammonia toward the weaker end. Do not treat a single list as universal for every metal complex.

The trans effect is kinetic. It must be distinguished from the trans influence, which describes a ground-state weakening or lengthening of the bond trans to a ligand. The two phenomena are related in some systems but are not identical.

15.1 Why the trans effect occurs

Two broad explanations are commonly used. Strong σ-donor ligands can increase electron density at the metal and weaken the bond trans to them. Strong π-acceptor ligands can stabilise the associative transition state through metal-to-ligand back-bonding interactions. The relative importance of these effects depends on the ligand and metal.

15.2 Synthetic use

When a ligand is introduced into a square-planar complex, the ligand with the stronger trans effect can direct the position at which the next ligand enters. By choosing the order of addition and the ligands already coordinated, chemists can favour cis or trans products. The trans effect is therefore a practical tool for preparing square-planar Pt(II) complexes with selected arrangements.

16. Electron-transfer reactions of coordination complexes

Electron-transfer reactions are redox reactions in which an electron moves from a reducing agent to an oxidising agent. Unlike ordinary ligand substitution, electron transfer changes the oxidation state of one or both metal centres. The reaction may occur with little or no change in the ligand arrangement, or it may be accompanied by ligand exchange.

16.1 Outer-sphere electron transfer

In an outer-sphere mechanism, the two coordination spheres remain intact during the electron-transfer event. The complexes approach one another, and an electron moves between them without a bridging ligand being formed. Metal–ligand bonds are not broken and remade as part of the electron-transfer pathway.

Outer-sphere transfer is possible when the reactants can approach closely enough for electronic interaction while retaining their coordination spheres. The rate depends on factors including the electronic coupling, the change in metal–ligand bond lengths required by electron transfer, solvent reorganisation and spin restrictions.

Schematic outer-sphere electron transfer: electron transfer occurs without a shared bridging ligand.

16.2 Inner-sphere electron transfer

In an inner-sphere mechanism, the oxidant and reductant become connected by a ligand that bridges the two metal centres. This bridged species is often called a precursor complex. Electron transfer occurs through the bridge, and the bridged intermediate then separates into products.

A typical situation involves one substitution-labile complex and one complex that is sufficiently inert to retain a ligand capable of bridging. Chloride can act as a bridge in classic cobalt(III)/chromium(II) examples. The bridging ligand may transfer to the other metal after electron transfer, but ligand transfer is not essential: the bridge can remain with one metal when the relative metal–ligand bond strengths favour that outcome.

Schematic inner-sphere pathway: a bridging ligand links the metal centres during electron transfer. The exact ligand destination in the products depends on bond strengths and product stability.

16.3 Remote attack and adjacent attack

In an inner-sphere reaction, the reducing agent may approach the bridging ligand at different positions. The terms describe the site through which the reductant interacts with the bridge:

  • Adjacent attack: the two metal centres are connected through the same donor atom of the bridging ligand. The electron-transfer pathway involves the atom directly bonded to both metal centres.
  • Remote attack: the reductant interacts with a different atom of the bridging ligand, rather than the atom directly attached to the oxidant metal. This can occur with ambidentate or polyatomic bridging ligands such as thiocyanate or carboxylate-containing ligands.

For example, thiocyanate can coordinate through nitrogen to one metal and provide sulfur as a site of interaction with the second metal. Which linkage is formed and which product is obtained depend on the ligand's donor atoms, the metal–ligand bond strengths and the reaction conditions. Steric crowding around the approach site can also influence the rate.

How to identify them: draw the bridging ligand and label its atoms. If both metals connect through the same bridge atom, the arrangement is adjacent; if the reductant approaches through a different atom of the bridge, it is remote.

16.6 Marcus–Hush relationship for cross reactions

The Marcus–Hush relationship connects the rate constant of an electron-transfer cross reaction with the rate constants of the corresponding self-exchange reactions and the equilibrium constant of the cross reaction. In the form used in the supplied reference:

k₁₂ = (k₁₁ k₂₂ K₁₂ f)1/2
  • k₁₁ and k₂₂ are the rate constants for the two self-exchange reactions.
  • k₁₂ is the rate constant for the cross reaction.
  • K₁₂ is the equilibrium constant for the overall cross reaction.
  • f is a statistical and steric correction factor that accounts for differences in the free energies of the reactants; it is often close to unity.

The equilibrium constant is related to standard Gibbs energy by ΔG° = −RT ln K₁₂. A larger K₁₂ makes the cross reaction more thermodynamically favourable. The relationship therefore helps estimate a cross-reaction rate from self-exchange data and the reaction equilibrium, within the assumptions of the model.

Worked example 6: Applying the Marcus–Hush relationship.

Suppose k₁₁ = 4.2 L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹, k₂₂ = 4.0 × 10³ L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹, K₁₂ = 2.1 × 10¹¹ and f = 0.85.

k₁₂ = [(4.2)(4.0 × 10³)(2.1 × 10¹¹)(0.85)]1/2

k₁₂ ≈ 5.48 × 10⁷ L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.

The estimated cross-reaction rate constant is much larger than either self-exchange rate constant in this example.

16.4 Two-electron transfer

Electron transfer between coordination compounds is often discussed as a one-electron process. A concerted transfer of two electrons is more difficult by an outer-sphere route because the electronic and metal–ligand bonding structures of the reactants and products may differ substantially, creating a high reorganisation requirement. The supplied reference describes established examples of two-electron transfer through inner-sphere pathways.

One example discussed in the reference is platinum-complex-catalysed exchange of radioactive chloride with chloride ligands in a platinum(II) complex. The key teaching point is that a coordinated pathway can provide a route for multi-electron change that is difficult to achieve by direct outer-sphere transfer. Do not generalise this into a rule that every two-electron redox process must be inner-sphere.

16.5 Complementary and non-complementary redox reactions

In a complementary redox reaction, the oxidation-state change proceeds directly between the stated oxidation levels. In a non-complementary reaction, intermediate oxidation states may be involved in the proposed mechanism. Such pathways can be important in transition-metal redox chemistry because many metals have several accessible oxidation states.

16.4 Photochemical reactions of coordination compounds

A coordination compound can absorb a photon and move from its ground electronic state to an excited state. The excited state may have different bonding, oxidation-state distribution and redox properties from the ground state. Therefore, light can initiate reactions that are slow or unfavourable under ordinary thermal conditions.

Excited states and relaxation

After absorbing light, a complex may return to its ground state by several pathways:

  • Luminescence: the excited complex emits a photon as it returns to a lower-energy state. This is a radiative process.
  • Internal conversion: electronic energy is converted into vibrational energy and ultimately dissipated as heat, without photon emission.
  • Intersystem crossing: the excited state changes spin multiplicity, for example from a singlet state to a triplet state. This is a non-radiative transition.

A simplified Jablonski-style scheme. Absorption raises the complex to an excited state; non-radiative and radiative pathways can then compete.

Metal-to-ligand charge-transfer excitation

In a metal-to-ligand charge-transfer (MLCT) transition, an electron is promoted from an orbital with substantial metal character to an unoccupied ligand orbital, commonly a ligand π* orbital. For a polypyridyl complex such as [Ru(bpy)3]2+, absorption produces an excited state with electron density shifted from the ruthenium-centred dπ orbitals towards a bipyridine π* orbital. The excited state can undergo rapid intersystem crossing to a relatively long-lived triplet MLCT state.

This redistribution of electron density changes the redox behaviour. The metal-centred electron deficiency can make the excited complex a stronger oxidising agent, while the electron occupying the ligand π* orbital can make it a stronger reducing agent. The exact potentials depend on the complex, solvent and excited-state energy; do not assume a universal numerical change.

Photosubstitution and photochemical lability

Light can also promote ligand substitution. In some octahedral complexes, excitation involving metal-centred d orbitals changes the occupancy of orbitals that are antibonding with respect to metal–ligand bonds. Increased population of such orbitals can weaken selected metal–ligand bonds and make ligand loss or substitution faster. This is called photosubstitution when irradiation promotes replacement of a coordinated ligand.

The outcome depends on the electronic state reached, the ligand set, solvent and wavelength. Photochemical substitution is therefore not simply heating the solution: it is a consequence of the electronic excitation pathway and the subsequent chemistry of the excited state.

Key distinction: luminescence returns energy as emitted light; internal conversion dissipates energy without light; photosubstitution uses the excited-state chemistry to produce a chemical change, such as ligand replacement.

17. A practical method for solving stability and mechanism questions

  1. Write the balanced complex-formation or substitution equation.
  2. Identify whether the question asks about equilibrium (thermodynamics) or rate (kinetics).
  3. For stability, calculate the metal oxidation state and write the appropriate formation-constant expression.
  4. For stepwise formation, multiply the stepwise constants to obtain the overall constant.
  5. For a mechanism, ask whether the leaving ligand departs first, the entering ligand attaches first, or both bond changes occur together.
  6. Use the rate law as evidence: independence from [Y] supports a limiting D pathway; dependence on [Y] supports an A contribution.
  7. For square-planar substitution, identify the ligand trans to the leaving group and consider the trans effect.
  8. For electron transfer, check whether the coordination spheres remain intact or a bridging ligand connects the metals.

18. Worked examples

Worked example 3: Identify the likely octahedral substitution pathway.

A reaction rate is measured as Rate = k[complex], and changing the concentration of entering ligand Y does not change the rate under the studied conditions.

The rate is first order in the complex and independent of [Y]. This is consistent with a limiting dissociative pathway in which loss of the leaving ligand is rate-determining. Kinetic evidence should be considered with other structural evidence before assigning a complete mechanism.

Worked example 4: Determine the type of electron-transfer pathway.

Two metal complexes react, and experiments show that a chloride ligand temporarily connects the metal centres in a binuclear intermediate.

A shared bridging ligand is present during electron transfer. This is evidence for an inner-sphere mechanism.

Worked example 5: Explain why a chelate can be more stable.

A bidentate ligand replaces two separate monodentate ligands. Explain two factors that can favour the chelate.

First, the number of independent particles in solution can increase, making the entropy change more favourable. Second, if one donor atom detaches, the other may remain attached and allow the free donor to re-coordinate before the entire ligand escapes.

20. Answers and explanations

1. Thermodynamic stability describes how strongly equilibrium favours a complex. Kinetic inertness describes how slowly it undergoes a specified reaction.

2. ΔG° = −RT ln β = −(8.314)(298)ln(10⁸) = approximately −45.6 kJ mol⁻¹.

21. Chapter summary

  • Thermodynamic stability concerns equilibrium; kinetic stability concerns reaction rate.
  • Formation constants quantify complex formation, and ΔG° = −RT ln β.
  • Overall formation constants are products of the stepwise formation constants.
  • Metal charge, ionic size, electronic configuration, ligand donor properties, covalency and steric effects influence stability.
  • The Irving–Williams series describes a common trend for many divalent first-row transition-metal complexes.
  • Chelating and macrocyclic ligands often form especially stable complexes.
  • Octahedral substitution can follow dissociative, associative or interchange pathways.
  • Acid and base hydrolysis can proceed by different pathways; the conjugate-base mechanism is important in some cobalt(III) ammine complexes.
  • Many square-planar Pt(II) substitutions are associative, and the trans effect helps direct product geometry.
  • Outer-sphere electron transfer does not require a bridge; inner-sphere transfer proceeds through a bridged precursor complex.
  • Inner-sphere reactions can involve adjacent or remote attack, and some multi-electron transfers proceed through coordinated pathways.
  • The Marcus–Hush relationship estimates cross-reaction rates from self-exchange rate constants and the cross-reaction equilibrium constant.
  • Photochemical excitation can change redox properties, produce luminescence or promote ligand substitution.
