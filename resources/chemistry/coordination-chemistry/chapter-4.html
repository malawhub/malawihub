Chapter 4: Colour and Electronic Spectra of Coordination Compounds

Why do many transition-metal complexes have intense colours? How does a spectrometer reveal the energy gap between electronic states? This chapter connects the colour we observe with the electronic structure of coordination compounds.

2. Why are coordination compounds coloured?

White light contains a range of visible wavelengths. When white light passes through or reflects from a coordination compound, some wavelengths may be absorbed more strongly than others. The remaining light reaches the eye, and this determines the colour observed.

In many transition-metal complexes, the metal has partially occupied d orbitals. When ligands approach the metal, their electric fields interact with the d electrons and remove the equality of energy among the five d orbitals. In an octahedral field, for example, the d orbitals separate into a lower-energy t2g set and a higher-energy eg set. The energy gap is called the octahedral crystal-field splitting energy, Δo.

Simplified octahedral splitting diagram: absorption can promote an electron from t₂g to e_g when the photon energy matches the separation.

If the absorbed photon has the correct energy, an electron can move from a lower electronic state to a higher one. This is an electronic transition. The energy absorbed is not arbitrary: it must match the difference between the two allowed energy states.

Important qualification: Not every coordination compound is coloured. A complex may be colourless if it has no suitable transition that absorbs visible light, if its relevant absorption lies in the ultraviolet or infrared region, or if its electronic configuration does not permit an appreciable visible d–d transition. Charge-transfer transitions can also produce colour and may be much more intense than d–d bands.

3. Absorbed colour and observed colour

The colour of light absorbed is generally complementary to the colour transmitted or reflected. Complementary colours lie opposite one another on a colour wheel. Therefore, a blue-looking solution commonly absorbs light in the orange region rather than absorbing blue light.

Illustrative colour wheel. The observed colour is approximately complementary to the colour absorbed.

These are broad relationships, not exact colour predictions. The observed shade depends on the width and position of absorption bands, concentration, path length, solvent and the presence of more than one absorbing species. A substance that absorbs nearly all visible wavelengths may appear very dark; one that absorbs little visible light may appear colourless or white.

4. Electronic transitions and photon energy

An electron in the ground state occupies the lowest available electronic state. Absorption of a photon can promote it to an excited state if the photon energy equals the energy difference between the states.

ΔE = Eexcited − Eground = hν = hc/λ

Here, h is Planck's constant (6.626 × 10−34 J s), ν is frequency, c is the speed of light (2.998 × 108 m s−1) and λ is wavelength.

Frequency and wavelength are related by:

c = νλ    and    ν = c/λ

Wavenumber, commonly written as ν̄, is the reciprocal of wavelength when wavelength is expressed in centimetres:

ν̄ = 1/λ(cm)    ;    E = hcν̄

Wavenumber is commonly reported in cm−1 in electronic spectroscopy. A larger wavenumber means a higher photon energy and a shorter wavelength.

Worked example 1: Convert wavelength to wavenumber and energy.

A complex has an absorption maximum at 500 nm. Find the wavenumber and the energy per mole of photons.

  1. Convert wavelength: 500 nm = 5.00 × 10−5 cm.
  2. Wavenumber: ν̄ = 1/(5.00 × 10−5) = 20,000 cm−1.
  3. Energy per photon: E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10−34)(2.998 × 108)/(5.00 × 10−7) = 3.97 × 10−19 J.
  4. Per mole: Em = 3.97 × 10−19 × 6.022 × 1023 = 239 kJ mol−1 (approximately).

5. Electronic absorption spectra

An electronic absorption spectrum records how strongly a sample absorbs light at different wavelengths or wavenumbers. A spectrophotometer compares the intensity of incident light with the intensity transmitted through the sample. An absorption band appears where the sample absorbs light.

Schematic spectrum showing an absorption band and its maximum, λmax. The curve is illustrative, not experimental data.

The wavelength at the top of a band is called the absorption maximum, λmax. The energy corresponding to λmax is often used to estimate the energy separation between the relevant states. A broad band may contain several unresolved transitions or reflect distortions and interactions that split electronic states.

For a simple d1 octahedral ion such as [Ti(H2O)6]3+, the principal d–d transition is from t2g to eg. In this simplified case, the transition energy gives an estimate of Δo. For more complicated d-electron configurations, electron–electron repulsion produces several electronic terms and more than one absorption band may occur.

6. Beer–Lambert law

The Beer–Lambert law relates the absorbance of a solution to the concentration of the absorbing species and the distance travelled by light through the solution.

A = log10(I0/I) = εlc
  • A = absorbance (no unit).
  • I0 = incident light intensity.
  • I = transmitted light intensity.
  • ε = molar absorption coefficient (commonly L mol−1 cm−1).
  • l = path length through the sample (cm).
  • c = concentration (mol L−1).

Transmittance is T = I/I0. Percentage transmittance is %T = 100T. Absorbance increases when concentration or path length increases, provided the law's conditions are satisfied.

Worked example 2: Calculate concentration.

A solution has absorbance 0.600 at a selected wavelength. Its molar absorption coefficient is 150 L mol−1 cm−1 and the cuvette path length is 1.00 cm. Find its concentration.

Rearrange: c = A/(εl) = 0.600/(150 × 1.00) = 0.00400 mol L−1.

When the law may fail: Deviations can occur at high concentration, when chemical equilibria change the absorbing species, when the light is not sufficiently monochromatic, or when the sample scatters light. Use a suitable blank and clean, correctly oriented cuvettes during measurement.

7. Factors that affect the absorption band

  • Metal ion and oxidation state: these determine the d-electron configuration and influence the energies of electronic states.
  • Ligand identity: ligands differ in field strength. A stronger ligand often produces a larger splitting for comparable complexes.
  • Geometry: octahedral, tetrahedral and square-planar arrangements produce different d-orbital energy patterns.
  • Metal–ligand distance: changes in distance alter the strength of the ligand field.
  • Distortion: departure from ideal geometry can split states further and broaden or divide bands.
  • Solvent and chemical environment: these can affect equilibria, structure and spectral position.

The spectrochemical series gives a useful approximate order of ligand-field strengths. A commonly used sequence is:

I− < Br− < Cl− < F− < OH− < H2O < NH3 < en < CN− < CO

This is a general guide. The actual splitting also depends on the metal, oxidation state and geometry; do not treat the sequence as a universal numerical scale.

8. d–d transitions and charge-transfer transitions

8.1 d–d transitions

A d–d transition involves excitation of an electron between metal-centred d-derived energy levels. In an octahedral complex this is often described as promotion between t2g and eg levels. The transition energy depends on the ligand field and on electron–electron interactions. d–d bands are often relatively weak in centrosymmetric octahedral complexes because of the Laporte selection rule.

8.2 Charge-transfer transitions

A charge-transfer transition involves movement of electron density between orbitals that are mainly ligand-centred and orbitals that are mainly metal-centred. Ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) moves electron density from a ligand to the metal; metal-to-ligand charge transfer (MLCT) moves electron density from the metal to a ligand. Such transitions are commonly more intense than d–d transitions because they can be allowed by the relevant selection rules.

Do not confuse: a coloured complex does not prove that its colour is caused only by d–d transitions. Charge-transfer absorption can dominate, particularly in complexes with suitable metal oxidation states and ligands.

9. Lanthanide electronic spectra

Many lanthanide ions show relatively sharp absorption bands compared with the broad bands commonly seen for transition-metal complexes. Their important transitions involve 4f electrons. The 4f orbitals are relatively well shielded by outer 5s and 5p electrons, so the ligand environment often has a smaller influence on the 4f energy levels than it has on metal d orbitals. As a result, many lanthanide spectral bands are comparatively narrow and their positions are less sensitive to ligand changes.

10. Electronic states and term symbols

For configurations containing more than one d electron, it is not enough to consider only the one-electron t2g and eg levels. Electron–electron repulsion creates groups of states with different total spin and orbital angular momentum. These groups are described using spectroscopic term symbols.

A free-ion term symbol is written:

2S+1LJ
  • S is the total spin angular momentum quantum number.
  • 2S + 1 is the spin multiplicity.
  • L is the total orbital angular momentum quantum number, represented by letters: S (L = 0), P (1), D (2), F (3), G (4), and so on.
  • J is the total angular momentum quantum number, arising from coupling L and S.

For example, a term with S = 1 and L = 2 has multiplicity 3 and the letter D, so it is a 3D term. In a coordination complex, the ligand field can split a free-ion term into components labelled with symmetry symbols such as A, E and T, with subscripts and sometimes g/u labels. These labels describe degeneracy and symmetry; they are not oxidation states or electron counts.

11. Selection rules and band intensity

11.1 Spin selection rule

A transition is spin-allowed when the total spin does not change:

ΔS = 0

A transition between states of different spin multiplicity is spin-forbidden and is generally much weaker. Spin–orbit coupling can relax this rule to some extent, so a forbidden transition is not necessarily completely absent.

11.2 Laporte selection rule

For a centrosymmetric species, an electronic transition must involve a change in parity to be electric-dipole allowed. A transition between two d orbitals is g → g and is therefore Laporte-forbidden in an ideal centrosymmetric octahedral complex. Such bands can still appear weakly because vibrations temporarily remove the centre of symmetry and because other interactions mix orbital character.

Tetrahedral complexes lack a centre of inversion. Their d–d transitions can therefore gain intensity through orbital mixing and are often more intense than comparable transitions in centrosymmetric octahedral complexes. This is a useful trend, not an absolute rule for every pair of compounds.

12. Orgel diagrams

Orgel diagrams are qualitative energy-level diagrams used to interpret the spin-allowed transitions of many high-spin octahedral and tetrahedral complexes. They show how free-ion terms split in a ligand field and how the energies of the resulting states vary as ligand-field strength changes. They are particularly useful for understanding the general pattern of bands; they do not provide the detailed quantitative treatment supplied by Tanabe–Sugano diagrams.

For a d1 ion in an octahedral field, the free-ion 2D term splits into a lower 2T2g ground state and a higher 2Eg state. One principal spin-allowed transition is therefore:

2T2g → 2Eg

For this simple d1 case, the transition energy is approximately Δo. As ligand-field strength increases, the energy gap and the transition energy increase. The absorption moves towards higher wavenumber and shorter wavelength.

Simplified d¹ octahedral level separation. It illustrates the single principal transition, not a full Orgel correlation diagram.

For configurations with more than one d electron, the free-ion terms split into several states. More than one spin-allowed band may then be observed. Distortion, spin–orbit coupling and overlapping bands can complicate the spectrum. For quantitative analysis of complex dn spectra, Tanabe–Sugano diagrams are often required.

13. Worked spectral interpretation

Worked example 3: Estimate octahedral splitting from a d¹ spectrum.

A d1 octahedral complex shows its principal absorption maximum at 20,000 cm−1. Estimate Δo in cm−1 and kJ mol−1.

For a simple d1 octahedral case, Δo ≈ transition wavenumber = 20,000 cm−1.

Convert to molar energy using E = hcNAν̄. This gives approximately 239 kJ mol−1. The estimate assumes the observed band corresponds to the t2g → eg transition.

Worked example 4: Predict the direction of a spectral shift.

Two comparable octahedral complexes contain the same metal ion and oxidation state. Complex A has a weaker-field ligand than complex B. Which is expected to have the larger Δo?

Complex B is expected to have the larger Δo. Its transition requires more energy, so the corresponding band tends to occur at a higher wavenumber and shorter wavelength. The comparison assumes that the geometry and other major factors remain comparable.

14. Common errors to avoid

  • Do not say that a blue complex absorbs blue light. The observed colour is generally complementary to the absorbed colour.
  • Do not confuse wavelength with wavenumber: increasing wavenumber means decreasing wavelength.
  • Do not state that all d–d transitions are intense. Many are weak because of selection rules.
  • Do not assume every coloured complex owes its colour to d–d transitions; charge transfer may be responsible.
  • Do not equate every absorption maximum directly with Δo. This is a useful approximation for a simple d1 octahedral case, not a universal rule.
  • Do not call a spin-forbidden transition impossible; it is usually weak, not necessarily absent.

15. Practice questions

1. Explain how splitting of d orbitals can lead to colour in a transition-metal complex.
2. A complex absorbs strongly in the blue region. What broad complementary colour may be observed?
3. Calculate the wavenumber in cm−1 for light of wavelength 600 nm.
4. A solution has A = 0.450, ε = 90.0 L mol−1 cm−1 and l = 1.00 cm. Calculate its concentration.
5. Distinguish a d–d transition from a ligand-to-metal charge-transfer transition.
6. State the spin selection rule and explain what is meant by a spin-forbidden transition.
7. Why are d–d bands often more intense in tetrahedral complexes than in centrosymmetric octahedral complexes?
8. What information does an Orgel diagram provide, and what is one limitation?
9. A simple d1 octahedral complex absorbs at 18,500 cm−1. Estimate Δo and state the assumption made.

Answer guide

  1. Ligands split the metal d levels; absorption of a photon matching the energy gap promotes an electron, and the remaining transmitted or reflected light gives the observed colour.
  2. Orange.
  3. 600 nm = 6.00 × 10−5 cm; ν̄ = 1/λ = 16,667 cm−1 (approximately).
  4. c = A/(εl) = 0.450/90.0 = 5.00 × 10−3 mol L−1.
  5. d–d: excitation between metal d-derived levels. LMCT: electron density is promoted from a ligand-centred orbital to a metal-centred orbital.
  6. ΔS = 0. A spin-forbidden transition changes total spin and is usually weak.
  7. Tetrahedral complexes lack a centre of inversion, so the Laporte restriction is relaxed.
  8. It qualitatively shows splitting of free-ion terms and spin-allowed transitions as field strength changes; it is not a detailed quantitative diagram for all configurations.
  9. Δo ≈ 18,500 cm−1, assuming the band is the principal t2g → eg transition of a d1 octahedral complex.

16. Chapter summary

  • Colour results when a compound selectively absorbs part of visible light; the observed colour is generally complementary to the absorbed colour.
  • Electronic excitation requires photon energy equal to the difference between the initial and final states: ΔE = hν = hc/λ.
  • Wavenumber is the reciprocal of wavelength in centimetres and is widely used in coordination chemistry spectroscopy.
  • Absorption spectra show band positions and intensities. Beer–Lambert law connects absorbance with concentration and path length.
  • d–d and charge-transfer transitions can both contribute to colour. Selection rules help explain band intensity.
  • Orgel diagrams provide a qualitative way to understand spin-allowed transitions in many high-spin octahedral and tetrahedral complexes.
