Chapter 5: Magnetism in Coordination Compounds

Magnetic measurements provide evidence about the number of unpaired electrons, the electronic state and sometimes the geometry of a coordination compound. This chapter develops the link between d-electron configuration and measured magnetic behaviour.

1. Learning outcomes

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish diamagnetic and paramagnetic substances using electron pairing.
  • Explain magnetic susceptibility and why measurements require diamagnetic correction.
  • Use the spin-only formula to calculate an effective magnetic moment.
  • Use a magnetic moment to estimate the number of unpaired electrons, while recognising limitations.
  • Explain why ligand strength and geometry can change the magnetic behaviour of a complex.
  • Describe orbital contributions, orbital quenching and the special behaviour of many lanthanide ions.
  • Distinguish ferro-, antiferro- and ferrimagnetism and explain spin crossover.

2. Origin of magnetism in coordination compounds

Electrons possess spin angular momentum and orbital angular momentum. Both can contribute to the magnetic moment of an atom or ion. In many complexes of the 3d transition series, the ligand environment strongly restricts the orbital motion of the d electrons. The orbital contribution is then said to be quenched, and the magnetic moment is often approximated using the spins of the unpaired electrons alone.

Electron pairing is therefore a useful first guide. A completely paired electron arrangement normally gives diamagnetic behaviour. A species with one or more unpaired electrons normally shows paramagnetic behaviour. However, magnetic behaviour in solids can also depend on interactions between neighbouring metal centres.

3. Diamagnetism and paramagnetism

3.1 Diamagnetism

When an external magnetic field is applied, the field induces small changes in electron motion. The induced magnetic response of a diamagnetic substance opposes the applied field. A diamagnetic substance is therefore weakly repelled by a magnetic field. Diamagnetism is present in all substances, but it is most readily observed when no stronger paramagnetic contribution is present.

Species with all electrons paired are usually classified as diamagnetic. Examples include many d0 and d10 metal-ion complexes, provided the ligands and other parts of the species do not introduce unpaired electrons.

3.2 Paramagnetism

A substance containing one or more unpaired electrons generally has a permanent magnetic moment associated with electron spin and, where not quenched, orbital motion. In an applied magnetic field, these moments tend to align with the field. Such a substance is attracted into a magnetic field and is described as paramagnetic.

Thermal motion works against alignment. As temperature increases, random motion becomes more important and the paramagnetic response of a simple paramagnet decreases.

Important: The presence of paired electrons does not remove diamagnetism from a paramagnetic complex. A complex with unpaired electrons has both diamagnetic and paramagnetic contributions; the paramagnetic contribution is usually larger.

4. Magnetic susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility describes how strongly a material responds to an applied magnetic field. It is measured experimentally and used to calculate the effective magnetic moment. The volume susceptibility is commonly represented by χv. Mass susceptibility and molar susceptibility are obtained by expressing the response per unit mass or per mole.

For a sample with mass susceptibility χg and molar mass M, the molar susceptibility is:

χM = χg × M

The measured susceptibility of a coordination compound includes a diamagnetic contribution from its electrons and constituent atoms. To estimate the paramagnetic contribution, the diamagnetic contribution is corrected for. In sign convention, if χdia is negative, then:

χM,corr = χM,observed − χM,dia

Since χM,dia is negative, subtracting it increases the corrected value. Some laboratory tables list diamagnetic correction as a positive magnitude to be added; always check the sign convention being used.

5. Curie's law and Curie–Weiss law

5.1 Curie's law

For a simple, sufficiently dilute paramagnetic material in which magnetic centres do not significantly interact, the molar paramagnetic susceptibility is inversely proportional to absolute temperature:

χM = C/T

Here C is the Curie constant and T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. Thus, increasing temperature decreases the susceptibility of a simple paramagnet.

5.2 Curie–Weiss law

When magnetic centres interact with one another, the simple Curie law may not describe the material. A common empirical relationship is:

χM = C/(T − θ)

θ is the Weiss constant. Its sign and magnitude provide information about the dominant interactions. A positive θ is commonly associated with predominantly ferromagnetic interactions, while a negative θ is commonly associated with predominantly antiferromagnetic interactions. The interpretation depends on the material and the temperature range.

6. Spin-only magnetic moment

For many 3d transition-metal complexes, orbital angular momentum is sufficiently quenched that the magnetic moment can be estimated from the number of unpaired electrons. The spin-only effective magnetic moment is:

μso = √[n(n + 2)] BM

where n is the number of unpaired electrons and BM means Bohr magneton. The Bohr magneton is the natural unit used to express atomic-scale magnetic moments.

Worked example 1: Calculate the spin-only moment.

A complex ion contains a metal centre with three unpaired electrons. Calculate its spin-only magnetic moment.

n = 3

μ = √[3(3 + 2)] = √15 = 3.87 BM.

Worked example 2: Estimate the number of unpaired electrons.

A measured effective magnetic moment is approximately 4.90 BM and the complex behaves close to the spin-only model. Which number of unpaired electrons is indicated?

For n = 4, μ = √[4(4 + 2)] = √24 = 4.90 BM. The result indicates four unpaired electrons, subject to the assumption that orbital and other contributions are small.

7. Ligand field strength, spin state and magnetism

The magnetic behaviour of a complex depends on its d-electron configuration. In an octahedral field, electrons occupy the lower t2g and higher eg sets. Whether electrons pair in t2g or occupy eg depends on the competition between the crystal-field splitting energy Δo and pairing energy P.

  • Weak-field ligands: when Δo is relatively small compared with P, electrons tend to occupy higher orbitals before pairing. This often gives a high-spin complex.
  • Strong-field ligands: when Δo is relatively large compared with P, electrons tend to pair in lower t2g orbitals before occupying eg. This can give a low-spin complex.

Orbital occupancy for octahedral d⁶: high-spin has four unpaired electrons; low-spin has none. Arrows represent electrons with opposite spins in a paired orbital.

Do not generalise high-spin/low-spin to every d count. In an octahedral field, the most important spin-state differences occur for d⁴ to d⁷. For d¹, d², d³, d⁸, d⁹ and d¹⁰, the number of unpaired electrons is not changed by the usual high-spin/low-spin choice in the same way.

8. Orbital contribution and orbital quenching

The spin-only formula is an approximation. In some complexes, orbital angular momentum contributes significantly to the magnetic moment. The ligand field can remove orbital degeneracy and restrict orbital motion; this is called orbital quenching. It is often substantial in 3d complexes, but not always complete.

Orbital contribution is more likely when the ground electronic state is orbitally degenerate, especially when it is triply degenerate (a T term). A non-degenerate A ground state often has a more strongly quenched orbital contribution. The actual magnetic moment can also be affected by spin–orbit coupling, temperature-independent paramagnetism and interactions between metal centres.

Consequently, an experimental magnetic moment that differs from the spin-only value does not automatically mean that the electron configuration has been assigned incorrectly. The electronic state and the type of metal ion must also be considered.

9. Lanthanide magnetic moments

For many lanthanide ions, the 4f electrons retain a significant orbital contribution because the 4f orbitals are relatively shielded from direct ligand-field effects. The spin-only expression is therefore often inadequate. A common free-ion expression for the effective magnetic moment is:

μeff = gJ √[J(J + 1)] BM

where J is the total angular momentum quantum number and gJ is the Landé g-factor:

gJ = 1 + [J(J + 1) + S(S + 1) − L(L + 1)] / [2J(J + 1)]

L is total orbital angular momentum and S is total spin angular momentum. For less-than-half-filled 4f subshells, Hund's rules give J = |L − S|; for more-than-half-filled subshells, J = L + S. The special half-filled case requires care because J may be zero.

Scope: The expression above is a free-ion approximation. Real lanthanide complexes can show effects from crystal fields, thermal population of levels and other interactions. Use the spin-only formula for many 3d ions only when orbital contributions are reasonably quenched.

10. Magnetic ordering in solids

10.1 Ferromagnetism

In a ferromagnetic material, neighbouring magnetic moments tend to align parallel. This produces a strong net magnetisation. Above the Curie temperature, thermal disorder destroys long-range ferromagnetic order and the material becomes paramagnetic.

10.2 Antiferromagnetism

In an antiferromagnetic material, neighbouring moments tend to align in opposite directions with equal or nearly equal magnitudes. Their contributions cancel, giving little or no net magnetisation below the ordering temperature. This temperature is commonly called the Néel temperature.

10.3 Ferrimagnetism

In a ferrimagnetic material, magnetic moments on different sites align in opposite directions but are unequal in magnitude. They do not cancel completely, so a net magnetic moment remains. Magnetite, Fe3O4, is a familiar example.

11. Spin crossover

Some coordination compounds can exist in both high-spin and low-spin electronic states. If the energy difference between the two states is comparable with thermal energy, changing temperature can alter their relative populations. This is called spin crossover.

For example, certain iron(II) complexes with d⁶ configuration can be high-spin at higher temperature and low-spin at lower temperature. In the high-spin state there are four unpaired electrons; in the low-spin state there are no unpaired electrons. The magnetic moment therefore decreases as the low-spin population increases. Spin crossover may also produce changes in metal–ligand bond lengths, colour and other physical properties.

12. A reliable method for solving magnetic-moment questions

  1. Find the oxidation state of the central metal.
  2. Determine the metal's d-electron count.
  3. Identify the coordination geometry.
  4. For octahedral d⁴–d⁷ complexes, decide whether the ligand field gives high spin or low spin. For tetrahedral complexes, the splitting is usually small and complexes are generally high spin.
  5. Draw the d-orbital occupancy and count unpaired electrons.
  6. Calculate μ = √[n(n + 2)] BM if the spin-only approximation is appropriate.
  7. Compare the calculated value with any experimental value and consider orbital contribution, spin–orbit coupling and magnetic interactions if they differ.
Worked example 3: [Fe(CN)6]4−.

Find the spin-only magnetic moment.

  1. Let the oxidation state of Fe be x: x + 6(−1) = −4, so x = +2.
  2. Fe(II) is d⁶.
  3. CN⁻ is a strong-field ligand in an octahedral complex, so the configuration is low-spin t2g⁶ eg⁰.
  4. There are zero unpaired electrons.
  5. μ = √[0(0 + 2)] = 0 BM; it is diamagnetic in the spin-only description.
Worked example 4: [FeF6]3−.

Find the number of unpaired electrons and the spin-only moment.

  1. Let Fe oxidation state be x: x + 6(−1) = −3, so x = +3.
  2. Fe(III) is d⁵.
  3. F⁻ is a weak-field ligand; the octahedral complex is high-spin.
  4. High-spin d⁵ has five unpaired electrons.
  5. μ = √[5(5 + 2)] = √35 = 5.92 BM.
Worked example 5: [NiCl4]2−.

Predict the magnetic behaviour and spin-only moment.

  1. Ni oxidation state is +2; Ni(II) is d⁸.
  2. The complex is tetrahedral. Tetrahedral complexes are normally high-spin because their splitting is relatively small.
  3. For tetrahedral d⁸ there are two unpaired electrons.
  4. μ = √[2(2 + 2)] = √8 = 2.83 BM.

13. Practice questions

1. Define diamagnetism and paramagnetism. Explain the role of electron pairing in each.
2. Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment for a complex with two unpaired electrons.
3. A complex has a spin-only magnetic moment of approximately 3.87 BM. Estimate the number of unpaired electrons.
4. Determine the number of unpaired electrons in octahedral [CoF6]3−. (Co(III) is d⁶.)
5. Determine the number of unpaired electrons in octahedral [Co(CN)6]3−. (Co(III) is d⁶.)
6. State Curie's law and explain the effect of increasing temperature on a simple paramagnet.
7. Distinguish ferromagnetism, antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism in terms of spin alignment.
8. Explain why the spin-only formula may not accurately predict the magnetic moment of some lanthanide ions.
9. What is meant by orbital quenching?
10. Explain spin crossover and predict how the magnetic moment changes when a d⁶ iron(II) complex changes from high-spin to low-spin.

Answer guide

  1. Diamagnetism is a weak response opposing the applied field and is usually dominant when all electrons are paired. Paramagnetism is attraction to a field due to one or more unpaired electrons.
  2. μ = √[2(2 + 2)] = 2.83 BM.
  3. n = 3 because √[3(5)] = 3.87 BM.
  4. Co(III) is d⁶; F⁻ is weak-field; high-spin t2g⁴eg²; 4 unpaired electrons.
  5. Co(III) is d⁶; CN⁻ is strong-field; low-spin t2g⁶; 0 unpaired electrons.
  6. χ = C/T. Increasing temperature decreases susceptibility because thermal motion disrupts alignment.
  7. Ferromagnetic: parallel; antiferromagnetic: equal opposite; ferrimagnetic: unequal opposite moments.
  8. Lanthanide 4f electrons can retain significant orbital angular momentum, so the spin-only model neglects an important contribution.
  9. Restriction of orbital angular momentum by the ligand environment, reducing its contribution to the magnetic moment.
  10. Thermal change in the population of high-spin and low-spin states. For d⁶ iron(II), changing high-spin to low-spin changes four unpaired electrons to zero, so the magnetic moment decreases.

14. Chapter summary

  • Diamagnetic substances are weakly repelled by a magnetic field; paramagnetic substances with unpaired electrons are attracted.
  • Magnetic susceptibility is measured experimentally and corrected for diamagnetic contributions when determining the paramagnetic moment.
  • For many 3d complexes with quenched orbital contributions, μ = √[n(n + 2)] BM.
  • Ligand strength and geometry determine electron pairing and therefore the number of unpaired electrons.
  • Orbital contributions, spin–orbit coupling and magnetic interactions can cause deviations from spin-only values.
  • Lanthanide moments often require the total-angular-momentum expression.
  • Ferromagnetism, antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism arise from different arrangements of neighbouring magnetic moments.
  • Spin crossover occurs when high-spin and low-spin states have comparable energies and their populations change, for example with temperature.
