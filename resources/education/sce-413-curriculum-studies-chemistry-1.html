Curriculum Studies in Chemistry I

1. Module overview and learning outcomes

Curriculum Studies in Chemistry examines the relationship between what a society expects learners to learn, what schools plan to teach, what teachers actually teach and what students finally understand. It connects curriculum theory with the practical responsibilities of a Chemistry teacher.

The supplied module outline identifies the following broad learning outcomes. By the end of the module, a student should be able to:

  • distinguish the core elements of the school Chemistry curriculum;
  • design teaching units from a curriculum;
  • appraise the purpose and practice of practical work;
  • identify essential features of a Chemistry laboratory and explain their importance;
  • discuss effective laboratory management;
  • design experiments for school learners; and
  • assess learners' performance during practical activities.
Central idea: effective Chemistry teaching requires alignment. Learning outcomes, content, activities, resources and assessment should support one another.

2. Understanding curriculum and curriculum design

2.1 Meaning of curriculum

A curriculum is the organised plan of learning that identifies the knowledge, skills, values, experiences and outcomes that an educational programme intends learners to develop. It is broader than a list of topics: it also includes how learning is organised and how achievement is evaluated.

For example, a Chemistry curriculum may require learners to understand acids and bases, perform a safe neutralisation experiment, interpret observations and apply the concept to soil treatment or water quality.

2.2 Curriculum design

Curriculum design is the deliberate process of selecting, organising and connecting learning outcomes, subject content, teaching experiences and assessment. Sound design begins by deciding what learners should know and be able to do, then planning backwards to determine what learning experiences will help them get there.

Learning standards and outcomes→Content and sequence→Teaching experiences→Assessment evidence→Review and improvement

2.3 Features of a good curriculum

  • Coherence: topics and skills connect logically within and across subjects.
  • Continuity: important concepts are revisited and developed over time.
  • Progression: learning moves from foundational ideas to more demanding applications.
  • Relevance: content connects with learners' lives, society, industry and the environment.
  • Clarity: outcomes and expectations are understandable to teachers and learners.
  • Evaluability: evidence can be collected to determine whether intended learning is taking place.
  • Adaptability: teachers can respond to learner needs and available resources without losing the intended outcomes.

2.4 Curriculum framework components

ComponentMeaningChemistry example
Content standardsBroad knowledge or skills learners should acquire.Understand the particulate nature of matter.
BenchmarksProgress checkpoints at a particular stage.Explain diffusion using particle movement by the end of a unit.
Performance indicatorsObservable evidence used to judge mastery.Interpret a diffusion investigation and explain the result.

3. Curriculum types and the secondary curriculum in Malawi

3.1 Three types of curriculum

TypeExplanationExample
Intended curriculumWhat education authorities expect learners to study.Syllabus, curriculum framework and approved teacher guidance.
Implemented curriculumWhat teachers and schools actually organise and deliver.Schemes of work, lesson plans, classroom and laboratory lessons.
Attained curriculumWhat learners actually acquire through their educational experiences.Knowledge, skills, attitudes and values demonstrated by students.

The three may differ. A syllabus can include practical investigation, but a school may lack equipment and teach the topic theoretically. Even when a lesson is delivered, some learners may not achieve the intended outcome. Curriculum evaluation therefore compares intended, implemented and attained learning.

3.2 Developmental and secondary education outcomes

The supplied material explains that Malawi's secondary curriculum is linked to broader national development aims and to the skills expected of a secondary-school graduate. The seven groups highlighted are citizenship; ethical and socio-cultural; economic development and environmental management; occupational and entrepreneurial; practical; creativity and resourcefulness; and scientific and technological skills.

These outcomes show why Chemistry should not be taught as memorisation alone. Learners should use scientific reasoning, practical competence, responsible decision-making and knowledge of environmental effects.

4. Curriculum mapping

4.1 Meaning and purpose

Curriculum mapping is the systematic recording of what is taught, when it is taught, how it is taught and how learning is assessed. It helps teachers compare the intended curriculum with classroom delivery and assessment evidence.

Mapping can reveal gaps (important content not taught), unnecessary repetition (the same content repeated without progression) and misalignment (tests assess content or skills that were not adequately taught).

4.2 Four forms of coherence

FormWhat it meansExample
VerticalLearning progresses from one grade or level to the next.Particle ideas introduced before explaining gas laws quantitatively.
HorizontalLearners at the same level receive comparable learning opportunities.Different Form 2 classes cover the same required Chemistry outcomes.
Subject-areaTopics and standards are coherent within Chemistry.Practical measurement skills are developed consistently across units.
InterdisciplinaryLearning connects meaningfully across subjects.Using mathematics for concentration calculations and geography for water-resource contexts.

4.3 Steps in creating a curriculum map

  1. Define clear, measurable learning outcomes.
  2. Identify the content and key topics that support each outcome.
  3. Select appropriate teaching and learning activities.
  4. Sequence the content and allocate realistic time.
  5. Build a curriculum matrix linking outcomes, content, activities, resources and assessment.
  6. Review the map with colleagues and gather feedback.
  7. Monitor actual teaching and learner progress, then revise the map.

4.4 Example: mapping chemical composition of matter

Learning outcome: learners appreciate the composition and properties of natural and synthetic substances in their environment.

Possible success criteria: define matter; explain the particulate model; distinguish elements, compounds and mixtures; interpret chemical formulae; classify solutions; investigate solubility; and select a suitable separation technique.

Mapping gap to look for: learners may memorise definitions without applying them to local materials, environmental questions or practical separation tasks. A stronger map includes investigations, real samples, data interpretation and an assessment of practical reasoning.

6. Development of teaching units

6.1 What is a teaching unit?

A teaching unit is a connected block of curriculum content and associated skills organised around a topic or group of related concepts. It usually takes several lessons and is designed to achieve specified learning outcomes.

6.2 Unit plan compared with a lesson plan

Unit planLesson plan
Organises a broader topic over several lessons or weeks.Organises one teaching session.
Shows the overall sequence, outcomes, assessment and resources.Specifies the objectives, activities, timing and assessment for the day.
Ensures continuity and balance across the topic.Guides the teacher through the immediate learning experience.

6.3 Why plan a unit?

  • It gives the teacher an overview of the topic and its relationship to the course.
  • It supports logical sequencing from simple to complex ideas.
  • It helps allocate time, equipment and other resources.
  • It balances theoretical content, practical skills and assessment.
  • It makes differentiation, remedial teaching and enrichment easier to plan.
  • It helps the teacher identify links between textbook content and competencies.

6.4 Steps in unit planning

  1. Estimate the full course content and required competencies.
  2. Calculate the teaching time available, allowing for school events and assessment.
  3. Arrange the content in a logical teaching sequence.
  4. Identify related concepts and prerequisite knowledge.
  5. Divide the course into manageable units. A unit should be coherent and achievable within a reasonable period.
  6. Break each unit into individual lessons.
  7. Choose teaching methods, learning materials, learner activities and assessment for each lesson.
  8. Record the plan in a table and review it during implementation.

6.5 Main elements of a teaching unit

  • Unit title and overview.
  • Learning outcomes and measurable success criteria.
  • Content outline and key vocabulary.
  • Lesson sequence and estimated time.
  • Teaching strategies and learner activities.
  • Resources, including apparatus and locally available materials.
  • Formative and summative assessment.
  • Unit summary, reflection and follow-up support.

6.6 Example unit: atomic structure and periodic table

Unit componentExample
OverviewExplore subatomic particles, atomic models, electron arrangement and periodic organisation.
OutcomeExplain atomic structure and relate an element's position to selected properties.
Success criteriaIdentify protons, neutrons and electrons; interpret atomic number and mass number; explain groups and periods; use electron arrangement to predict simple trends.
ActivitiesBuild particle models, interpret periodic-table data, solve electron-configuration exercises and discuss patterns.
AssessmentQuestioning, worksheets, model explanation, short quiz and unit test.

6.7 Limitations and how to respond

Planning can be time-consuming, difficult for inexperienced teachers and too rigid if followed without adjustment. Dividing connected content into units can also be artificial. A good plan is therefore a guide, not a rule: teachers should revise pacing and activities when evidence shows that learners need more time or a different approach.

7. Schemes of work and records of work

7.1 Meaning

A scheme of work (also called a scheme of records in the supplied lecture material) is a structured plan for distributing curriculum content and learning activities across a term or academic year. A record of work documents what was actually covered, when it was taught and any issues that affected progress.

7.2 Why schemes and records matter

  • Promote systematic syllabus coverage and realistic time management.
  • Guide weekly and daily lesson preparation.
  • Support consistency between classes and teachers.
  • Provide evidence for supervision, evaluation and accountability.
  • Help teachers continue work after a staff transfer or absence.
  • Support continuous assessment and future planning.

7.3 Main components

ColumnWhat to record
Week/dateTeaching period and dates.
Success criteriaWhat learners should demonstrate.
ActivitiesTasks learners and teacher will carry out.
MethodologiesDiscussion, demonstration, practical work, group work or other approaches.
ResourcesApparatus, chemicals, textbooks, charts and references.
OutcomesWhat learners actually achieved.
RemarksDifficulties, follow-up, reteaching and adjustments.

7.4 Developing a scheme

  1. Review the syllabus and identify all required topics.
  2. Break the content into teachable weekly sections.
  3. Check the school calendar, holidays, examinations and other events.
  4. Choose suitable teaching approaches for each topic.
  5. Allocate resources and references.
  6. Set success criteria and expected outcomes.
  7. Plan assessment activities.
  8. Record the decisions in a clear table and update the record after teaching.

Important factors include available time, breadth of content, learner needs, class size, resources, assessment dates and school policy. Schemes should be flexible enough to accommodate interruptions without losing track of essential outcomes.

8. Role and practice of practical work in Chemistry

8.1 Meaning

Practical work is a learning activity in which learners observe, handle or manipulate real materials, objects or direct representations of scientific phenomena. It may involve planning, carrying out procedures, collecting data, analysing evidence and communicating conclusions.

8.2 Aims of practical work

  1. Motivation: practical experiences can stimulate curiosity and interest.
  2. Laboratory skills: learners practise measuring, observing, handling apparatus and recording data.
  3. Scientific method: learners develop questions, hypotheses, predictions, control variables and interpret evidence.
  4. Scientific attitudes: careful work encourages honesty, objectivity, open-mindedness and willingness to revise a conclusion.
  5. Understanding theory: observations can make abstract ideas more concrete and help learners connect models with evidence.

8.3 Why practical work does not automatically produce learning

A practical can become a recipe-following exercise if students focus only on completing steps or obtaining an expected result. Busy laboratory activity may distract from the scientific idea. Some procedures do not allow learners to practise every stage of scientific investigation. Teachers should therefore make the learning purpose explicit, ask learners to predict and explain, and assess reasoning as well as technique.

8.4 Common challenges

  • Limited lesson time and pressure to cover theoretical content.
  • Complex procedures that overload learners.
  • Limited or uneven access to apparatus, chemicals and safe facilities.
  • Difficulty assessing practical skills consistently.
  • Large classes and insufficient supervision.

8.5 Improving practical work

  • Plan experiments around a small number of clear learning outcomes.
  • Break complex tasks into scaffolded stages.
  • Use common assessment criteria and observable performance indicators.
  • Adapt activities to safe, low-cost locally available materials where scientifically appropriate.
  • Use simulations to supplement—not falsely claim to replace—skills that require real apparatus handling.

9. Types of practical work

TypeDescriptionBest use
DemonstrationTeacher performs an experiment while learners observe, predict and explain.Hazardous, costly, difficult or visually striking experiments, provided learners can see and engage with the evidence.
Whole-class experimentLearners or groups perform the same task.Developing common practical skills and comparing results.
Circus of experimentsGroups rotate through stations with different related tasks.When equipment is limited and several short activities support one topic.
Simulation or role-playLearners use models, digital tools, data or roles to represent a process.Visualising difficult-to-observe processes or supplementing limited equipment.
InvestigationLearners explore a question, collect evidence and evaluate conclusions.Developing inquiry, experimental design and independent reasoning.
Problem-solving activityLearners use evidence and chemical principles to solve a defined problem.Applying knowledge to unfamiliar or real-world situations.

9.1 Demonstration: Predict–Observe–Explain

Predict→Observe→Explain

Before the demonstration, learners write what they expect and why. They then record what actually happens and compare the observation with their prediction. Finally, they explain the result using Chemistry. Arrange seating so all learners can see, provide a recording task and prepare alternative learning activities for groups not directly interacting with the teacher.

9.2 Whole-class practical stages

  1. Set the scene: explain the purpose, safety rules, procedure and group roles.
  2. Gather results: decide how data will be recorded and shared.
  3. Discuss observations and compare results.
  4. Interpret evidence and develop conclusions with learner participation.
  5. Write up the work where appropriate, including method, results, analysis and limitations.

9.3 Organising a practical-work circus

Prepare and label every station before learners enter. Make tasks similar in duration, provide clear written instructions, plan the rotation sequence and include extension work for groups that finish early. Check that each station is safe and adequately supervised.

9.4 Investigative process

Identify problem→Plan→Investigate→Collect data→Interpret→Conclude→Evaluate

10. Laboratory design and safety

A school Chemistry laboratory should enable safe movement, effective teaching, preparation, storage, cleaning and emergency response. Design should be based on the number and age of users, experiments performed, accessibility and applicable safety standards.

FeatureReason it matters
Clearly marked, accessible emergency exitsAllow orderly evacuation without obstruction.
Stable, appropriately sized benchesProvide safe working surfaces and reduce spills or breakage.
Adequate aisles and circulation spaceReduce collisions and allow access during an emergency.
Sinks and suitable glassware-washing facilitiesSupport hygiene and safe cleaning.
Lockable cupboards and suitable storageSecure chemicals and equipment and reduce clutter.
Suitable ventilation and fume cupboardsControl hazardous vapours where risk assessment requires them.
Preparation roomAllows staff to prepare materials away from active student work areas.
Emergency washing facilities and first-aid arrangementsSupport prompt response to exposure or injury.
Technician workspace and records accessSupport inventory, maintenance and preparation.
Separate staff rest areaKeeps food and drink away from laboratory contamination risks.

10.1 General laboratory safety principles

  • Complete a risk assessment before each practical and follow institutional procedures.
  • Use suitable eye protection, protective clothing and other required PPE.
  • Label all containers clearly and keep chemicals in compatible, approved storage.
  • Never eat, drink, taste chemicals or mouth-pipette in the laboratory.
  • Know the locations and correct use of exits, eyewash, safety shower, spill equipment and fire-response equipment.
  • Dispose of chemical waste using approved procedures; do not assume that dilution or pouring into a sink is safe.
  • Report incidents, near misses, damaged equipment and missing labels promptly.
Safety correction: chemical compatibility, storage temperature, ventilation and peroxide-forming chemicals require current safety data sheets and institutional risk assessment. Do not follow informal storage or chemical-testing instructions from old slides as a substitute for approved procedures. Peroxide testing and handling of unstable chemicals must be performed only by trained personnel using an approved protocol.

11. Storage, ordering and stock control

11.1 Why good storage matters

Insufficient storage encourages chemicals and apparatus to be left on benches or floors, increasing the risk of spills, breakage, contamination and obstruction. Storage should be planned before equipment and chemicals accumulate.

11.2 Equipment storage

  • Keep similar equipment together in labelled, suitable drawers or cupboards.
  • Store fragile glassware so it cannot fall or be crushed.
  • Maintain a stock record showing item name, identification details, supplier, purchase date, quantity and location.
  • Record damaged, missing or out-of-service equipment separately.

11.3 Chemical storage

  • Use original or approved compatible containers with clear labels.
  • Separate incompatible chemicals using a recognised chemical-compatibility system, not simply alphabetical order.
  • Keep containers secure and store corrosives and heavy bottles at safe, accessible heights.
  • Restrict access to hazardous substances as required by policy.
  • Use suitable ventilation and temperature controls specified by the chemical's safety information.
  • Maintain an up-to-date inventory and safety data sheets.

11.4 Ordering chemicals and apparatus

Before ordering, confirm the teaching requirement, available stock, quantity, grade or specification, catalogue reference where applicable, pack size, supplier and budget. Buy quantities that can be used safely before expiry or deterioration. Use a reorder level so essential supplies can be replaced in time.

11.5 Inventory and stock control

Stock control is the process of keeping the right quantity of suitable materials available for laboratory activities while limiting waste, deterioration, unnecessary cost and storage risk. A designated person should oversee issue and return records.

Stock-book fieldPurpose
Item name and specificationIdentifies the exact material or apparatus.
Quantity receivedRecords new stock.
Date received and supplierProvides traceability.
Expiry or review dateSupports safe use and stock rotation where relevant.
Quantity issued and balanceTracks consumption and remaining stock.
Storage location and responsible personHelps staff find and account for items.

12. Designing and conducting experiments

An effective school experiment should be scientifically valid, safe, achievable with available resources and aligned with the intended learning outcomes. It should be designed for the learners' age, prior knowledge and practical experience.

12.1 A systematic design process

  1. Identify the learning outcome and the chemical idea to be investigated.
  2. Write a focused investigable question.
  3. Identify the independent variable, dependent variable and important control variables.
  4. Choose apparatus and materials that are appropriate, available and safe.
  5. Plan a method that produces observable or measurable evidence.
  6. Consider repeats, measurement precision and a suitable results table.
  7. Carry out a teacher trial to identify hazards, timing issues and procedural problems.
  8. Prepare learner instructions, safety information and assessment criteria.
  9. Plan how results will be analysed and linked back to the Chemistry.

12.2 Example: investigating how temperature affects dissolving time

Question: How does water temperature affect the time taken for a fixed amount of a chosen soluble solid to dissolve?

Variables: independent—water temperature; dependent—time taken to dissolve; controls—water volume, solid identity and mass, stirring method and container.

Evidence: learners record the temperature and dissolving time for each trial, repeat measurements where practical and compare the trend. The teacher should select a safe substance and temperature range through a risk assessment before the activity.

12.3 Apparatus and improvisation

Improvised apparatus can support learning when standard equipment is unavailable, but it must be stable, chemically compatible, accurate enough for the learning purpose and safe. Do not improvise pressure vessels, heating systems, electrical equipment or containers for hazardous chemicals. Explain the limitations of improvised measuring devices so learners understand measurement uncertainty.

12.4 Laboratory records

Records should be clear, dated and traceable. Include the experiment title, aim, risk controls, materials, method, raw observations, processed results, calculations, conclusion, limitations and any incident or deviation. Do not alter raw data to make it fit an expected result.

13. Facilitating student laboratory activities

13.1 Before the lesson

  • Review the procedure, curriculum objective and current safety requirements.
  • Check apparatus, chemicals, labels, waste arrangements and emergency facilities.
  • Perform a trial run and estimate the time required.
  • Prepare worksheets, pre-lab questions, observation checklists and report rubrics.
  • Plan group size, seating, movement and support for learners with different needs.

13.2 Preparing learners

  • Give a safety briefing that addresses hazards specific to the experiment.
  • Explain the aim, underlying theory, procedure and expected type of evidence.
  • Ask learners to make predictions or formulate a hypothesis.
  • Use a short pre-lab quiz or questioning to identify misunderstandings.
  • Demonstrate unfamiliar apparatus and techniques before independent use.

13.3 During the experiment

  • Move around the room and observe every group.
  • Check safe handling, measurement technique, recording and cooperation.
  • Ask questions that make learners explain what they are doing and why.
  • Stop unsafe work immediately and correct serious procedural errors.
  • Give timely feedback that identifies both effective practice and improvements needed.

13.4 After the experiment

  • Ensure equipment is cleaned, waste is handled correctly and chemicals are returned safely.
  • Discuss results, unexpected observations and possible sources of error.
  • Connect evidence to the relevant chemical model or theory.
  • Use post-lab questions and learner reflection to identify what needs reteaching.

14. Assessing practical skills and laboratory learning

Assessment should examine not only the final result but also how learners plan, work safely, use apparatus, collect evidence and interpret findings.

MethodWhat it assessesStrength
Direct observationTechnique, safety, collaboration and procedure.Allows immediate feedback during the activity.
Laboratory reportRecording, calculations, analysis, conclusion and scientific communication.Shows how learners interpret evidence.
Practical examinationIndividual performance of specified techniques.Provides direct evidence of competence.
PortfolioPractical records, reflections and progress over time.Shows development rather than one isolated performance.
Quiz or simulationTheoretical understanding, safety decisions and interpretation.Useful for formative checks and situations where real practical work is not possible.

14.1 Example practical assessment rubric

CriterionEvidence of achievement
PreparationUnderstands the aim, method and safety requirements.
SafetyUses PPE correctly and follows safe handling and waste procedures.
TechniqueHandles apparatus appropriately and follows the method carefully.
Data qualityRecords observations accurately with units and suitable precision.
InterpretationUses results to explain patterns and identify limitations.
CommunicationPresents a clear report and justifies the conclusion with evidence.

14.2 Reflection and peer assessment

Reflection questions may ask what was most difficult, how a problem was resolved, how the experiment connects to real applications and what should change if the experiment is repeated. Peer assessment should use clear criteria and focus on constructive feedback. Self-assessment can help learners monitor safety, measurement accuracy, apparatus use and data interpretation.

15. Using locally available materials and facilities

Local contexts can make Chemistry meaningful and reduce barriers to practical learning. Possible learning contexts include water treatment facilities, food preparation and preservation, soap and detergent use, cement and mining industries, dairy processing, clinics, pharmacies, agricultural activities, local craft production and environmental sites.

Teachers should distinguish between using a local context for discussion, using a safe household material in a controlled activity and visiting an industrial site. Each has different risks. Do not collect, mix or use unknown chemicals or industrial waste without expert approval and a formal risk assessment.

Examples of curriculum connections

  • Water treatment: filtration, sedimentation, disinfection and water-quality indicators.
  • Food preservation: acidity, oxidation, concentration and microbial growth.
  • Soils and agriculture: pH, fertilisers, nutrients and environmental effects.
  • Soap and detergents: surfactants, emulsification and cleaning action.
  • Building materials: properties of cement, lime and mineral resources.

Guided answer points

1. Intended curriculum is what the syllabus expects; implemented curriculum is what is taught; attained curriculum is what learners actually acquire.
2. Outcomes clarify the destination. Content, teaching methods and assessment can then be selected to produce and measure the required learning.
3. Vertical coherence connects learning across grades or levels; horizontal coherence provides comparable learning at the same level across classes or schools.
4. Mapping identifies gaps and repetition, aligns assessment with teaching, supports progression and helps teachers coordinate resources and timing.
5. Analytical skills; inorganic compounds; chemical composition of matter; chemical reactions; organic chemistry; environmental chemistry.
6. A unit plan organises several related lessons around a broader outcome; a lesson plan gives the detailed structure for one session.
7. Estimate content and time, sequence topics, identify links, group content into units, break units into lessons, choose activities and assessment, then document and review.
8. Week/date, success criteria, activities, methodologies, resources/references, outcomes and remarks. These connect timing, intended learning, delivery and reflection.
9. Motivation, laboratory skills, scientific-method understanding, scientific attitudes and support for conceptual understanding.
10. Learners may follow instructions mechanically, focus on obtaining an expected result or become distracted by procedural demands. Explicit questions and discussion are needed to connect evidence with theory.
11. A demonstration is teacher-led; a whole-class experiment has learners perform the same activity; a circus rotates groups through different stations.
12. Learners predict an outcome, observe the actual evidence and explain similarities or differences using scientific ideas.
13. Examples include accessible emergency exits, adequate bench spacing, sinks, secure storage, suitable ventilation and a preparation room. Explain each in relation to safety and function.
14. Alphabetical order does not account for chemical reactions between incompatible substances. Compatibility-based storage reduces the risk of fire, toxic release or other dangerous reactions.
15. A stock book records what is held, received and issued; a reorder level signals when replenishment should begin before stock runs out.
16. Define outcomes and question, identify variables, select safe materials, plan measurements and repeats, trial the method, prepare instructions and assess evidence.
17. Before: check risk controls, materials and instructions. During: monitor technique, understanding and safety. After: manage waste, discuss evidence, assess and reflect.
18. Observation assesses performance as it occurs; reports assess recording and interpretation; practical exams assess individual technique under controlled conditions.
19. Examples include water treatment, agricultural soils and fertilisers, food preservation, household detergents and industrial waste. Explain the Chemistry and safety considerations.
20. Identify which outcomes were achieved, analyse misconceptions or skill gaps, then adjust sequencing, time, explanations, practical tasks and support.
Study tip: For essay questions, define the concept, explain its purpose, give a Chemistry classroom example and evaluate at least one limitation or practical consideration.
