Thermal Expansion and Heat Transfer

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

When substances are heated, their particles gain energy and generally move farther apart. As a result, most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled.

Heat can be transferred from one place to another by conduction, convection and radiation.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Explain thermal expansion using particle theory.
  • Describe expansion of solids, liquids and gases.
  • Explain everyday applications and disadvantages of expansion.
  • Distinguish conduction, convection and radiation.
  • Explain how heat is transferred through different materials.
  • Explain good and poor thermal conductors.
  • Apply heat-transfer ideas to everyday situations.

3. Thermal Expansion

Thermal expansion is the increase in size of a substance when its temperature increases.

When heated, particles vibrate or move more vigorously. Their average separation increases, causing the substance to expand.

StateExpansion when heated
SolidSmallest expansion
LiquidGreater expansion than most solids
GasGenerally greatest expansion

4. Expansion of Solids

Solids expand in length, area and volume when heated.

The expansion of a solid is usually small, but it becomes important when structures are long or exposed to large temperature changes.

Examples of applications:

  • Gaps are left between railway tracks.
  • Expansion joints are provided in bridges and concrete structures.
  • Overhead electrical wires are installed with some slack.
  • Metal lids can become easier to open after heating.

5. Bimetallic Strip

A bimetallic strip consists of two different metals joined together. The metals expand by different amounts when heated.

Because one metal expands more than the other, the strip bends.

Applications include:

  • thermostats;
  • automatic switches;
  • temperature-control devices.
The bending occurs because the two metals have different rates of thermal expansion.

6. Expansion of Liquids

Liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled. This property is used in liquid-in-glass thermometers.

When a liquid is heated inside a narrow tube, a small volume change produces a noticeable movement of the liquid column.

7. Expansion of Gases

Gases expand significantly when heated.

For example, heating air in a balloon can cause the air to expand. Hot air also becomes less dense and can rise, which is important in convection and hot-air balloons.

8. The Anomalous Expansion of Water

Water behaves differently from most substances near its freezing point.

When water is cooled from 4 °C to 0 °C, it expands rather than continuing to contract.

Water has its maximum density at approximately 4 °C. This unusual behaviour helps aquatic life survive in very cold conditions because ice forms at the surface.

9. Heat Transfer

Heat can be transferred by three main processes:

MethodMain requirementTypical example
ConductionParticles transfer energy through a materialMetal spoon in hot tea
ConvectionBulk movement of a fluidBoiling water
RadiationElectromagnetic radiationHeat from the Sun

10. Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a substance without bulk movement of the substance.

In solids, particles transfer energy to neighbouring particles. Metals are especially good conductors because their mobile electrons also help transfer energy.

Good conductors include:

  • copper
  • aluminium
  • iron
  • steel

Poor conductors, or thermal insulators, include:

  • wood
  • plastic
  • rubber
  • air

11. Convection

Convection is the transfer of heat through the bulk movement of a fluid such as a liquid or gas.

When a fluid is heated, it generally expands and becomes less dense. The warmer fluid rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks. This produces a convection current.

Example: In a saucepan of water being heated, warm water rises while cooler water moves downward towards the heat source.

12. Radiation

Thermal radiation is the transfer of thermal energy by electromagnetic waves.

Unlike conduction and convection, radiation does not require a material medium and can travel through a vacuum.

This is how energy from the Sun reaches Earth.

Dark, dull surfaces are generally good absorbers and emitters of thermal radiation, while shiny, light-coloured surfaces are generally poor absorbers and emitters.

13. Absorption and Emission of Radiation

SurfaceAbsorptionEmission
Black and dullGoodGood
White and shinyPoorPoor

This principle is useful in designing solar heaters, cooking equipment and thermal insulation.

14. Applications in Everyday Life

  • Cooking pots: Metal conducts heat efficiently.
  • Pot handles: Plastic or wood reduces heat transfer to the hand.
  • Thermos flasks: Designed to reduce conduction, convection and radiation.
  • Refrigerators: Insulating materials reduce unwanted heat transfer.
  • Buildings: Roof and wall insulation reduces heat transfer.
  • Solar heaters: Dark surfaces absorb radiation effectively.

15. Thermos Flask

A thermos flask is designed to reduce heat transfer.

PartPurpose
VacuumReduces conduction and convection.
Silvered/shiny surfacesReduce radiation.
Insulating stopperReduces conduction and convection through the top.
Double wallsHelp reduce heat transfer.
No practical thermos flask completely stops heat transfer; it only reduces it.

17. MANEB-Style Practice Questions

1. Define thermal expansion.
2. Explain why gaps are left between sections of railway track.
3. State the three methods of heat transfer.
4. Explain why convection occurs in a heated liquid.
5. Why can heat from the Sun reach Earth through space?
6. State whether a black dull surface or a shiny surface is the better absorber of thermal radiation.
7. Explain how the vacuum in a thermos flask reduces heat transfer.
8. Explain why metal is used for the base of many cooking pots but plastic is used for their handles.

18. Answers

  1. The increase in size of a substance when its temperature increases.
  2. To provide space for expansion when the rails become hotter and prevent buckling.
  3. Conduction, convection and radiation.
  4. Heating causes the fluid to expand and become less dense, so it rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks.
  5. Radiation does not require a material medium and can travel through a vacuum.
  6. Black and dull surfaces are better absorbers.
  7. A vacuum contains very few particles, so conduction and convection are greatly reduced.
  8. Metal is a good conductor and transfers heat to the food efficiently; plastic is a poor conductor and reduces heat reaching the hand.

19. Common Mistakes

  • Saying that convection occurs in solids.
  • Saying radiation requires air or another medium.
  • Confusing conduction with convection.
  • Forgetting that gases generally expand more than solids.
  • Failing to explain the particle movement involved in thermal expansion.
  • Confusing good absorbers with good reflectors of radiation.

20. Examination Tips

  • For conduction questions, mention energy transfer between neighbouring particles.
  • For convection questions, mention density changes and movement of the fluid.
  • For radiation questions, mention electromagnetic waves and that no medium is required.
  • For thermal expansion questions, explain what happens to particles when heated.
  • Use clear everyday examples when explaining applications.
