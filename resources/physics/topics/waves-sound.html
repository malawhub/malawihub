Waves and Sound

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter over the whole distance.

Waves are responsible for many familiar phenomena, including sound, water waves, light, radio communication and vibrations.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Define a wave.
  • Distinguish transverse and longitudinal waves.
  • Identify amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period.
  • Use the wave equation.
  • Explain reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference.
  • Describe sound as a longitudinal wave.
  • Explain pitch, loudness and quality of sound.
  • Describe echoes and applications of ultrasound.

3. Types of Waves

TypeParticle motionExample
TransversePerpendicular to direction of wave travelWater surface waves, light
LongitudinalParallel to direction of wave travelSound in air
Transverse wave: A wave in which particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer.
Longitudinal wave: A wave in which particles vibrate parallel to the direction of energy transfer.

4. Parts of a Transverse Wave

  • Crest: Highest point of a transverse wave.
  • Trough: Lowest point.
  • Amplitude: Maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.
  • Wavelength: Distance between two successive points in phase, such as crest to crest.

5. Wave Quantities

Wavelength (\(\lambda\)): Distance between successive corresponding points on a wave.
Frequency (\(f\)): Number of complete waves produced per second. Unit: hertz (Hz).
Period (T): Time taken for one complete wave.
Amplitude: Maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.
\[ T=\frac{1}{f} \]

6. Wave Equation

\[ v=f\lambda \]

where:

  • \(v\) = wave speed in m/s
  • \(f\) = frequency in Hz
  • \(\lambda\) = wavelength in m
Example: A wave has frequency 5 Hz and wavelength 2 m. Calculate its speed.
\[ v=f\lambda \] \[ v=5\times2 \] \[ \boxed{v=10\text{ m/s}} \]

7. Longitudinal Waves

Longitudinal waves consist of alternating regions of:

  • Compressions: regions where particles are close together.
  • Rarefactions: regions where particles are spread apart.
Sound travelling through air is a longitudinal wave.

8. Wave Behaviour

Reflection

Reflection is the bouncing back of a wave when it reaches a boundary.

Refraction

Refraction is the change in direction of a wave when its speed changes as it enters a different medium.

Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading of waves when they pass through a gap or around an obstacle.

Interference

Interference occurs when two or more waves meet and combine.

TypeEffect
Constructive interferenceWaves reinforce each other, producing a larger amplitude.
Destructive interferenceWaves partially or completely cancel each other.

9. Sound

Sound is a form of mechanical wave produced by vibrating objects and transmitted through a medium.

Sound requires a medium such as a solid, liquid or gas. Therefore, sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

In air, sound travels as a longitudinal wave.

10. Production of Sound

Sound is produced when an object vibrates.

Examples:

  • A guitar string vibrates.
  • A loudspeaker cone vibrates.
  • The vocal cords vibrate when speaking.
  • A tuning fork vibrates when struck.
When a vibrating tuning fork is placed near the ear, its vibrations cause nearby air particles to vibrate, producing a sound wave.

11. Characteristics of Sound

CharacteristicMainly depends on
PitchFrequency
LoudnessAmplitude

Higher frequency means higher pitch. Greater amplitude generally means louder sound.

12. Audible and Inaudible Sound

For a typical healthy human ear:

Frequency rangeDescription
Below about 20 HzInfrasound
About 20 Hz – 20 000 HzAudible sound
Above about 20 000 HzUltrasound

The exact limits can vary between individuals, especially with age.

13. Speed of Sound

Sound generally travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids and slowest through gases.

The speed of sound in air depends on conditions such as temperature.

Example: Lightning is seen before thunder is heard because light travels much faster than sound.

14. Echoes

Echo: A reflected sound heard separately from the original sound.

If the speed of sound is \(v\), the distance to a reflecting surface can be found using:

\[ d=\frac{vt}{2} \]

The division by 2 is necessary because the sound travels to the surface and then back.

Example: An echo returns after 0.4 s. If the speed of sound is 340 m/s, calculate the distance to the wall.
\[ d=\frac{340\times0.4}{2} \] \[ \boxed{d=68\text{ m}} \]

15. Ultrasound

Ultrasound consists of sound waves with frequencies above the normal human hearing range.

Applications include:

  • medical scanning;
  • checking unborn babies;
  • detecting cracks in materials;
  • cleaning delicate objects;
  • SONAR and underwater measurements.

17. MANEB-Style Practice Questions

1. Define wavelength.
2. A wave has a frequency of 20 Hz and wavelength 0.5 m. Calculate its speed.
3. State one difference between transverse and longitudinal waves.
4. Explain why sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
5. A sound wave has a frequency of 500 Hz and travels at 340 m/s. Calculate its wavelength.
6. Explain the difference between pitch and loudness.
7. An echo is heard 2 s after a sound is produced. If sound travels at 340 m/s, calculate the distance to the reflecting surface.
8. State two uses of ultrasound.

18. Answers

  1. The distance between two successive corresponding points on a wave.
  2. \(v=f\lambda=20\times0.5=\boxed{10\text{ m/s}}\).
  3. Particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of travel in transverse waves, but parallel in longitudinal waves.
  4. Sound requires particles in a medium to transfer its energy, and a vacuum has no material particles.
  5. \[ \lambda=\frac{v}{f}=\frac{340}{500} \] \[ \boxed{\lambda=0.68\text{ m}} \]
  6. Pitch depends mainly on frequency, while loudness depends mainly on amplitude.
  7. \[ d=\frac{340\times2}{2}=\boxed{340\text{ m}} \]
  8. Medical scanning and SONAR. Other correct examples include industrial crack detection and cleaning.

19. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing frequency with wavelength.
  • Forgetting that frequency is measured in hertz.
  • Using \(v=f/\lambda\) instead of \(v=f\lambda\).
  • Saying sound can travel through a vacuum.
  • Confusing pitch with loudness.
  • For echo calculations, forgetting that the sound travels twice the distance.

20. Examination Tips

  • Always write the wave equation before calculating.
  • Convert wavelength to metres when necessary.
  • For echo questions, remember the outward and return journeys.
  • Use precise definitions for transverse and longitudinal waves.
  • When comparing pitch and loudness, mention frequency and amplitude respectively.
