Electromagnetic Induction

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

Electromagnetic induction is the production of an electromotive force (e.m.f.) when the magnetic field through a conductor or coil changes. It is the principle behind electric generators and transformers.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Describe experiments demonstrating electromagnetic induction.
  • Explain factors affecting induced e.m.f.

  • Explain the basic operation of a transformer.
  • Describe applications of electromagnetic induction.

3. Electromagnetic Induction

Electromagnetic induction is the production of an induced e.m.f. in a conductor when the magnetic flux linking the conductor changes.

No current is produced if the circuit is open, but an induced e.m.f. can still exist.

A changing magnetic field is essential. Simply placing a stationary magnet near a stationary coil does not continuously produce an induced current.

4. Demonstrating Electromagnetic Induction

Connect a coil of wire to a sensitive galvanometer and move a bar magnet towards and away from the coil.

Moving the magnet changes the magnetic field through the coil and produces an induced e.m.f.

Observations:

  • Moving the magnet towards the coil causes a deflection.
  • Moving it away causes deflection in the opposite direction.
  • Holding the magnet stationary produces no continuous deflection.
  • Moving the magnet faster produces a larger deflection.

5. Factors Affecting Induced E.M.F.

The induced e.m.f. can be increased by:

  • moving the magnet or coil faster;
  • using a stronger magnet;
  • increasing the number of turns in the coil;
  • increasing the rate at which the magnetic field changes.
The important factor is the rate of change of the magnetic field linking the conductor.

6. Lenz's Law

Lenz's law: The induced current flows in a direction such that its magnetic effect opposes the change that produced it.

For example, when a north pole of a magnet approaches a coil, the near face of the coil becomes a north pole. This repels the approaching magnet and opposes the change.

7. Fleming's Right-Hand Rule

Fleming's right-hand rule can be used to determine the direction of induced current in a conductor moving through a magnetic field.

  • Thumb → direction of motion of conductor
  • First finger → magnetic field
  • Second finger → induced current
Do not confuse Fleming's right-hand rule with Fleming's left-hand rule. The left-hand rule is associated with the motor effect; the right-hand rule is used for generator action.

8. Generator

Generator: A device that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy using electromagnetic induction.

A simple AC generator contains:

  • a coil;
  • a magnetic field;
  • slip rings;
  • brushes;
  • an external circuit.

When the coil rotates in the magnetic field, the magnetic flux linking the coil changes and an induced e.m.f. is produced.

Energy conversion:
Mechanical energy → electrical energy

9. Alternating Current

Alternating current (AC) is a current that repeatedly changes magnitude and reverses direction.

AC is commonly produced by generators.

Direct current (DC) flows in one direction in a circuit.

10. Transformer

Transformer: A device that uses electromagnetic induction to change the magnitude of an alternating voltage.

A transformer consists mainly of:

  • primary coil;
  • secondary coil;
  • laminated soft iron core.

There is no direct electrical connection between the primary and secondary coils. Energy is transferred through the changing magnetic field in the core.

\[ \frac{V_s}{V_p}=\frac{N_s}{N_p} \]

where \(V_p\) and \(V_s\) are the primary and secondary voltages, while \(N_p\) and \(N_s\) are the numbers of turns.

A transformer has 500 primary turns and 100 secondary turns. If the primary voltage is 240 V, calculate the secondary voltage.
\[ \frac{V_s}{240}=\frac{100}{500} \] \[ V_s=240\times\frac{100}{500} \] \[ \boxed{V_s=48\text{ V}} \]

12. Transformer Efficiency

An ideal transformer transfers energy without losses. Real transformers lose some energy through heating, sound and other effects.

\[ \text{Efficiency}= \frac{\text{useful power output}} {\text{power input}}\times100\% \]

For an ideal transformer:

\[ V_pI_p=V_sI_s \]

13. Why the Core is Laminated

The soft iron core is laminated to reduce energy losses caused by eddy currents.

The core also provides a good path for the changing magnetic field linking the two coils.

14. National Electricity Transmission

Electrical power is transmitted at high voltage to reduce energy losses in transmission lines.

\[ P=VI \]

For a fixed power, increasing voltage decreases the current:

\[ I=\frac{P}{V} \]

Since heating loss in transmission wires depends on current, reducing current reduces power loss.

17. MANEB-Style Practice Questions

1. Define electromagnetic induction.
2. State two observations made when a magnet is moved into and out of a coil connected to a galvanometer.
3. State three ways of increasing the induced e.m.f.
4. State Lenz's law.
5. Distinguish between Fleming's left-hand and right-hand rules.
6. State the energy conversion in an electric generator.
7. A transformer has 1000 primary turns and 200 secondary turns. If the primary voltage is 250 V, calculate the secondary voltage.
8. Explain why a transformer cannot normally operate from a steady DC supply.
9. Give one reason for transmitting electricity at high voltage.
10. State one difference between AC and DC.

18. Answers

  1. Production of an induced e.m.f. when magnetic flux linking a conductor changes.
  2. The galvanometer deflects when the magnet moves; the direction reverses when the magnet is moved in the opposite direction.
  3. Move the magnet faster, use a stronger magnet, increase the number of turns, or increase the rate of change of magnetic field.
  4. The induced current opposes the change that produces it.
  5. Left-hand rule determines force in the motor effect; right-hand rule determines induced current in generator action.
  6. Mechanical energy → electrical energy.
  7. \[ \frac{V_s}{250}=\frac{200}{1000} \] \[ \boxed{V_s=50\text{ V}} \]
  8. A transformer requires a continuously changing magnetic field. Steady DC does not continuously change the magnetic field after the initial switching transient.
  9. High voltage allows lower current for the same transmitted power, reducing energy losses in the transmission lines.
  10. AC reverses direction periodically; DC flows in one direction.

19. Common Mistakes

  • Thinking that a stationary magnet continuously induces current.
  • Confusing electromagnetic induction with the motor effect.
  • Using Fleming's left-hand rule instead of the right-hand rule for generator action.
  • Reversing primary and secondary turns in transformer calculations.
  • Forgetting that ordinary transformers require alternating current.
  • Assuming all transformers increase voltage.

20. Examination Tips

  • Always identify whether the question concerns motor action or generator action.
  • Remember: generator = mechanical to electrical energy.
  • Step-up transformer: secondary has more turns.
  • Step-down transformer: secondary has fewer turns.
  • Write the transformer formula before substituting values.
  • Include units in all numerical answers.
