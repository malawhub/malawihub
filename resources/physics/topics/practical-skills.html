Physics Practical Skills

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

Physics is an experimental science. Practical work helps learners test ideas, collect measurements, identify relationships and draw conclusions from evidence.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Identify common laboratory apparatus.
  • Take measurements correctly.
  • Record results in tables.
  • Calculate derived quantities.
  • Plot and interpret graphs.
  • Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  • Calculate experimental errors and percentage uncertainty where appropriate.
  • Evaluate experimental procedures.
  • Suggest improvements to experiments.
  • Follow laboratory safety rules.

4. Reading Scales

When reading an instrument:

  1. Identify the smallest scale division.
  2. Read the scale at eye level where possible.
  3. Record the value with appropriate precision.
  4. Include the correct unit.
Avoid parallax error by viewing the scale straight on rather than from an angle.

5. Zero Error

Zero error occurs when an instrument does not read zero when the measured quantity should be zero.

A zero error can cause every measurement to be systematically too large or too small.

Where appropriate:

\[ \text{Corrected reading} = \text{Observed reading} - \text{Zero error} \]

6. Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the true or accepted value.
Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with each other.

A set of measurements can be precise but inaccurate if they are consistently affected by a systematic error.

7. Random and Systematic Errors

Causes readings to vary unpredictably.Produces a consistent shift in measurements.

8. Variables

Independent variable: The quantity deliberately changed by the experimenter.
Dependent variable: The quantity measured or observed in response.
Controlled variables: Other quantities kept constant so that the investigation is fair.
Example: Investigating how length affects the resistance of a wire:
Independent variable → length of wire
Dependent variable → resistance
Controlled variables → material, thickness and temperature

9. Fair Tests

A fair test changes one main independent variable while keeping relevant conditions constant.

A good experiment should allow another person to repeat the procedure and obtain comparable results.

10. Repeated Measurements

Repeating measurements helps identify unusual readings and reduces the effect of random error.

\[ \text{Mean} = \frac{\text{sum of measurements}} {\text{number of measurements}} \]
Measurements of a length are 10.2 cm, 10.4 cm and 10.3 cm. Find the mean.
\[ \text{Mean} = \frac{10.2+10.4+10.3}{3} \] \[ =\frac{30.9}{3} \] \[ \boxed{10.3\text{ cm}} \]

12. Graphs

Graphs help reveal relationships between physical quantities.

General rules:

  • Use a suitable scale.
  • Label both axes.
  • Include units.
  • Plot points accurately.
  • Use a smooth best-fit line or curve where appropriate.
  • Do not simply join every point unless the experiment requires it.

13. Gradient

The gradient of a straight-line graph is:

\[ \text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \]

Use two points on the best-fit line, preferably far apart, rather than two points that are necessarily experimental measurements.

If \(y\) changes from 4 to 10 while \(x\) changes from 2 to 5:
\[ \text{gradient} = \frac{10-4}{5-2} = \frac63 = \boxed{2} \]

14. Intercept

The intercept is where a graph crosses an axis.

For a straight-line relationship:

\[ y=mx+c \]

\(m\) is the gradient and \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept.

15. Direct Proportionality

Two quantities are directly proportional when one is equal to a constant multiplied by the other.
\[ y\propto x \] \[ y=kx \]

A graph of \(y\) against \(x\) passes through the origin for direct proportionality.

16. Investigating Density

Density can be determined experimentally using mass and volume.

\[ \rho=\frac{m}{V} \]

For a regular solid, calculate volume from measured dimensions.

For an irregular solid, water displacement can be used.

A solid has mass 150 g and volume 50 cm³. Calculate its density.
\[ \rho=\frac{150}{50} \] \[ \boxed{\rho=3.0\text{ g cm}^{-3}} \]

17. Investigating Motion

Motion can be investigated by measuring distance and time.

\[ v=\frac{s}{t} \]

A ticker timer, light gates, stopwatch or video analysis can be used depending on available equipment.

18. Investigating Electrical Circuits

When investigating the relationship between voltage and current:

  • connect the ammeter in series;
  • connect the voltmeter in parallel;
  • change the potential difference;
  • measure the current;
  • record the results;
  • plot a graph of \(V\) against \(I\) or \(I\) against \(V\), depending on the required relationship.

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature:

\[ V=IR \]

19. Investigating Heat

Heating experiments can be used to investigate specific heat capacity.

\[ Q=mc\Delta T \]

Measure:

  • mass \(m\);
  • energy supplied \(Q\);
  • temperature change \(\Delta T\).

Reduce heat losses where possible by using insulation and suitable experimental methods.

20. Safety in Physics Practical Work

  • Follow the teacher's instructions.
  • Keep electrical connections dry.
  • Do not touch exposed live electrical conductors.
  • Handle glass equipment carefully.
  • Use suitable eye protection when required.
  • Do not look directly into intense light sources.
  • Keep the working area organised.
  • Report damaged equipment immediately.
Safety procedures must always take priority over obtaining a measurement.

21. Writing a Practical Report

A clear practical report commonly contains:

  1. Title
  2. Aim
  3. Apparatus
  4. Variables
  5. Method
  6. Results
  7. Calculations
  8. Graph
  9. Conclusion
  10. Sources of error
  11. Improvements

22. Conclusion

A good conclusion must answer the aim using evidence from the results. Avoid unsupported statements.

Weak: The experiment worked.

Better: The results show that increasing the length of the wire increased its resistance, as indicated by the upward trend in the graph.

23. Evaluating an Experiment

When evaluating an experiment, consider:

  • accuracy;
  • precision;
  • repeatability;
  • reliability;
  • sources of error;
  • limitations;
  • possible improvements.

An improvement should directly address an identified problem.

24. MANEB Examination Focus

  • Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  • Complete results tables correctly.
  • Read measuring instruments.
  • Calculate averages.
  • Plot graphs accurately.
  • Calculate gradients.
  • Interpret relationships from graphs.
  • Identify sources of experimental error.
  • Suggest realistic improvements.
  • State appropriate safety precautions.

25. MANEB-Style Practice Questions

1. Distinguish between accuracy and precision.
2. State one difference between random and systematic error.
3. In an experiment investigating the effect of length on resistance, identify the independent and dependent variables.
4. A student records lengths of 12.1 cm, 12.3 cm and 12.2 cm. Calculate the mean.
5. State three rules for drawing a scientific graph.
6. A graph has a change in \(y\) of 12 units and a change in \(x\) of 4 units. Calculate its gradient.
7. State how an ammeter and voltmeter should be connected.
8. Give two ways of reducing heat loss in a specific-heat-capacity experiment.
9. State two safety precautions when working with electrical circuits.
10. Give two ways of improving an experiment affected by random errors.

26. Answers

  1. Accuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is closeness of repeated measurements to one another.
  2. Random error varies unpredictably; systematic error produces a consistent shift.
  3. Independent variable = length; dependent variable = resistance.
  4. \[ \text{Mean}=\frac{12.1+12.3+12.2}{3} =\boxed{12.2\text{ cm}} \]
  5. Label axes with units, choose a suitable scale and plot points accurately.
  6. \[ \text{gradient}=\frac{12}{4}=\boxed{3} \]
  7. Ammeter in series; voltmeter in parallel.
  8. Use insulation and a lid/cover where appropriate; reduce unnecessary exposure of the heated object to the surroundings.
  9. Keep connections dry and never touch exposed live conductors.
  10. Repeat measurements and calculate a mean; use more precise measuring equipment where appropriate.

27. Common Mistakes

  • Leaving units out of tables and answers.
  • Putting units beside every individual value when the unit belongs in the table heading.
  • Joining points randomly instead of drawing a best-fit line.
  • Using a very poor graph scale.
  • Confusing independent and dependent variables.
  • Calling every experimental problem a random error.
  • Suggesting an improvement that does not address the identified error.

28. Examination Tips

  • Read practical questions carefully before identifying variables.
  • Always state the measuring instrument when asked how a quantity is measured.
  • Use units consistently.
  • Show all working in calculations.
  • When evaluating an experiment, explain why a change would improve the result.
  • For graphs, use most of the available graph area.
