Magnetism and Electromagnetism

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

Magnetism is the study of magnetic forces and magnetic fields. Electromagnetism describes the relationship between electricity and magnetism. These ideas are used in electric bells, relays, motors, loudspeakers, generators and many other devices.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Describe the properties of magnets.
  • Identify magnetic and non-magnetic materials.
  • Explain magnetic fields and field lines.
  • Describe the Earth's magnetic field.
  • Explain electromagnetism.
  • Describe the magnetic field around a current-carrying conductor.
  • Explain factors affecting electromagnet strength.
  • Describe applications of electromagnets.
  • Explain the motor effect.

3. Magnets

Magnet: A material or object that produces a magnetic field and can attract magnetic materials.

Common magnetic materials include:

  • iron;
  • steel;
  • nickel;
  • cobalt.

Examples of non-magnetic materials include wood, glass, plastic and copper.

4. Magnetic Poles

A magnet has two poles:

  • North pole (N)
  • South pole (S)
Like poles repel. Unlike poles attract.

If a magnet is broken into smaller pieces, each piece still has both a north and a south pole. Isolated magnetic poles are not normally obtained by simply breaking a magnet.

5. Magnetic Field

Magnetic field: The region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor in which a magnetic force can be detected.

A magnetic field can be represented using magnetic field lines.

Typical magnetic field around a bar magnet

Outside a bar magnet, field lines are directed from N to S.

6. Properties of Magnetic Field Lines

  • They indicate the direction of the magnetic field.
  • Outside a magnet they run from north to south.
  • They never cross one another.
  • Closer lines represent a stronger magnetic field.
  • The field is strongest where the lines are most crowded.

7. Plotting Magnetic Fields

Magnetic fields can be investigated using iron filings or a plotting compass.

Method using a plotting compass:
  1. Place the magnet on a sheet of paper.
  2. Mark the position of the magnet.
  3. Place a compass near one pole.
  4. Mark the direction indicated by the compass needle.
  5. Move the compass to the new position.
  6. Continue until a field line is obtained.
  7. Repeat from different starting positions.

8. Magnetic Effect of Electric Current

When electric current flows through a conductor, a magnetic field is produced around the conductor.

Electromagnetism: The relationship between electricity and magnetism, particularly the production of magnetic effects by electric currents.

The magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire consists of circular field lines around the wire.

9. Right-Hand Grip Rule

The direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying conductor can be found using the right-hand grip rule.

Grip the conductor with the right hand so that the thumb points in the direction of conventional current. The curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field.

The direction used is the direction of conventional current, not electron flow.

10. Electromagnets

Electromagnet: A temporary magnet produced when electric current passes through a coil of wire, usually wound around a soft iron core.

An electromagnet can be switched on and off by controlling the current.

A simple electromagnet consists of:

  • an insulated wire coil;
  • a soft iron core;
  • a source of electrical energy;
  • a switch.

11. Factors Affecting Electromagnet Strength

The strength of an electromagnet can be increased by:

  • increasing the current;
  • increasing the number of turns of the coil;
  • using a suitable soft iron core.
A soft iron core is useful because it becomes strongly magnetised when current flows and loses most of its magnetism when the current is switched off.

13. The Electric Bell

An electric bell demonstrates the repeated action of an electromagnet.

  1. The switch is pressed.
  2. Current flows through the coil.
  3. The coil becomes an electromagnet.
  4. The electromagnet attracts the armature.
  5. The hammer strikes the gong.
  6. The contact opens.
  7. Current stops and the electromagnet loses magnetism.
  8. The armature returns and the contact closes again.
  9. The cycle repeats rapidly.

14. Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field can experience a force. This is called the motor effect.

Motor effect: The force experienced by a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field.

The direction of the force depends on:

  • the direction of the current;
  • the direction of the magnetic field.

15. Fleming's Left-Hand Rule

Fleming's left-hand rule can be used to determine the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor.

  • First finger → magnetic field
  • Second finger → conventional current
  • Thumb → force or motion
The three directions are mutually perpendicular.

16. Simple Electric Motor

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.

Its basic parts include:

  • coil of wire;
  • magnet;
  • split-ring commutator;
  • brushes;
  • power supply.

When current passes through the coil in a magnetic field, opposite forces act on the two sides of the coil. These forces produce a turning effect.

Energy conversion:
Electrical energy → mechanical energy + some heat and sound energy

18. Magnetic Materials

Magnetic materials can be magnetised by several methods, including stroking, electrical methods and induction.

Magnetic induction: The process by which a magnetic material becomes magnetised when placed in a magnetic field.

Soft iron is easy to magnetise and demagnetise, while steel tends to retain magnetism for longer.

19. Uses of Magnetism in Everyday Life

  • compasses for navigation;
  • electric motors;
  • generators;
  • speakers and headphones;
  • magnetic locks;
  • relays;
  • electromagnetic cranes;
  • separation of iron materials.

22. Answers

  1. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.
  2. The region around a magnet in which a magnetic force can be detected.
  3. They show field direction; they never cross; closer lines indicate stronger fields.
  4. Soft iron is easily magnetised and loses most of its magnetism when current is switched off.
  5. Increase current, increase number of turns, and use a suitable soft iron core.
  6. Current magnetises the coil, which attracts the armature; the contact opens, current stops, the armature returns, and the cycle repeats.
  7. The force experienced by a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field.
  8. First finger shows magnetic field, second finger shows conventional current and thumb shows force or motion.
  9. A permanent magnet remains magnetised and cannot easily be switched off; an electromagnet depends on current and can be switched on and off.
  10. Electric bells, relays, magnetic cranes, motors and loudspeakers.

23. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing magnetic field direction with electron flow.
  • Forgetting that conventional current is used in Fleming's left-hand rule.
  • Using Fleming's left-hand rule when finding the direction of induced current.
  • Confusing the motor effect with electromagnetic induction.
  • Drawing magnetic field lines crossing each other.
  • Thinking that breaking a magnet produces an isolated north or south pole.

24. Examination Tips

  • Always label N and S poles clearly in magnetic diagrams.
  • Draw field lines smoothly and show arrows.
  • When explaining an electric bell, write the events in the correct sequence.
  • Memorise the three parts of Fleming's left-hand rule.
  • For electromagnets, remember: more turns + more current = stronger magnet, provided other conditions remain suitable.
