Light and Optics

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation that enables us to see objects. It travels through a vacuum and can also travel through transparent materials.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Explain how light travels.
  • Distinguish luminous and non-luminous objects.
  • Explain reflection and refraction.
  • Apply the laws of reflection.
  • Describe images formed by plane and curved mirrors.
  • Explain refraction through glass and water.
  • Use refractive index relationships.
  • Explain dispersion and colour.
  • Describe lenses and their applications.

3. Sources of Light

Non-luminous object: An object that does not produce its own light but can be seen because it reflects light.

4. Rectilinear Propagation of Light

Light travels in straight lines through a uniform transparent medium. This is called rectilinear propagation.

This explains:

  • formation of shadows;
  • eclipses;
  • the operation of a pinhole camera.

5. Reflection

Reflection is the bouncing back of light when it strikes a surface.

Important terms:

  • Incident ray: incoming ray.
  • Reflected ray: ray leaving the surface.
  • Normal: line perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence.
  • Angle of incidence: angle between incident ray and normal.
  • Angle of reflection: angle between reflected ray and normal.
\[ i=r \]
The angles of incidence and reflection are measured from the normal, not from the surface.

6. Plane Mirror

A plane mirror has a flat reflecting surface.

The image formed is:

  • virtual;
  • upright;
  • the same size as the object;
  • laterally inverted;
  • the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front.
A person standing 2 m in front of a plane mirror forms an image 2 m behind the mirror. The object-image separation is therefore 4 m.

7. Refraction

Refraction is the change in direction of light when it passes from one medium into another because its speed changes.

When light enters a denser optical medium at an angle, it generally bends towards the normal. When it enters a less optically dense medium, it generally bends away from the normal.

The frequency of light remains unchanged when it passes between transparent media, but its speed and wavelength change.

8. Refractive Index

\[ n=\frac{c}{v} \]

where:

  • \(n\) = refractive index
  • \(c\) = speed of light in vacuum
  • \(v\) = speed of light in the material
Example: Light travels through a material at \(2.0\times10^8\) m/s. Take \(c=3.0\times10^8\) m/s. Calculate its refractive index.
\[ n=\frac{3.0\times10^8}{2.0\times10^8} \] \[ \boxed{n=1.5} \]

9. Snell's Law

\[ n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r \]

For light travelling from air into a material, this can be written approximately as:

\[ n=\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} \]

where \(i\) is the angle of incidence and \(r\) is the angle of refraction.

10. Critical Angle and Total Internal Reflection

When light travels from a more optically dense medium to a less optically dense medium, increasing the angle of incidence eventually produces a refracted ray travelling along the boundary.

Critical angle: The angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction is 90°.

For angles greater than the critical angle, total internal reflection occurs.

Applications include:

  • optical fibres;
  • medical endoscopes;
  • communication systems;
  • some reflecting prisms.

11. Dispersion

Dispersion is the separation of white light into its component colours.

White light can be separated into a spectrum containing:

Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo and Violet.

Different colours are refracted by different amounts. Violet is generally deviated more than red in glass.

A rainbow forms when sunlight undergoes refraction, dispersion and internal reflection in water droplets.

12. Shadows

A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light travelling from a source.

With an extended source, two regions may occur:

  • Umbra: region of complete shadow.
  • Penumbra: region of partial shadow.

13. Curved Mirrors

MirrorMain effectCommon use
ConcaveConverges parallel raysReflectors, shaving/makeup mirrors
ConvexDiverges parallel raysVehicle side mirrors, security mirrors

A concave mirror can produce real or virtual images depending on the position of the object.

A convex mirror produces an image that is generally virtual, upright and smaller than the object.

14. Lenses

A lens is a transparent optical device that refracts light.

LensActionTypical use
Convex lensConverges lightMagnifying glass, cameras
Concave lensDiverges lightSome spectacles
Principal focus: Point where parallel rays converge, or from which they appear to diverge, after passing through a lens.

15. Lens Formula

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} \]

where:

  • \(f\) = focal length
  • \(u\) = object distance
  • \(v\) = image distance
Example: A convex lens has focal length 10 cm and an object is placed 20 cm from the lens. Using the simple magnitude form, calculate the image distance.
\[ \frac{1}{10}=\frac{1}{20}+\frac{1}{v} \] \[ \frac{1}{v}=\frac{1}{10}-\frac{1}{20}=\frac{1}{20} \] \[ \boxed{v=20\text{ cm}} \]

16. Magnification

\[ m=\frac{\text{image height}}{\text{object height}} \]

In the simple magnitude form:

\[ m=\frac{v}{u} \]

If \(m>1\), the image is enlarged. If \(m<1\), the image is diminished.

17. Uses of Optics

  • Eyeglasses correct some vision defects.
  • Cameras use lenses to form images.
  • Microscopes magnify small objects.
  • Telescopes allow distant objects to be viewed.
  • Optical fibres transmit information.
  • Periscopes use reflection to allow viewing over obstacles.

19. MANEB-Style Practice Questions

1. State the two laws of reflection.
2. An incident ray makes an angle of 35° with the normal. Find the angle of reflection.
3. Explain why a pencil partly immersed in water appears bent.
4. Light travels through glass at \(2.0\times10^8\) m/s. Calculate its refractive index.
5. State two conditions necessary for total internal reflection.
6. State the colours of the visible spectrum in order from red to violet.
7. State two properties of the image formed by a convex mirror.
8. A convex lens has \(f=15\) cm and \(u=30\) cm. Calculate \(v\) using the lens formula.

20. Answers

  1. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane; angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.
  2. \(\boxed{35^\circ}\).
  3. Light is refracted when passing between water and air, changing its direction.
  4. \(n=3.0\times10^8/2.0\times10^8=\boxed{1.5}\).
  5. Light must travel from a more optically dense medium to a less optically dense medium, and the angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.
  6. Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
  7. The image is generally virtual, upright and smaller than the object.
  8. \[ \frac1{15}=\frac1{30}+\frac1v \] \[ \frac1v=\frac1{30} \] \[ \boxed{v=30\text{ cm}} \]

21. Common Mistakes

  • Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.
  • Confusing reflection with refraction.
  • Forgetting that frequency remains unchanged during refraction.
  • Mixing up convex and concave lenses.
  • Using centimetres inconsistently in calculations.
  • Forgetting that total internal reflection requires the correct direction of travel.

22. Examination Tips

  • Always draw the normal as a 90° line to the surface.
  • Label incident and reflected/refracted rays clearly.
  • Show the formula before substituting numbers.
  • Keep units consistent.
  • For ray diagrams, use a ruler and arrows to show direction of travel.
  • When explaining refraction, mention the change in speed.
