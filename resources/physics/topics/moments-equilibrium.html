Moments, Turning Effects and Equilibrium

Physics • Secondary School

1. Introduction

A force can make an object move in a straight line, but it can also make an object rotate. The turning effect of a force is called a moment.

Learning objectives

  • Define moment of a force.
  • Calculate moments using perpendicular distance.
  • Explain clockwise and anticlockwise moments.
  • Apply the principle of moments.
  • Explain equilibrium.
  • Solve simple lever and balance problems.

2. Turning Effect of a Force

Moment of a force is the turning effect of a force about a pivot or fixed point.
\[ \text{Moment}=F\times d \]

where \(F\) is the force and \(d\) is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

The SI unit of moment is newton metre (N m).

The distance used must be the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

3. Pivot and Line of Action

The pivot is the point about which an object rotates. The line of action is the straight line along which a force acts.

A force of 10 N acts at a perpendicular distance of 0.5 m from a pivot.

\[ M=Fd \] \[ M=10\times0.5=\mathbf{5\ N\,m} \]

4. Clockwise and Anticlockwise Moments

A force can cause an object to rotate either clockwise or anticlockwise about a pivot.

DirectionDescription
ClockwiseRotation in the direction of clock hands
AnticlockwiseRotation opposite to clock hands

For an object in equilibrium, clockwise and anticlockwise moments about the same pivot balance.

5. Principle of Moments

For an object in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about a pivot equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about that pivot.
\[ \sum M_{\text{clockwise}} = \sum M_{\text{anticlockwise}} \]
A 20 N force acts 2 m from a pivot. What force acting 4 m on the opposite side will balance it?

Clockwise moment: \[ 20\times2=40\ N\,m \]
Let the balancing force be \(F\): \[ F\times4=40 \] \[ F=\mathbf{10\ N} \]

6. Centre of Gravity

The centre of gravity is the point through which the weight of an object may be considered to act.

For a symmetrical uniform object, the centre of gravity is usually at its geometrical centre.

The position of the centre of gravity affects the stability of an object.

7. Stability

An object is stable when it tends to return to its original position after a small displacement.

Factors affecting stability include:

  • width of the base
  • height of the centre of gravity
  • position of the centre of gravity relative to the base
An object is generally more stable when it has a wide base and a low centre of gravity.
A racing car is designed with a low body and a wide wheelbase to reduce the risk of toppling during turning.

8. Equilibrium

An object is in equilibrium when there is no resultant force and no resultant turning effect acting on it.

For complete equilibrium:

\[ \sum F=0 \]

and

\[ \sum M=0 \]

Therefore, an object in equilibrium can be stationary or move with constant velocity while having no resultant force.

9. Levers

A lever is a rigid object that turns about a fixed point called a pivot or fulcrum.

TermMeaning
FulcrumPivot about which the lever turns
EffortForce applied to the lever
LoadResistance or force being moved

Examples of levers

  • crowbar
  • spanner
  • scissors
  • wheelbarrow
  • pliers
A long spanner makes it easier to loosen a tight nut because increasing the distance from the pivot increases the moment for the same applied force.

10. Mechanical Advantage of a Lever

\[ MA=\frac{\text{load}}{\text{effort}} \]
A lever lifts a load of 600 N using an effort of 150 N.

\[ MA=\frac{600}{150}=\mathbf{4} \]

A lever can provide a mechanical advantage greater than one when the effort arm is suitably arranged relative to the load arm.

11. Gears and Turning Effects

Gears transfer rotational motion and can change speed, torque and direction. A smaller driving gear turning a larger driven gear generally produces a larger turning effect but lower rotational speed.

Examples of gears are found in bicycles, clocks, machines and vehicles.

12. Practical Investigation of Moments

A simple moments experiment can be performed using a metre rule, pivot, weights and a balance point.

  1. Place the metre rule on a pivot.
  2. Hang a known weight on one side.
  3. Place another weight on the opposite side.
  4. Adjust the distance until the rule balances.
  5. Measure the perpendicular distances.
  6. Calculate both moments.
  7. Compare clockwise and anticlockwise moments.
For a good experiment, repeat measurements and take care when measuring distances from the pivot.

13. MANEB-Style Examination Practice

  1. Define the moment of a force.
  2. State the SI unit of moment.
  3. A force of 25 N acts at a perpendicular distance of 0.4 m from a pivot. Calculate the moment.
  4. A 40 N force acts 2 m from a pivot. What force acting 4 m from the pivot on the opposite side will balance it?
  5. State the principle of moments.
  6. Explain why a long spanner is more effective than a short spanner.
  7. Define centre of gravity.
  8. State two ways of increasing the stability of an object.
  9. What two conditions must be satisfied for complete equilibrium?
  10. Explain why a wide-based object is generally more stable.

Answers

  1. The turning effect of a force about a pivot.
  2. Newton metre (N m).
  3. \(10\ N\,m\).
  4. \(20\ N\).
  5. Clockwise moments equal anticlockwise moments for an object in rotational equilibrium.
  6. It provides a greater perpendicular distance, producing a greater moment for the same force.
  7. The point through which the weight of an object may be considered to act.
  8. Increase the base width and lower the centre of gravity.
  9. Resultant force must be zero and resultant moment must be zero.
  10. A larger base provides a wider support area and makes toppling more difficult.

14. Common Mistakes

  • Using the distance along the object instead of perpendicular distance.
  • Forgetting to identify the pivot.
  • Mixing clockwise and anticlockwise moments incorrectly.
  • Using centimetres without converting when SI units are required.
  • Confusing moment with force.
  • Thinking equilibrium means that no forces are acting.

15. Examination Tips

  • Always identify the pivot first.
  • Draw a clear force diagram where necessary.
  • Use perpendicular distance in moment calculations.
  • Write clockwise and anticlockwise moments separately.
  • Show the equation before substituting values.
  • Give moments in N m and forces in N.

Topic Summary

Moments describe the turning effects of forces. The principle of moments is used to analyse balances and levers, while equilibrium requires both translational and rotational balance. Centre of gravity and stability explain why some objects are easier to topple than others.
