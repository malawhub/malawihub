Atomic and Nuclear Physics

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

Atomic and nuclear physics deals with the structure of atoms, the atomic nucleus, isotopes, radioactivity, nuclear radiation, nuclear reactions and applications of nuclear physics.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Describe the basic structure of an atom.
  • Distinguish protons, neutrons and electrons.
  • Explain atomic number and mass number.
  • Define isotopes.
  • Explain radioactivity.
  • Identify alpha, beta and gamma radiation.
  • Compare the penetrating and ionising abilities of nuclear radiation.
  • Explain radioactive decay and half-life.
  • Describe uses and dangers of radioactive materials.
  • Distinguish nuclear fission from nuclear fusion.

3. Structure of the Atom

Atom: The smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element.

An atom consists of a small, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons.

4. Atomic Number and Mass Number

Atomic number \(Z\) is the number of protons in the nucleus.
Mass number \(A\) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
\[ A=Z+N \]

where \(N\) is the number of neutrons.

An atom has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. Find its atomic number and mass number.
\[ Z=17 \] \[ A=17+18=35 \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{Z=17,\quad A=35} \]

5. Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Because isotopes have the same number of protons, they have the same atomic number.

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon. Both have 6 protons, but carbon-12 has 6 neutrons while carbon-14 has 8 neutrons.

6. Radioactivity

Radioactivity is the spontaneous and random emission of radiation from unstable atomic nuclei.

Radioactive decay is:

  • spontaneous;
  • random;
  • not significantly affected by ordinary physical conditions such as temperature and pressure.

7. Types of Nuclear Radiation

Beta (β)Fast electron or positron−1 or +1MediumMedium

Alpha is highly ionising but has low penetrating ability. Gamma is weakly ionising but highly penetrating.

8. Deflection in Electric and Magnetic Fields

Alpha and beta radiation are charged and can be deflected by electric and magnetic fields. Gamma radiation has no charge and is not deflected by these fields.

Beta particles are deflected more strongly than alpha particles because they have much smaller mass.

Actual shielding requirements depend on radiation energy and the activity of the source.

10. Half-Life

Half-life is the time required for the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei in a sample to decrease to half its original value.
A radioactive sample has an initial activity of 800 counts per minute. If its half-life is 2 hours, find its activity after 6 hours.

Number of half-lives:

\[ n=\frac{6}{2}=3 \]

Activity changes as:

\[ 800\rightarrow400\rightarrow200\rightarrow100 \] \[ \boxed{100\text{ counts per minute}} \]

12. Nuclear Equations

In nuclear equations, both mass number and atomic number must be conserved.

For alpha decay:

\[ {}^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha \]

For beta-minus decay, a neutron changes into a proton and an electron is emitted:

\[ n\rightarrow p+e^{-} \] \[ {}^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z+1}Y + {}^{0}_{-1}\beta \]

13. Background Radiation

Background radiation is the ionising radiation normally present in the environment.

Sources include:

  • rocks and soil;
  • cosmic radiation;
  • radon gas;
  • some food and building materials;
  • medical procedures.

15. Radiation Safety

Exposure to ionising radiation must be controlled because radiation can damage living cells.

Three important protection principles are:

  • Time: minimise exposure time.
  • Distance: maximise distance from the source.
  • Shielding: use suitable absorbing material.
Radioactive sources should only be handled using appropriate safety procedures and by trained personnel.

16. Nuclear Fission

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large atomic nucleus into smaller nuclei, usually releasing energy and additional neutrons.

The released neutrons can cause further fission reactions, producing a chain reaction.

Fission is used in nuclear reactors to generate electrical energy.

17. Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the joining of light atomic nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing a large amount of energy.

Fusion powers the Sun and other stars.

18. Nuclear Energy

Nuclear reactions release energy because a small amount of mass can be converted into energy.

\[ E=mc^2 \]

where \(E\) is energy, \(m\) is mass and \(c\) is the speed of light.

20. MANEB-Style Practice Questions

1. State the charge and relative mass of a proton.
2. An atom has atomic number 12 and mass number 24. Determine the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in the neutral atom.
3. Define an isotope.
4. Which nuclear radiation is most penetrating?
5. Which radiation has the greatest ionising power?
6. A radioactive sample has an activity of 640 counts per minute and a half-life of 3 hours. Find its activity after 9 hours.
7. State three methods of reducing exposure to radiation.
8. Distinguish between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.
9. Give two uses of radioactive materials.
10. Explain why gamma radiation is useful in some medical treatments but also presents a health risk.

21. Answers

  1. Charge = +1; relative mass = 1.
  2. Protons = 12, neutrons = 24 − 12 = 12, electrons = 12.
  3. Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
  4. Gamma radiation.
  5. Alpha radiation.
  6. \[ 640\rightarrow320\rightarrow160\rightarrow80 \] After 3 half-lives: \[ \boxed{80\text{ counts per minute}} \]
  7. Reduce exposure time, increase distance and use suitable shielding.
  8. Fission splits a large nucleus; fusion combines light nuclei.
  9. Radiotherapy, medical tracers, thickness monitoring, dating and smoke detection.
  10. Gamma rays can penetrate tissue and destroy cancer cells, but excessive exposure can also damage healthy cells and increase cancer risk.

22. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing atomic number with mass number.
  • Forgetting that neutrons have zero charge.
  • Thinking isotopes have different numbers of protons.
  • Confusing penetrating power with ionising power.
  • Forgetting that half-life means half of the remaining nuclei, not half of the original sample every time.
  • Confusing fission with fusion.

23. Examination Tips

  • Memorise the charges of the three atomic particles.
  • Use \(A=Z+N\) carefully.
  • For half-life questions, count the number of complete half-lives first.
  • Remember: alpha = most ionising; gamma = most penetrating.
  • When comparing fission and fusion, clearly state whether nuclei split or combine.
  • Always explain both benefits and risks when asked about radiation applications.
