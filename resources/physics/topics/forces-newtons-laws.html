Forces and Newton's Laws

Physics • Secondary School

1. Introduction

A force is a push or pull that can change the motion, direction or shape of an object. Forces are central to the study of mechanics.

Learning objectives

  • Define force and identify common forces.
  • Distinguish contact and non-contact forces.
  • State and apply Newton's laws of motion.
  • Calculate resultant force and acceleration.
  • Explain balanced and unbalanced forces.

2. What Is a Force?

Force: A push or pull that acts on an object and can change its motion or shape.

The SI unit of force is the newton (N).

Effects of a force

  • Start an object moving.
  • Stop a moving object.
  • Change the speed of an object.
  • Change the direction of motion.
  • Change the shape of an object.

4. Resultant Force

Resultant force is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together on an object.
A box is pulled with 20 N to the right while friction acts with 8 N to the left.

Resultant force = \(20-8=\mathbf{12\ N}\) to the right.

If the resultant force is zero, the forces are balanced.

If the resultant force is not zero, the forces are unbalanced and the object will accelerate.

5. Newton's First Law

An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a resultant external force.

This is sometimes called the law of inertia.

When a bus suddenly stops, passengers tend to move forward. Their bodies tend to continue moving because of inertia.

Inertia

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion. Mass is a measure of an object's inertia.

6. Newton's Second Law

Newton's second law states that the acceleration of an object is directly related to the resultant force and inversely related to its mass.

\[ F=ma \]

where:

  • \(F\) = resultant force in newtons (N)
  • \(m\) = mass in kilograms (kg)
  • \(a\) = acceleration in m/s²
Worked example: A 5 kg object accelerates at 3 m/s². Calculate the resultant force.

\[ F=ma \] \[ F=5\times3=\mathbf{15\ N} \]
Worked example: A force of 40 N acts on a 10 kg object. Calculate its acceleration.

\[ a=\frac{F}{m}=\frac{40}{10}=\mathbf{4\ m/s^2} \]

7. Newton's Third Law

When one object exerts a force on another object, the second object exerts an equal-sized force in the opposite direction on the first object.
When a swimmer pushes water backwards, the water exerts a force forwards on the swimmer, helping the swimmer move forwards.
The action and reaction forces act on different objects. They therefore do not simply cancel each other.

8. Weight and Mass

Mass is the amount of matter in an object and is measured in kilograms.

Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.

\[ W=mg \]

where \(g\) is gravitational acceleration.

A person has a mass of 60 kg. Taking \(g=10\ m/s^2\):

\[ W=60\times10=\mathbf{600\ N} \]

9. Friction

Friction is a force that opposes relative motion or attempted motion between surfaces in contact.

Useful effects

  • Allows people to walk without slipping.
  • Allows vehicles to grip the road.
  • Allows brakes to stop vehicles.

Unwanted effects

  • Causes wear of machine parts.
  • Produces unwanted heat.
  • Reduces efficiency.

Friction can be reduced by lubrication, polishing and using bearings.

10. Free-Body Diagrams

A free-body diagram represents the forces acting on an object using labelled arrows.

11. Equilibrium

An object is in equilibrium when the resultant force acting on it is zero.

For an object at rest:

\[ \text{upward forces}=\text{downward forces} \]

For horizontal equilibrium:

\[ \text{forces to the right}=\text{forces to the left} \]
A hanging lamp has a weight of 50 N. If it is stationary, the upward tension must be 50 N.

12. MANEB-Style Examination Practice

  1. Define force and state its SI unit.
  2. Distinguish between contact and non-contact forces.
  3. State Newton's first law.
  4. A 4 kg object accelerates at 5 m/s². Calculate the resultant force.
  5. Calculate the weight of a 50 kg object when \(g=10\ m/s²\).
  6. State Newton's third law.
  7. Explain why passengers move forward when a bus stops suddenly.
  8. A box is pulled with 30 N to the right while friction is 12 N to the left. Find the resultant force.
  9. State two useful effects of friction.
  10. Explain why action and reaction forces do not cancel each other.

Answers

  1. A push or pull; newton (N).
  2. Contact forces require contact; non-contact forces act without direct contact.
  3. An object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant external force.
  4. \(20\ N\).
  5. \(500\ N\).
  6. For every force exerted by one object on another, an equal and opposite force is exerted back.
  7. Because of inertia, their bodies tend to continue moving forward.
  8. \(18\ N\) to the right.
  9. Walking and vehicle braking, for example.
  10. They act on different objects.

13. Common Mistakes

  • Using mass in kilograms as if it were weight in newtons.
  • Forgetting that \(F=ma\) uses the resultant force.
  • Thinking action and reaction act on the same object.
  • Ignoring the direction of a force.
  • Confusing balanced forces with absence of forces.

14. Examination Tips

  • Draw a force diagram when several forces act on an object.
  • State directions clearly.
  • Use kilograms and m/s² when applying \(F=ma\).
  • For weight calculations, use \(W=mg\).
  • Show all working and include units.

Topic Summary

Forces can change motion or shape. Newton's three laws explain how forces, mass and motion are related. Understanding resultant force, weight, friction and free-body diagrams provides the foundation for many mechanics problems.
