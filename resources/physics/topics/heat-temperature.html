Heat and Temperature

Physics • Secondary School • MANEB-focused study notes

1. Introduction

Heat and temperature are closely related, but they are not the same thing. Temperature tells us how hot or cold an object is, while heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

2. Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between heat and temperature.
  • Explain temperature using particle motion.
  • Describe different temperature scales.
  • Explain thermal equilibrium.
  • Describe thermometers and their properties.
  • Calculate temperature changes.
  • Explain specific heat capacity.

3. Heat and Temperature

Heat: Energy transferred from a hotter body to a colder body because of a temperature difference.
Temperature: A measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body and, microscopically, an indication of the average kinetic energy of its particles.
HeatTemperature
Energy transferred between bodies.Measure of hotness or coldness.
Measured in joules (J).Measured in °C or K.
Depends on the amount of substance and temperature change.Does not directly depend on the amount of substance.
Heat naturally flows from a higher-temperature body to a lower-temperature body until thermal equilibrium is reached.

4. Particle Explanation of Temperature

Matter is made of particles that are constantly moving.

  • When temperature increases, particles generally move faster.
  • When temperature decreases, particles generally move more slowly.
  • In a solid, particles vibrate about fixed positions.
  • In liquids and gases, particles have greater freedom of movement.

5. Temperature Scales

ScaleImportant reference points
Celsius (°C)Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at about 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure.
Kelvin (K)Absolute temperature scale; 0 K is absolute zero.
\(T(K)=T(°C)+273\)
Example: Convert 27 °C to kelvin.
\[ T=27+273=\boxed{300\text{ K}} \]

6. Thermal Equilibrium

Thermal equilibrium occurs when two objects in thermal contact reach the same temperature and there is no net transfer of heat between them.

For example, when a thermometer is placed in warm water, heat flows between the water and thermometer until both reach the same temperature.

7. Thermometers

A thermometer is an instrument used to measure temperature.

A good thermometer should have:

  • a sensitive thermometric property;
  • a uniform scale;
  • a small thermal capacity;
  • a quick response;
  • a suitable temperature range;
  • clear and accurate readings.
Thermometric property: A physical property that changes predictably with temperature.

Examples include the expansion of a liquid, electrical resistance and thermoelectric voltage.

8. Liquid-in-Glass Thermometer

A liquid-in-glass thermometer uses the expansion and contraction of a liquid when temperature changes.

Common thermometer liquids include coloured alcohol and, in older instruments, mercury.

Why does the liquid rise when heated? The liquid expands as its temperature increases, causing it to occupy more space in the narrow tube.

9. Calibration of a Thermometer

To calibrate a simple Celsius thermometer:

  1. Place the thermometer in melting pure ice and mark the fixed point as 0 °C.
  2. Place it in steam above boiling water at standard atmospheric pressure and mark the fixed point as 100 °C.
  3. Divide the distance between the two fixed points into 100 equal divisions.
The boiling point of water depends on atmospheric pressure, so the standard reference is boiling water at standard atmospheric pressure.

10. Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity: The amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C or 1 K.
\[ Q=mc\Delta T \]

where:

  • \(Q\) = thermal energy transferred in joules (J)
  • \(m\) = mass in kilograms (kg)
  • \(c\) = specific heat capacity in J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹
  • \(\Delta T\) = temperature change in °C or K
Example: A 2 kg metal block has a specific heat capacity of 400 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Calculate the energy required to raise its temperature by 10 °C.
\[ Q=mc\Delta T \] \[ Q=2\times400\times10 \] \[ \boxed{Q=8000\text{ J}} \]

11. High and Low Specific Heat Capacity

A substance with a high specific heat capacity requires a large amount of energy for a given temperature increase.

A substance with a low specific heat capacity heats up and cools down more quickly for the same energy transfer.

Water has a relatively high specific heat capacity. This makes it useful in applications such as cooling systems and some heating systems.

12. Change of Temperature

\[ \Delta T=T_{\text{final}}-T_{\text{initial}} \]
Example: A substance is heated from 20 °C to 75 °C. Find the temperature change.
\[ \Delta T=75-20=\boxed{55°\text{C}} \]

14. MANEB-Style Practice Questions

1. Define temperature.
2. State two differences between heat and temperature.
3. Convert 45 °C to kelvin.
4. Explain why heat flows from a hot object to a cold object.
5. Calculate the energy required to heat 0.5 kg of water through 20 °C if its specific heat capacity is 4200 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹.
6. Explain why a thermometer must be allowed to reach thermal equilibrium before taking a reading.
7. State the two fixed points used when calibrating a Celsius thermometer.

15. Answers

  1. A measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body.
  2. Heat is transferred energy measured in joules; temperature indicates hotness/coldness and is measured in °C or K.
  3. \(45+273=\boxed{318\text{ K}}\).
  4. Because energy is transferred naturally from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature.
  5. \[ Q=mc\Delta T \] \[ Q=0.5\times4200\times20 \] \[ \boxed{Q=42000\text{ J}} \]
  6. So that the thermometer and the object reach the same temperature, giving an accurate reading.
  7. Melting ice at 0 °C and boiling water/steam at 100 °C under standard atmospheric pressure.

16. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing heat with temperature.
  • Using grams instead of kilograms in \(Q=mc\Delta T\).
  • Forgetting that temperature change is final minus initial temperature.
  • Writing °C as the unit of energy instead of joules.
  • Forgetting to add 273 when converting °C to K.

17. Examination Tips

  • Write the formula before substituting values.
  • Check the units of mass and specific heat capacity.
  • Show all working in numerical questions.
  • Use clear definitions for theory questions.
  • Remember that heat is energy transfer, not simply a measurement of temperature.
